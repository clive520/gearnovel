# Chapter 2: The Unidentified Floater's Final Registered Letter

4:40 AM.

In the narrow alleys of Sanchong, New Taipei City, the night was gradually shifting from thick inky black to a pale grayish-blue. Vegetable trucks for the morning market were just rumbling into the street, and the air was scented with the rich aroma of simmering soy milk forming a thin skin, the sizzling crackle of deep-fried crullers hitting hot oil, and the stale, damp earthy mud wafting from ancient drainage grates.

"Putt-putt-putt—BANG!"

A second-hand Wild Wolf 125 motorcycle over fifteen years old—its fuel tank scarred with scratches on both sides—shuddered to an abrupt stop by the roadside with a deafening backfire that echoed down the entire alley.

Forensic Pathologist Chen Nian-Zhou planted one foot firmly on the pavement, impassively removing his scratched helmet.

His eye sockets were deeply sunken and his lips chapped and dry. Swathed in a faded grey windbreaker hoodie, his clothes faintly exuded the faint antiseptic tang carried over from the autopsy suite.

"Cough, cough, cough! Young punk, not to nitpick..."

Hovering half a meter above the pillion seat, old Lin Fu-Lai from the Dahan River drowning case floated translucently in mid-air, rubbing his phantom buttocks while complaining in his raspy, broken voice:

"Did you salvage the shock absorbers on this jalopy from a scrap metal yard? Hitting that massive pothole back on Chongxin Bridge nearly jolted my entire soul straight into the Erchong Floodway! I had spinal surgery twice in my life for bone spurs; riding like this, even without physical bones, my soul is going to be rattled to pieces!"

Nian-Zhou pocketed the ignition key without even bothering to lift an eyelid, squeezing words through gritted teeth:

"Old man, rule number one: your current mass is exactly zero, and gravitational acceleration does not apply to you in the slightest, so what on earth are you rattling about? Rule number two: you weighed 58.4 kilograms in life, and right now you weigh less than thin air. You're complaining about a free driver running errands through the night for your dying wish? Believe me, I can turn this bike around right now, head back to the mortuary, and shove you straight into the minus-eighteen-degree Freezer No. 4."

The old ghost shrank back his neck, instantly plastering on a groveling, ingratiating smile:

"Aiya, Doctor Chen, just a little joke! Young folks shouldn't carry so much bile! Pulling all-nighters damages your liver!"

Rubbing his translucent palms together in mid-air, the old man pointed toward the deep end of the alley:

"Look, down at the end of the lane, on the rooftop of that five-story walk-up—that blue corrugated sheet-metal rooftop add-on is the 'Palace Penthouse' where I've lived for a whole decade!"

Nian-Zhou looked up, following the direction of the old man's finger.

It was a dilapidated four-story walk-up wedged between two peeling, aged apartment blocks. Perched crookedly atop the roof was a sheet-metal shack, its exterior streaked with dark red rust and greenish-black water stains. The narrow fire alley below was cluttered with flattened cardboard boxes, stacks of plastic bottles bound with nylon twine, and an old bicycle with completely rusted chains and deflated tires.

"Where's the key?"

Stepping along the narrow, damp concrete stairs, Nian-Zhou climbed all the way to the rooftop terrace in a single breath, halting before a weathered iron door sealed with a heavy-duty stainless-steel padlock.

"Aiya, when I tumbled into the Dahan River yesterday, the keychain was hooked to my belt... It probably sank into the riverbed silt along with the mud," the old man chuckled casually, scratching his messy white hair.

Nian-Zhou took a deep breath of the damp, chilly morning wind, fighting down the burning urge to crumple the old phantom into a ball and stuff him into the Wild Wolf's exhaust pipe.

Reaching into his inner coat pocket, he produced a slender stainless-steel probe with a subtle curve at the tip—a specialized forensic instrument normally reserved for extracting trace evidence from under a corpse's fingernails. Slipping on a thin latex glove, Nian-Zhou inserted the probe into the keyway and gave his wrist two deft flicks.

"Click."

The corroded padlock sprang open cleanly.

Hovering overhead, the old ghost's eyes nearly popped from their sockets:

"Holy smokes! Young punk! That flick was slicker than the master locksmiths back in Wanhua! Tell the truth, did you moonlight as a cat burglar before becoming a forensic doctor?!"

"Forensic scientists don't dissect only human tissue; occasionally, mechanical structures corroded by time fall under our purview as well," Nian-Zhou remarked coldly, stowing the probe and nudging open the creaking sheet-metal door.

The moment the door opened, an indescribably stifling air rushed over him.

A space barely four ping in size.

The ceiling was so low it felt suffocating, and the walls exposed bare, rough corrugated tin. There was no air conditioning—only a small plastic desk fan in the corner with a yellowed casing, its blades coated in thick, greasy grayish-black grime.

Pushed against the wall was a makeshift bed cobbled from four rough wooden pallets, covered by an old, pilled blanket with frayed edges. On the small wooden stool beside the bed sat a half-eaten instant noodle bowl with dried, congealed broth, next to a plastic thermos holding soggy tea dregs that had turned sour and black from countless brews.

The entire room was steeped in a scent unique to reclusive elderly people—a blend of medicinal pain-relief plaster, cheap tobacco, and the musty sourness of long-endured loneliness.

Yet amidst this miserable disarray, Nian-Zhou's gaze came to rest on a makeshift hanger bent from thick wire beside the bed.

Hanging there were two ragged work shirts caked with dried cement dust.

However, right next to the work clothes, protected inside a spotless, clear dustproof plastic garment bag with immense reverence, hung a man's black suit.

The suit was undeniably outdated—an unmistakable second-hand cut from twenty years ago—yet the collar had been pressed immaculately flat, and a cheap scented mothball was tucked inside the bag, keeping it pristine without a speck of dust.

Nian-Zhou paused before the suit, watching in silence.

Hovering beside him, the old ghost lowered his head awkwardly, rubbing his hands against his thighs in nervous agitation, his voice turning faint and hoarse:

"That... that's a second-hand suit I bought at a flea market three years ago for three hundred dollars..."

"I thought to myself back then... if... if by some miracle the day came when Ya-Ting got married, and I could sneak behind the trees outside the church to catch just one glimpse of her... then at least... at least I couldn't show up covered in cement dust... I was terrified that if she saw me, she'd think her father was a filthy old tramp who looked like a beggar..."

The old man's chapped lips trembled as a mournful light flickered in his hollow eyes:

"Yet... after buying it, I never dared try it on even once. I looked in the mirror, and I realized that with this weathered old face and these ruined hands... putting on that suit would only make me look uglier and dirtier..."

Nian-Zhou's heart felt as though it had been struck by a blunt weight, aching with dull pain.

He didn't reply, merely turning around to focus on the floor tiles:

"The third tile to the left of the doorway—is it here?"

"Yes... yes, right there," the old man floated closer, his voice laced with nervous awe.

Nian-Zhou knelt down.

The floor was laid with cheap unglazed terracotta tiles from decades past, most cracked and chipped at the edges. Counting to the third tile, Nian-Zhou hooked his fingertips along the seam and lifted gently.

With a muted scraping sound, the heavy tile came up easily.

Beneath it lay not solid concrete, but a secret hollow cavity framed by four red bricks. The bottom was lined with thick layers of dry newspaper, and resting quietly in the center was a rectangular Danish butter cookie tin.

Its corners were heavily pitted with orange-brown rust, yet someone had wrapped it with obsessive, tender care inside three spotless, heavy-duty zip-lock bags.

Each layer had the air pressed out, tightly bound at the top with five or six turns of thick red rubber bands, sealing it as hermetically as a mummy.

"Old man, why a Danish butter cookie tin?" Nian-Zhou asked softly as he unwound the rubber bands.

Floating overhead, the old phantom's eyes grew distant, gazing into empty space as if peering across thirty years of memory:

"Thirty years ago... on Ya-Ting's sixth birthday... I had a lucky streak at the gambling den and bought her a tin of the most expensive Danish butter cookies in all of Keelung... She had never tasted such sweet, buttery cookies in her life, and she jumped around hugging that tin all day long..."

The translucent mist around his eyes trembled violently, his voice choking up:

"Later... when I lost everything and ran for my life that rainy night... I took nothing of my worthless life, but I reached onto the kitchen table and stuffed that empty tin into my sack... It was the only tangible memory I carried of my wife and daughter..."

Nian-Zhou paused for a second before tearing open the final plastic sleeve and prying off the corroded tin lid.

Inside lay no clutter, only three items:

A dark green Chunghwa Post savings passbook with frayed edges;

A square wooden personal seal wrapped carefully in bright red velvet;

And an impressively thick white envelope preserved within a waterproof zip-lock pouch.

On the front of the envelope, written in an awkward, childish ballpoint scrawl reminiscent of an elementary schooler tracing copybook characters, was a line of large, reverent characters: [To Miss Lin Ya-Ting, Zhong 4th Road, Ren'ai District, Keelung City].

Affixed neatly in the upper-right corner was an express registered mail stamp worth thirty dollars.

Nian-Zhou flipped open the dark green post office passbook.

The printed black ledger entries filled the yellowed pages from top to bottom:

[2019/03/15 Deposit: 3,000]

[2019/04/18 Deposit: 2,500]

[2019/11/03 Deposit: 1,200]

[2020/01/22 Deposit: 8,000]

[2022/05/19 Deposit: 2,000]

[2023/08/09 Deposit: 3,500]...

Eight years. Over two thousand nine hundred days.

Every single deposited sum was the condensation of blood and sweat from the city's lowest depths. Some were twelve hundred dollars earned from hauling cement on scaffolding for three grueling days; some were an eight-thousand-dollar holiday bonus earned from washing greasy woks seven straight nights through the Lunar New Year without ever returning home on New Year's Eve.

On the final page, the balance line stopped at a distinct, unmistakable figure: [NT$ 302,450].

Hovering beside Nian-Zhou's shoulder, the old man stared fixedly at that sequence of digits, his translucent form heaving with silent tremors:

"Every single thousand-dollar bill in this three hundred thousand... is completely clean."

The old man wept, his voice raspy like sandpaper grinding against stone:

"I scraped this together dollar by dollar, carrying cement until my shoulders peeled raw, scrubbing woks until my skin blistered from spitting oil... There is not a single copper in here tainted by blood or gambling..."

Returning the passbook to the tin, Nian-Zhou slid out the thick wad of letter paper from the waterproof pouch.

It wasn't proper stationery.

It was five yellowed sheets torn from an inexpensive lined notebook. Every single page was crowded with handwriting—clumsy and crooked, packed with phonetic Zhuyin symbols and scratched-out corrections. On the floor beneath the bedside stool, Nian-Zhou spotted a worn, dog-eared copy of the Elementary Chinese Dictionary, its spine broken and pages stained with sweat.

This letter had not been written in haste.

It was the final confession of an uneducated man with barely an elementary school education, penned across six long months beneath a dim lightbulb, wearing reading glasses while thumbing through a broken dictionary and wiping away tears, word by agonizing word:

[Ya-Ting, my precious daughter:]

[By the time your eyes read this letter, your father will likely be dead, or still be a coward too ashamed to ever show his face before you.]

[Thirty years ago, gambling blinded my mind. I lost the ancestral home left by your grandfather and ran up mountains of debt with loan sharks. That winter, your mother burned with severe pneumonia. We couldn't even scrounge five hundred dollars for the clinic registration fee, and she closed her eyes forever on that cold bed. I was an animal, worse than a pig or a dog; I never deserved to be called your father. When the loan sharks came threatening to sever my hands, I was terrified. In the dead of a rainy night, hearing you weep and cry 'Daddy, don't go' behind me, I ran without looking back...]

[All these thirty years, that cry of 'Daddy, don't go' has cut into my heart like a serrated blade in my nightmares every single night. I hid in Taipei washing greasy dishes and carrying cement. Whenever I passed a young woman your age on the street, my heart broke wishing I could ask if you were doing well, but I never dared. I was terrified the curse of my worthless life would shatter the peaceful existence you fought so hard to build.]

[Last month, an old neighbor from Keelung told me at the Wanhua market that you're getting married to an honest, kind junior high school teacher. I was so overjoyed I fell to my knees on the asphalt outside Hsing Tian Kong and kowtowed to the heavens. The gods blessed you; Lin Fu-Lai's daughter wasn't dragged down by her sinful father.]

[These three hundred thousand dollars are the only worldly possession I have left to leave to you. In today's Keelung, it cannot buy half a bathroom, but your father hopes it can buy you the most pristine, beautiful white bridal gown without any regrets.]

[Ya-Ting, listen to your father. On your wedding day, if you spot a wrinkled old man reeking of kitchen smoke watching you from beneath the trees in the distance, do not acknowledge him. Just hold your husband's hand tightly, raise your head high, and walk into that chapel with your most radiant smile. As long as you are happy, your father's soul would taste sweetness even burning in the cauldron of the deepest hell.]

[Wishing you a lifetime of peace and happiness. From a sinful father who does not deserve a name, this final letter.]

At the bottom of the last page, there was no written signature.

Only a dark brown, dried bloodstain—an indelible seal pressed when the old man bit through his own thumb the night he finished the final word.

Inside the cramped, dim tin shack, absolute silence reigned.

Only the first morning breeze outside rattled the corrugated metal with a faint, trembling rhythm.

Hovering mid-air, the old ghost had long dissolved into shattered sobbing. Curled into the corner against the cold concrete floor, he clutched his head with both hands, his translucent shoulders heaving violently as he let out choked, guttural cries from the depths of his throat.

It was a father's thirty-year dirge of penance, written with the blood and tears of an entire lifetime.

Nian-Zhou sat quietly before the exposed floor cavity, his eyes burning faintly.

Taking out a clean handkerchief from his coat, he carefully refolded the five sheets of paper stained with phonetic notes and dried tears, slipped them back into the waterproof pouch, and nestled them securely inside the Danish butter cookie tin alongside the wooden seal and the savings passbook.

Snapping the rusted lid shut, Nian-Zhou wiped away every speck of dust from the tin's surface with his handkerchief.

He stood up, brushed the dust from his jeans, and turned his gaze toward the weeping, near-transparent old man in the corner:

"Done crying?"

The old ghost trembled, lifting tear-streaked eyes to gaze blankly at the young medical examiner.

Without expression, Nian-Zhou produced his dark green leather notebook, clicked his pen, and wrote several decisive lines upon the page:

[Client: Lin Fu-Lai (Dahan River Unidentified Floater Case, Day 1 Post-Mortem)]

[Items Entrusted: NT$300,000 Postal Passbook, Wooden Name Seal, Handwritten Final Letter]

[Recipient: Miss Lin Ya-Ting, Zhong 4th Road, Ren'ai District, Keelung City]

[Time Remaining: 6 Days and 18 Hours]

Closing the notebook, Nian-Zhou packed the tin box carefully into his canvas backpack, pulled the zipper shut, and strapped it against his chest.

Walking to the iron door, he looked back at the old man:

"Mount up."

The old man blinked in bewilderment: "R-Right now? Where are we going?"

Nian-Zhou twitched the corner of his lips, strapping on his helmet:

"Keelung. Didn't your letter tell her not to acknowledge the grease-stained old man under the trees?"

Turning the key, Nian-Zhou kicked the starter peg, and the vintage Wild Wolf's exhaust pipe erupted once more in a cataclysmic blast:

"Since you insist on being a coward hiding in the shade, I'll condescend to moonlighting as your pair of eyes. Hurry up and drift aboard; if you dare soil my backpack on the highway, I'll spend every cent of your three hundred grand on black coffee."

A sudden, burning radiance flared within the old ghost's hollow eyes.

Wiping his spectral face with all his might, his form condensed into a gentle bluish-grey glow, settling softly over the pillion seat of the motorcycle:

"Yes... yes! Doctor Chen... thank you... thank you from the bottom of my soul..."

The first rays of dawn pierced through the heavy morning mist, splashing golden light across the newly awakened city.

A sputtering Wild Wolf motorcycle charged headlong into the morning sun, carrying an intangible yet profoundly heavy soul behind its handlebars—bearing thirty years of the humblest, purest fatherly love, speeding resolutely toward the harbor by the sea.
