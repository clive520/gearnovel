# Chapter 1: The Seventh Day Beneath the Scalpel

2:45 AM.

Basement level 2 of the New Taipei City Funeral Parlor, Autopsy Suite 4.

The air was thick with the suffocating stench of formalin, bleach disinfectant, and the lingering, bitter aroma of canned black coffee. The aging exhaust fan in the basement rattled with a heavy, tractor-like rumble, barricading the incessant, chilly autumn rain outside the thick concrete walls.

Forensic Pathologist Chen Nian-Zhou stood impassively before the washbasin. Frigid tap water gushed over his knuckles as he scrubbed his joints with antiseptic soap, stroke after stroke, in a manner bordering on obsessive-compulsive.

The mirror reflected a face exceedingly youthful yet heavily haggard. Beneath deep-set sockets hung two dark circles as black as coal pits, and bluish-black stubble peppered his pale chin. Swathed in a dark green waterproof dissection apron, his neck bore the sickly, chilled pallor of someone who rarely saw daylight.

"Ah-Da, pass me the No. 7 round-bellied scalpel, and while you're at it, peel off those yellow talismans you taped over the exhaust filter."

Flicking the droplets from his hands, Nian-Zhou snapped on his first layer of taut white latex gloves, his tone completely flat without a ripple of inflection.

"Brother Nian-Zhou! Absolutely not!"

Standing a cautious three meters away from the stainless-steel table, his back pressed firmly against the tiled wall, intern assistant Ah-Da huddled like a terrified prairie dog.

Draped around Ah-Da's neck was a Lord Guan protection amulet from Hsing Tian Kong; his wrist was bound with three coils of temple-blessed red cotton thread from Dajia Jenn Lann Temple, and his white coat pockets bulged with fresh mugwort leaves plucked from the roadside that afternoon. He clasped the instrument tray with both hands, trembling so hard the metal forceps clinked against one another in a frantic, metallic rhythm.

"Brother Nian-Zhou, this is an unidentified floater dredged out of the lower Dahan River this morning!"

Gulping dryly, Ah-Da's voice quivered as though he were on the verge of being exorcised on the spot:

"Didn't you read the preliminary inquest file sent over by the precinct? The body was recovered at the most ominous backwater eddy beneath the Xinhai Bridge! The old-timers say that eddy is a veritable treasure bowl for water ghosts seeking living scapegoats! Besides, Ghost Month barely ended a few days ago, and the boundary between yin and yang is at its most treacherous... My grandma specifically warned that when dealing with drowned floaters, we must purify the room with mugwort and realgar water first..."

"Huang Zhi-Da," Nian-Zhou snapped on his second layer of latex gloves, producing a crisp, rubbery pop, "as a Master of Forensic Pathology earning a civil service salary from the state, you're fumigating the morgue with mugwort until it smells like a street barbecue. If the High Prosecutors Office evaluation committee catches wind of this, your performance rating next month will be visiting King Yanluo for real."

Walking over to the autopsy slab, Nian-Zhou took the No. 7 scalpel from the tray and casually nudged aside the half-drained can of discounted convenience-store black coffee resting on the rim.

"Furthermore, the most terrifying thing in this world is never the dead."

Gazing down at the bulky black body bag before him, Nian-Zhou spoke in a tone as deep as an abyss:

"The dead simply lie on the slab in silence. Only the living will stab you in the back, or dock your overtime pay in the office."

Ah-Da tucked in his neck, not daring to utter another peep, merely hugging the clipboard tighter against his ribs.

"Zzzzzzzip————"

The rasping screech of the zipper sliding open tore abruptly through the icy stillness of the basement.

The heavy-duty body bag peeled back toward both sides, unveiling the deceased resting upon the stainless-steel table.

It was a male in his early sixties, exceedingly gaunt, the distinct ridges of his ribcage starkly visible beneath his sunken chest. He was dressed in a cheap, faded dark-blue nylon windbreaker soaked with river mud and frayed at the cuffs, paired with dark grey cotton trousers caked in black silt.

Nian-Zhou expertly picked up a spray bottle of physiological saline, methodically rinsing away the layer of dark green river weeds and sediment blanketing the dead man's visage.

As the silt washed away, a face carved by decades of harsh elements emerged. The eyes were sunken; the skin was bleached and swollen from prolonged immersion in cold river water, his fingertips and soles exhibiting the classic corrugated wrinkling of "washerwoman's skin."

Lowering his gaze, Nian-Zhou examined the dead man's hands with meticulous care.

They were a pair of hands that made one's heart ache at a single glance. The palms were calloused with yellowed, leathery horn, while the backs of his hands were dappled with deep grease stains and crisscrossed by countless scarred burns of varying depths. The friction ridges of his right thumb and index finger had been eroded flat by industrial solvents or rough masonry, and the subungual spaces were deeply embedded with unyielding black motor oil.

This was not a man who had lived in comfort. This was a laborer who had spent his entire life struggling through the mud at the very bottom of society, expending every ounce of flesh and bone.

Before making the first incision, Nian-Zhou stepped back half a pace, resting his arms at his sides, and bowed his head in a quiet, respectful nod toward the cold stainless-steel table.

"Forgive the intrusion, Mr. Lin."

Nian-Zhou murmured the silent ritual in his heart.

Then, extending the gloved index and middle fingers of his right hand, he pressed gently against the left carotid artery and the supraclavicular fossa, seeking to evaluate the degree of rigor mortis and rule out manual throttling or external ligature marks.

Through the thin latex, his fingertips made contact with the dead man's flesh, cold as winter frost.

The instant they touched—

“HUMMMMM————!”

A deep, ancient chime of a bronze bell detonated with deafening clarity right at the center of Nian-Zhou's skull!

The sound seemed to echo from the deepest abyss of the Netherworld, causing the overhead fluorescent lights to ripple and twist before his eyes. Immediately following, a biting, bone-chilling cold surged up through his fingertips, accompanied by a phantom agony of filthy river water flooding his lungs and his heart convulsing in terminal asphyxiation, slamming violently against his nervous system!

At his chest, three inches beneath his collarbone, flared a familiar, searing burn—like a brand pressed into his flesh.

Nian-Zhou's breath hitched slightly.

He knew all too well what it was: the curse etched into his soul since childhood—the "Seventh-Day Hourglass."

One hundred and sixty-eight hours. The seven-day countdown for the deceased's stay upon the mortal coil had officially begun.

"Sweet Mother of Mercy! Young punk! How the heck are your paws so bloody frigid?!"

A booming, raspy roar steeped in a thick southern Tainan brogue exploded right beside Nian-Zhou's right ear like a string of firecrackers set off without warning!

"It's bad enough you're feeling up my neck, but now you're pinching my collarbone! I drowned, I didn't catch a stiff neck! Is rubbing me there gonna make my blood circulate again?!"

Nian-Zhou's fingers froze mid-air.

His left eyelid twitched three times in violent succession. Drawing in a lungful of cold air laden with formalin, he mentally flipped a textbook middle finger at the ceiling.

Damn it. Here we go again.

In every post-mortem examination, what tormented him most was never the mutilated remains or the stench of decay, but these freshly minted, utterly clueless chatterbox ghosts.

Nian-Zhou impassively shifted his gaze toward the right side of the autopsy table.

There, hovering in mid-air at an unknown moment, was a translucent figure.

It was the spirit of a stooped old man in a shabby blue windbreaker. Bathed in a pale, grayish-blue luminescence, floating a foot above the floor tiles, he was rubbing the back of his neck while glaring indignantly at Nian-Zhou.

After glaring for a few seconds, the old man seemed drawn by the object resting upon the slab, floating forward and craning his spectral neck to peer at the tray:

"Huh? Well, that's mighty strange..."

The old man scratched his disheveled, thinning white hair, muttering in pure bewilderment:

"That old codger lying on the stainless-steel board with the round belly, looking as pale as water-injected pork at the market... why does he look spitting like how I used to look running my street cart when I was younger?"

Nian-Zhou remained frozen in place, his fingers outstretched as if carved from stone.

Watching Nian-Zhou freeze motionless, intern assistant Ah-Da went pale as a sheet, the bundle of mugwort in his hands nearly tumbling to the floor:

"B-Brother Nian-Zhou? What happened? Did your hand cramp up? Or... did that water ghost really... grab your wrist?!"

"Nothing,"

Nian-Zhou slowly withdrew his hand, his tone as mechanical as an automated recording:

"I was merely contemplating a philosophical conundrum."

"What philosophical conundrum?" Ah-Da stammered.

"Why there are so many people in this world who, despite having reached their final hour with their souls detached from their flesh, lack even the most fundamental awareness that they are thoroughly dead."

The instant those words fell, the old phantom hovering mid-air jolted violently.

Like a stray cat whose tail had been stepped on, the old man jabbed an accusatory finger at Nian-Zhou's nose, hollering:

"Hey! Young punk! Who are you calling thoroughly dead?! I'm in tip-top shape, alright! I wake up at four every morning to push wheelbarrows at the construction site, and I can down two massive bowls of braised pork rice for lunch! Just yesterday evening, I wrestled through the wet market to snag two bunches of water spinach for ten bucks! How on earth am I dead..."

The old man's furious rant was strangled midway in his throat.

Because his eyes fell upon his own hands.

They were nearly transparent hands.

He stared dumbfounded at his palms, able to see right through the spectral skin and bone down to the greyish-white floor tiles of the morgue.

Next, the old man attempted to take a step forward.

His body did not collide with the metal utility cart before him; instead, he drifted effortlessly, gliding straight through the entire stainless-steel trolley stacked with surgical clamps!

"HOLY CRAP ON A CRACKER——!!"

A blood-curdling screech detonated against the ceiling of the autopsy suite!

The old ghost bounced backward like a compressed spring, his back slamming against the overhead air ducts. His limbs flailed and kicked wildly in the air like an overturned frog tossed onto dry land:

"Ghosts! It's a bloody ghost! How am I floating?! My... my body is down there... Did I really kick the bucket?!"

"Quiet down. Shut your mouth."

Nian-Zhou lifted the metal scalpel, the icy words forced through the seam of his dark blue surgical mask.

Ah-Da's knees gave out completely, and he hit the tiled floor with a loud thud, real tears bursting from his eyes on the spot:

"B-Brother Nian-Zhou! Who on earth are you telling to shut their mouth?! There are only two living people and one corpse in this room! Stop terrifying me, please! I swear I just felt a freezing gale of minus-ten-degree ghost wind whip past the crown of my head... My grandma was right, there's really a water ghost here trying to drag me down as a scapegoat, waaaah!"

Nian-Zhou looked down coldly, his eyes gazing upon the kneeling assistant from on high:

"Huang Zhi-Da, I am telling you to shut your mouth. Go to the office and fetch the external examination log for unidentified persons. Right now. Immediately."

"Yes! I'm scrambling! I'll get out of your sight right this second!"

Clutching his pockets full of mugwort, Ah-Da scrambled on all fours as if pursued by eight hundred vengeful demons, bolting through the doorway and slamming the heavy steel fire door shut with a tremendous crash.

Inside the dissection suite, only a cold corpse, a living coroner, and a floating ghost remained.

The old man drifted slowly down from the ceiling.

He circled Nian-Zhou twice, his cloudy, translucent eyes bulging like bronze bells, his expression brimming with incredulous terror and probing shock:

"Young punk... you can see me? You were... really listening to me talk just now?!"

Nian-Zhou did not look up.

His left hand gently stabilized the dead man's cold, rigid ribs while his right hand gripped the sharp scalpel. His voice dropped to a low, unforgiving rumble without a trace of warmth:

"You can see me, and I can see you. But I am extremely busy and under no obligation to make small talk with a freshly minted ghost. You currently have two options: first, float quietly in the ceiling corner until I finish your external examination and tissue sampling; second, continue shouting, and I guarantee that when I suture your chest later, I will sew your flesh shut exceedingly lopsided."

The old man froze entirely.

He looked down at his lifeless shell on the autopsy table, then looked up at the sharp-eyed, ruthless young medical examiner.

After three full seconds of silence, the old man suddenly let out a gut-wrenching wail and threw himself flat before Nian-Zhou's table!

His translucent palms clasped together in desperate supplication like a drowning man seizing a floating reed, his tone instantly shifting from uncouth bravado to humble, weeping misery:

"Doctor! Venerable Master Coroner! Jade Emperor above! Save me! I can't die! I really, truly cannot die just like this!"

"You are already dead."

Nian-Zhou aligned the blade along the midline of the dead man's chest, his voice clinical and unsparing:

"Judging by your core temperature and post-mortem rigidity, your heart stopped at least twelve hours ago. Your lungs inhaled vast quantities of river silt, resulting in irreversible mechanical asphyxiation. Even if supreme immortals descended with defibrillator paddles right now, they could not buy you a single second of heartbeat. Furthermore, step back thirty centimeters; your soul emits a sub-zero draft that impairs my muscular precision."

"No, that's not it! I'm not asking you to save my physical life!"

Hovering mid-air, the old man stamped his ethereal feet in frantic agitation, the pale spiritual mist around his eyes swirling violently as a profound, bone-deep sorrow leaked from his voice:

"My passbook! And my letter! My... my daughter is getting married next Sunday!"

The thumb of Nian-Zhou's right hand gripping the scalpel twitched ever so slightly.

"Your daughter?" Nian-Zhou's eyelids lowered.

"Yes... my daughter up in Keelung!"

The old ghost sniffled at a nose devoid of mucus, rubbing awkwardly at dry phantom eyes with his tattered sleeve. Deep within his gaze surged thirty years of unfading remorse, like an ancient ulcer ripped raw once more:

"As a father... I wasn't human. I was an absolute, irredeemable monster."

Hovering in the air, his spirit curled inward like a dying shrimp:

"Thirty years ago, Ya-Ting was barely six years old... I got hooked on Mark Six lottery and underground gambling dens, squandering our only dilapidated house and piling up massive debts with loan sharks. That winter, Ya-Ting's mother came down with severe pneumonia and burning fever. We couldn't even scrape together five hundred dollars for the hospital clinic registration fee, and she died just like that... When the loan sharks showed up threatening to chop off my hands and feet, I was terrified. In the dead of a rainy night, leaving behind little Ya-Ting who was weeping and calling for her daddy... I fled alone."

The old man's voice broke into a choked tremor, his translucent shoulders shaking with violent sobs:

"All these thirty years... I hid in Taipei, never daring to use a national health insurance card or an identity document. I spent my whole life scrubbing scorching grease woks at stir-fry eateries—those massive cast-iron pans were heavy and scalding, and the caustic detergents ate away at my skin until not an inch was whole. By day, I lugged fifty-kilogram bags of cement on construction sites, carrying them until my spine could no longer straighten..."

He pointed toward his rough, blackened hands on the autopsy table, covered in calluses, burns, and scars, weeping bitterly:

"I lived in a tin rooftop shack in Sanchong under four ping in size. In summer, it was scorching enough to peel my skin; in winter, the cold penetrated right into my aching bones. I couldn't bear to spend money on meat, eating only plain steamed buns with fermented bean curd every single day... After all that scraping and pinching, saving a thousand here and five hundred there... I scraped together a whole three hundred thousand dollars..."

Nian-Zhou listened quietly without interrupting.

"Last month, I bumped into an old neighbor from Keelung at the Wanhua wet market."

A faint, tender glimmer rekindled in the old man's eyes—the sole redemption amidst an abyss of darkness:

"My old neighbor said Ya-Ting grew up so strong and capable. She works as an accountant in Keelung and is getting married next Sunday to an honest, gentle junior high school teacher..."

"The night I learned about it, I was so overwhelmed with joy that my whole body trembled inside that tin shack. I couldn't sleep a wink all night; I walked all the way to the pavement outside Hsing Tian Kong, knelt on the stone, and kowtowed ten times to the gods..."

His gaze dimmed once more, sinking back into boundless self-reproach and desolate grief:

"I didn't have the courage to acknowledge her. I reek of kitchen grease from head to toe, my hands covered in grime and scabs. How could I have the face to show up at her beautiful, pristine wedding? I was terrified my shameful past would bring disgrace upon her, making her future in-laws look down on her..."

The old man clutched his head with both hands, wailing in helpless torment as he floated:

"I had planned... just last night, to pack the passbook, personal seal, and a letter together, and take them to the post office for express registered delivery... as the only bridal dowry I could ever offer her in this life..."

"But last midnight the rain poured down so heavily. Terrified the letter would get soaked, I tucked it deep inside my inner shirt. I walked too hurriedly along the riverside park in the pitch-black darkness... my foot slipped, and I plunged straight into the Dahan River..."

Hovering mid-air, he looked down upon his cold corpse coated in river mud, curling into a tight ball, his voice breaking with heart-wrenching humility:

"Young punk... Doctor Chen... I know that in today's society, three hundred thousand dollars can't even buy half a bathroom in downtown Keelung..."

"Yet... yet that is the only treasure I have left to bequeath to her after steeping my life in grease woks and cement dust for thirty years..."

"I beg of you... at least let her purchase a decent white bridal gown... Let her know that her good-for-nothing old man who deserves to rot in hell never went a single day without missing her... never went a single day forgetting her..."

In the quiet of the morgue, only the monotonous, heavy hum of the exhaust fan persisted.

The air bit with razor-sharp chill.

Nian-Zhou quietly gazed upon the old soul weeping until he was nearly transparent.

This was not a homicide case, nor did it conceal any grand conspiracy. It was merely a cowardly father who had crawled through guilt and penance his entire life, yearning with every ounce of his remaining dignity to fulfill one final act before the end.

Nian-Zhou raised his left wrist, checking his watch.

3:02 AM.

The pulse on the inside of his wrist beat with steady cadence, while the burning sensation at his chest served as an unambiguous reminder: the time remaining for this soul upon the mortal plane was exactly one hundred and sixty-seven hours and forty-three minutes.

"Old man," Nian-Zhou spoke coolly.

The ghost looked up in a daze, gazing at him through misty eyes.

"Where did you stash the passbook and the letter?"

A burst of wild, incredulous delight detonated within the phantom's eyes as he stammered:

"In... in the rooftop tin shack on Section 2, Lixing Road in Sanchong! Under the third loose red floor tile to your left when you walk in! I packed it in a Danish butter cookie tin! Wrapped in three layers of waterproof bags on the outside!"

"Understood."

Withdrawing his gaze, Nian-Zhou brought the No. 7 scalpel down steadily along the midline of the deceased's sternum.

The cold blade parted the skin with a faint, crisp parting sound. Nian-Zhou's gaze was focused and surgical, his tone still aloof, yet carrying the heavy gravity of an unspoken oath:

"I am only responsible for running the errand; I am not responsible for wiping your tears. When the underworld constables come to arrest you at midnight on your seventh day, remember to give me a five-star review before King Yanluo, and pray my civil service evaluation next month doesn't get docked to a B."

The old man stared blankly at this young coroner—masked, hollow-eyed, sharp-tongued, yet guiding the blade with exquisite gentleness.

Amidst the biting, pungent fumes of formalin, the old ghost bowed deeply, his translucent form trembling under the harsh fluorescent glare:

"Thank you... Doctor Chen... thank you so very much..."

The scalpel glided onward in silent grace.

The basement remained as cold and desolate as ever, yet before this dissection table that had witnessed countless frigid departures, the final lingering warmth of the human world was quietly chasing away the bitter frost of death.
