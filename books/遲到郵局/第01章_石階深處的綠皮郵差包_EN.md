# 《遲到郵局》（The Overdue Post Office）

## 第一卷：修復自我之信 / Volume I: Letters of Self-Restoration
### 第 01 章：石階深處的綠皮郵差包 / Chapter 01: The Green Leather Mailbag in the Depths of Stone Stairs

---

#### 1

Jiufen's rain did not fall straight from the heavens.

It drifted up slowly from the leaden gray surface of the open sea beyond Keelung Islet, carried by the damp, chilling northeast monsoon. Like a layer of faded, discarded gauze, it clung stratum by stratum to the tiered, tar-felt roofs of the mountain town, before trickling noiselessly down the deep, sunken blue-stone stairways worn smooth by centuries of footsteps, seeping into the marrow of anyone who dared walk among them.

Lin Zi-yang pulled the hood of his sweatshirt down low, thrusting both hands deep into his denim pockets.

His sneakers had long since soaked through; with every step down the waterlogged stone stairs, the rubber soles let out an irritable, squelching squish. The seventeen-year-old boy kept his jawline clamped tight, his lips pulled down at the corners, radiating a deliberate, bristling aura of cold detachment and hostility.

Deep within his right pocket, his fingers were tightly crumpled around a folded official document, softened by sweat and moisture. It was a special protective disposition issued just last week by the Juvenile Court of the Keelung District Court, stamped with vivid red cinnabar seal ink:

*【Juvenile Protective Disposition No. 0418】*  
*【Person Subject to Disposition: Lin Zi-yang (Male, 17 years old)】*  
*【Cause of Action: Unlicensed late-night riding of a modified motorcycle, reckless speeding, destruction of public historical property, and hit-and-run】*  
*【Order of Disposition: Reformatory education waived; assigned effective immediately to Special Community Observation Station No. 7, Jinguashi, Ruifang District, Keelung City, to complete 120 days of mandatory community labor and character probation.】*

"Special Community Observation Station No. 7..." Zi-yang muttered a curse under his breath, spitting out a plume of pale condensation.

He lifted his head.

On either side of the stairway stood the shuttered storefronts of taro ball stalls and teahouses, their red paper lanterns swaying listlessly in the raw, wet night wind. Following the hand-drawn map on the back of the judicial summons, he bypassed the uppermost landing of Shuqi Road, squeezed through a dark alley so narrow that only a single person could pass sideways, and stepped onto a derelict mining trail stairway covered in slick, ancient moss, where even the streetlights had ceased to function.

At the very terminus of those stone stairs, an immense, century-old camphor tree—so thick it would take four grown men with outstretched arms to encircle it—towered over the edge of a sheer cliff.

The gnarled limbs of the ancient camphor stretched wildly toward the pitch-black ocean below. Tucked into the sheltered recess beside its trunk stood a quiet, two-story Western-style brick building.

Dark green maidenhair ferns grew profusely from the mortar joints between the red bricks. The roof was pitched with weathered Japanese black ceramic tiles, and the windows were all framed by aged wooden cross-lattices. Beside the entryway stood a cylindrical cast-iron postbox that reached up to one's chest—not the modern, utilitarian square sheet-metal bins, but an antique Meiji-34 pattern pillar postbox inherited from the Japanese colonial period, coated in a thick skin of dark green lacquer and crowned with a domed iron cap.

Above the peeling, battered wooden entrance hung a lacquered timber sign inscribed in golden calligraphy. In the softening mist of the drizzle, beneath the dim, warm amber glow of a solitary tungsten porch bulb, four large characters gleamed with a quiet, weathered dignity:

*【The Overdue Post Office】*

"The Overdue Post Office...?" Zi-yang furrowed his brows, a scoffing, contemptuous smirk tugging at his lips. "Even social service agencies nowadays come up with chuunibyou names like this?"

He raised his knuckles, took hold of a tarnished bronze lion-head door knocker affixed to the panel, and struck it heavily twice.

*Clang. Clang.*

The impact of bronze against the thick, centuries-old Formosan cypress timber produced not a sharp rap, but a deep, resonant rumble, akin to an ancient bell being lowered into a bottomless pond.

From within came the leisurely, deliberate sound of footsteps.

It was not the crisp click of hard leather soles, but the soft scuff of cloth slippers, accompanied by the gentle tapping of a bamboo walking cane: *Tap, scuff. Tap, scuff.*

With a prolonged, resonant creak steeped in the fragrance of tung oil, the wooden door swung smoothly inward.

Standing framed in the doorway was a lean, slightly stooped elderly gentleman.

He wore a faded, meticulously pressed dark-green postal uniform of a vintage cut, buttoned conscientiously all the way up to his throat. Pinned to his lapel was a silver Flying Goose postal insignia whose sharp edges had long been rounded by the friction of years. His hair was entirely snow-white and combed neatly back; his face was gaunt and weathered, crisscrossed with wrinkles as deep and textured as the bark of the old camphor tree outside. Yet behind his wire-rimmed reading glasses, his eyes were remarkably clear, calm, and serene, like the surface of an alpine lake in late autumn.

The old man cast an appraising glance over Zi-yang. His gaze lingered for half a second on the boy’s rain-soaked hair, his clenched jawline, and the crumpled legal paper bulging in his pocket.

Then, leaning slightly aside, the old postmaster used a withered yet steady hand to swing the heavy door wide open:

"Forty-three minutes later than the scheduled reporting hour. Though... inside this post office, no one ever arrives on time."

The old gentleman turned serenely, his cane tapping lightly against the stone floor:

"Come in, Student Lin Zi-yang. Shut the door firmly behind you. The mountain air is heavy with dampness; we cannot afford to let the cold rain rot the stationery preserved within."

---

#### 2

The moment he stepped across the threshold, Zi-yang froze in his tracks.

The whistling wind and torrential rain outside seemed, at the exact instant the wooden door sealed shut, to be utterly swallowed by an invisible, suffocating layer of heavy velvet.

The air inside was extraordinarily dry and warm, permeated with a rich mingling of aged fountain pen ink, pine resin, sun-cured straw paper, and the comforting sweetness of longan-red date tea simmering atop an iron stove.

It bore zero resemblance to any modern postal counter or bureaucratic government facility.

In the center of the hall stood no bulletproof acrylic glass partitions, only a massive, four-meter-long sorting table planed from a single slab of virgin Formosan red cypress. Stacked upon this timber expanse were dozens of rows of solid wood pigeonholes of varying heights, each labeled in meticulous regular script with a place name and an era—ranging from "Jishan Street, Jiufen · 34th Year of the Republic," extending through "Fifth Adit, Benshan, Jinguashi · 72nd Year of the Republic," and all the way back to "Dadaocheng, Taihoku · 16th Year of Showa."

Thousands of envelopes rested neatly within the slots. Some were brittle and yellowed, others sealed with thick cakes of sealing wax, while still others were fashioned by hand out of coarse kraft wrapping paper.

At the far end of the red cypress table, an antique desk lamp with an emerald-green glass banker's shade cast a soothing pool of amber light. Curled up atop a felt wool cushion was a solid black cat, its fur gleaming like polished velvet, purring softly and steadily without even deigning to open an eye.

"Take off your drenched jacket and hang it on the charcoal drying rack behind the door."

The old man had already made his way to a wide, cane-woven rocking chair behind the table and seated himself. Lifting a coarse ceramic teapot from the stove, he poured a steaming cup of deep amber brew and slid it gently toward the edge of the wood:

"Old ginger and dried longan tea; it dispels the chill. Drink it dry, then place the Juvenile Court document on the table."

Zi-yang stiffly peeled off his dripping hoodie, hung it by the door, and walked up to the timber counter. He made no move to touch the hot tea; instead, wearing his defensive, defiant smirk like armor, he slammed the crumpled court paper onto the red cypress surface:

"Look, old man, let's get things straight right now. The judge sentenced me to one hundred and twenty days of community labor. I'm here to clear my criminal record, not to put on an emotional act of 'rehabilitation and redemption' for you. Cleaning the toilets, sweeping leaves, hauling heavy crates—assign whatever grunt work you want. When the time is up, stamp my paperwork so I can walk away, and we'll be square."

The old man lifted his white porcelain teacup, blew lightly across the floating leaves, and took a measured sip.

He displayed not the slightest irritation at the boy's brash insolence, nor did he preach a single moral sermon. He merely took off his reading glasses, slowly wiped the lenses clean with a piece of soft chamois cloth, and spoke in an even, tranquil tone:

"My name is Gu Yuan-shan. Inside this post office, others call me Old Gu, or the Old Postmaster."

Old Gu put his glasses back on and raised his gaze, meeting Zi-yang's prickly glare with absolute stillness:

"Lin Zi-yang, seventeen years old. Suspended from his sophomore year in the evening mechanical division of Keelung Vocational High School. Father is a chief engineer on a deep-sea fishing vessel, absent all year round; mother runs a noodle stall at the Keelung Miaokou Night Market, working sixteen hours every day."

Old Gu's cadence was steady and unhurried, as though reciting the lines of a routine postcard:

"Last Thursday at two forty-five in the morning, riding a modified YAMAHA motorcycle with altered exhaust and ignition coils on Ruijin Highway, you lost control while attempting an overtake on a hairpin curve. You demolished the eighty-year-old cast-iron shop sign hanging outside 'Ah-Quan's Blacksmith Shop,' a three-generation heritage forge in Jinguashi. The tumbling sign sheared through the porch post, and shattered iron fragments pierced an antique Acacia-wood forge bench dating back to the Ming Dynasty."

Zi-yang's pupils contracted slightly, and the fingers hidden beneath the timber edge curled tightly into fists.

"After the collision, you realized no one had come out of the dark shop," Old Gu continued, his voice devoid of accusation, stating pure facts. "You picked yourself up, saw that the engine would still turn over, killed your headlights, and coasted in the dark all the way down the mountain road back to Keelung."

"The following afternoon, police reviewed the infrared wildlife surveillance cameras installed along the forestry trail, matched the scrape patterns on your reflective license plate strip, and apprehended you in a private booth at an internet cafe."

"In court, when the magistrate asked you: why did you not knock on the door to apologize? Why did you not leave a note?"

Old Gu paused. Those calm, clear eyes drilled straight into Zi-yang's pale lips:

"You answered the magistrate: 'It was just a piece of scrap iron and rotten firewood; at worst, I'll earn money later to pay him back. In the dead of night, who was I leaving a note for anyway? Besides, nobody got killed—what's the big deal?'"

"Nobody DID get killed!" Zi-yang flared up like a cornered stray, his voice cracking into an angry shout, his chest heaving violently. "That decrepit shop was about to collapse on its own! Nobody goes there anymore! I broke a piece of junk metal, and I accept the fine! If the court wants money, my mom will borrow it! Why did they sentence me to be locked up in a dump like this for four whole months?! What right do you have to treat me like a criminal?!"

The young boy's furious roar bounced against the high ceilings of the old Western-style house.

Atop the desk, the sleeping black cat merely twitched an ear, shifted its posture, and drifted back to sleep.

Old Gu looked quietly at the panting, crimson-faced Lin Zi-yang. Only after the boy's shouts had completely dissipated into the heavy timber rafters did the elder slowly extend a withered right hand to slide open the central drawer of the red cypress desk.

The drawer rolled out with a muted metallic purr.

From its deepest recesses, Old Gu brought forth an aged, dark-green cowhide mailbag whose leather was rubbed white at the corners and stitched by hand with coarse hemp thread along its seams.

Directly beneath the polished bronze postal crest on the front flap rested a solitary, meticulously inserted letter.

It was an envelope crafted from ordinary brown kraft paper. Its edges were undulating and water-stained, warped from being soaked in rain and dried repeatedly across the decades. Upon its front, penned in a trembling fountain pen hand, was the recipient's address:

*【No. 7, Sector A, Under the Stone Stairs, Jinguashi, Ruifang Township, Taipei County / Ah-Quan's Blacksmith Shop / For the Attention of Master Zhang Quan-sheng】*

And in the sender's box, there were only three brush-written characters:

*【From the Deserter】*

"What is this?" Zi-yang stared at the envelope, his brows knotting into a scowl.

"This is a letter that has been overdue for thirty-two years."

Old Gu extended a calloused finger, sliding the deceptively heavy green leather satchel across the timber expanse toward Zi-yang:

"Thirty-two years ago, there was another seventeen-year-old boy, just like you. On a midnight drowned in torrential rain, he drank alcohol, rode a stolen bicycle, and crashed through the bamboo shed storing charcoal outside Ah-Quan's Blacksmith Shop. The impact triggered a smoldering fire. Though it did not consume the house, the residual heat ruined a 'Nine-Ring Purple Copper Censer Shovel' that Master Zhang Quan-sheng had spent three whole years forging by hand for the Keelung Mazu Pilgrimage, melting it into slag."

Old Gu's voice was low and gravelly, like mountain wind whistling through an abandoned mine shaft:

"That boy was terrified out of his wits. He scrambled away on all fours and fled into the night. The next day, he penned this letter of restitution, promising he would work any odd job to save money and buy the finest purple copper ingots to pay Master Zhang back. But... when he walked to the threshold of the forge and saw Master Zhang sitting before the charred ruins smoking his dry pipe without uttering a single word, the boy's knees turned to jelly. He shoved the letter into the old iron postbox by the roadside."

"He thought that as long as the letter was mailed, the weight of guilt gnawing at his conscience would lighten."

"What he did not understand was this: the rule of this post office is that **any letter lacking the courage of an in-person reckoning can never carry a mailman beyond our front doors.**"

Old Gu slowly rose to his feet, his tall, stooped shadow falling across Zi-yang like a mantle. In the elder's eyes, for the very first time, flashed a severe, immovable gravity reminiscent of ancient mountains:

"Lin Zi-yang. Your community labor is not sweeping floors, nor is it hauling bricks."

"Slap this mailbag across your shoulder."

"Walk down to the very foot of the stone stairs, to Ah-Quan's Blacksmith Shop. Take the letter that your 'predecessor' lacked the guts to hand over thirty-two years ago, along with the cast-iron sign you smashed last week—"

"And deliver them directly into Master Zhang's hands."

---

#### 3

The stone stairs of Jinguashi resembled an endless slide carved from slick blue rock in the pitch-black night.

The rain was falling in torrents now.

Icy sheets of water whipped against Zi-yang's thin sweatshirt, carried on the raw ocean gale. Yet what chilled him to the bone was not the storm water, but the green leather mailbag slung across his chest.

It was visibly nothing more than an aged cowhide satchel holding a single parchment envelope, yet the moment Zi-yang settled the strap over his shoulder, the leather band seemed to transmute into a bar of solid cast iron, biting relentlessly into the flesh of his collarbone.

With every sodden step he took down the slick, mossy stairs, the uncanny, leaden drag from the bottom of the bag forced his breathing to grow shallower and more ragged.

"Crazy old lunatic... playing ghosts and gods..."

Zi-yang cursed under his breath, steadying himself against the damp stone wall as he descended.

Half an hour later, at the absolute foot of the stone stairway near the tracks of a disused light rail trolley, a low-slung, dark-tiled stone cottage materialized out of the rain and fog.

The cast-iron sign that had hung above the door for eighty years was gone, leaving only a jagged, sheared-off granite pillar jutting raw into the wind. Across the stone pavement before the threshold, black, dried skid marks from motorcycle tires were still clearly etched into the rock.

This was the scene of the hit-and-run Zi-yang had fled four nights prior.

Inside the cottage, no modern fluorescent tube was illuminated; only a low-hanging, dim tungsten bulb swayed gently in the draft leaking through the doorframe.

Looking in through the open timber plank door, the air was saturated with the heavy stench of burned charcoal, the metallic tang of oxidized iron filings, and the pungent sting of scorched machine oil.

A gaunt, severely hunched old man in a thick, soot-stained cowhide apron knelt on the floor, holding a heavy wire brush, scrubbing with labored, agonizing motions beside a massive Acacia-wood workbench riddled with gouges and fractures.

With every sweep of the brush, a raspy, asthmatic wheeze tore from the old man's windpipe: *Haaah—shuck. Haaah—shuck.*

Zi-yang halted three paces outside the threshold, stranded in the pelting rain.

His legs felt as though they had been filled with liquid lead; he could not force his feet to take a single step forward.

Four nights ago, seeing the dark storefront shatter in his rearview mirror, he had dismissed it as nothing more than a worthless heap of junk. But now, separated by less than three meters of night air, he could see with devastating clarity:

The old man's right hand was wrapped in thick, grubby gauze—an injury sustained that night from the jagged flying shrapnel of the iron sign. And that ancient Acacia bench, split down the middle by the impact of his handlebars, was being held together by dozens of strands of baling wire and hemp twine that the old man was patiently, fruitlessly attempting to lash back into one piece.

"Who's standing out there catching a chill in the rain?"

The old blacksmith did not lift his head. The wire brush continued to scrape rhythmically against the darkened anvil, his voice as coarse as two sheets of emery paper rubbing together:

"Need a cleaver or a hoe? The forge... isn't taking work today. The sign is broken, the spirit is scattered. Can't forge good iron anymore."

The words lodged in Zi-yang's throat like a fistful of dry cotton soaked in ice water.

He opened his mouth, trying to spit out the glib, dismissive line he had rehearsed a hundred times in his head: *'Hey, old guy, here's a letter for you; take it, I'm out.'*

Yet not a single syllable would emerge.

The green leather satchel across his shoulder seemed at that instant to weigh as much as Mount Jade itself, pressing down upon his collarbone and sternum with such crushing force that his knees buckled involuntarily.

He was afraid.

In all seventeen years of his life, talking back to school deans, street racing with delinquent crews, brawling in alleys, pulling all-nighters in smoky gaming cafes... he had fancied himself fearless, an untamable rebel who took nothing from anyone.

Yet before this frail, hunchbacked old blacksmith who could barely straighten his spine, before the shattered ruins he had so casually wrought—

For the very first time in his existence, he understood the meaning of **abject, suffocating shame**.

"Master... Ah-Quan..."

Zi-yang finally dragged those three words from between his clenched teeth. His voice trembled so violently that he barely recognized the sound.

The scraping wire brush stopped dead.

The old blacksmith slowly turned around. Through eyes reddened and clouded by decades of coal dust and forge smoke, he stared through the beaded curtain of rain at the drenched boy standing upon his stoop.

The elder's gaze dropped straight to the green leather mailbag.

The instant he saw that weathered satchel, the old blacksmith's face, carved with liver spots and burn scars, twitched with a profound, violent tremor.

"The green leather bag..." The blacksmith let go of the wire brush, braced his trembling palms against his knees, and struggled upright, shuffling toward the threshold. "Thirty years... Brother Gu... has finally sent that letter...?"

---

#### 4

With shaking fingers, Zi-yang reached down and unfastened the cold brass buckle of the mailbag.

He drew out the envelope.

The kraft paper exuded a faint scent of pine resin in the mountain mist. Holding the envelope in both palms, Zi-yang stepped across the threshold, his sodden shoes leaving muddy tracks across the floor covered in iron dust.

"Old Gu didn't send this."

Zi-yang came to a halt half a meter before the old blacksmith. His head hung so low that his chin nearly brushed his chest, not daring to meet the elder's bandaged right hand:

"This letter... was written thirty-two years ago by the guy who wrecked your purple copper shovel. But today... the person delivering it is me."

Zi-yang drew a long, ragged lungful of the acrid air, his vision blurring with hot, sudden moisture. Gathering every scrap of strength left in his body, he bent his spine and bowed deeply toward the old blacksmith.

It was an angle well past ninety degrees.

His forehead dipped so low it nearly grazed his chilled kneecaps:

"And... I'M SORRY!"

The boy's voice shattered the stillness of the small forge, vibrating with a raw, cracking sob that tore open the floodgates of his tears:

"Four nights ago... the coward who was speeding on his bike, who smashed your sign, cracked your bench, and ran like a dog in the dark... was ME! It was me! I don't have any money... I only have two hundred dollars on me right now... but I'll work! Every day after school, I'll come here to pump the bellows, haul coal, sweep the iron shavings! I'll earn the money to forge you a brand-new sign! I'm sorry... I'm so sorry!!"

Scalding tears cascaded down his cheeks, splashing onto the cold, dust-covered stone floor, kicking up tiny clouds of dark soot.

A long, suffocating silence descended upon the workshop.

Only the sound of rain dripping from the eaves broke the quiet: *Drip. Drop.*

A rough, warm hand—thick with calluses and scarred by hot sparks—settled gently onto the crown of Zi-yang's soaked head.

The old blacksmith did not strike him, nor did he curse.

That weathered palm merely rubbed the boy's dripping hair with a slow, tender patience.

"Silly boy..." The old blacksmith let out a long, shuddering sigh, his voice carrying the weathered warmth of sea and stone:

"Lift your head up. The floor is cold."

Sniffling hard, Zi-yang raised his tear-streaked face.

The old blacksmith took the yellowed envelope from his trembling hands. The elder did not tear it open; he simply used his rough, calloused thumb to caress the faded fountain pen script on the front, over and over: *【Ah-Quan's Blacksmith Shop / For the Attention of Master Zhang Quan-sheng】*.

Two trails of clear tears escaped the corners of the old man's cloudy eyes, tracking silently down through his gray whiskers.

"Thirty-two years..." the blacksmith whispered to himself, a faint, peaceful smile touching his lips. "The boy who wrote this... died the following year in an industrial crane accident down at Keelung Port. Before he passed, he had someone bring me a message, saying the thing he regretted most in his whole life was that purple copper censer shovel."

The old blacksmith looked up, gazing into Zi-yang's reddened eyes:

"I've waited for this letter for thirty-two years. I wasn't waiting for that shovel, nor was I waiting for money. I just wanted to know... after he ran away, whether that boy ever learned inside his heart—**the true weight of the words 'I am sorry.'**"

The blacksmith turned, walked back to the split Acacia-wood workbench, and reverently placed the weathered letter directly beneath the heavy cast-iron anvil:

"Today, the letter has arrived."

The old man glanced back at Zi-yang, his gaze as gentle as dusk falling over the mountains:

"Child, a broken sign can be recast. A split workbench can be bound tight with iron hoops."

"But if a person's heart breaks, if their spine turns to jelly, if they learn to flee and lie their way through life... then for the rest of their days, they will never be able to forge iron strong enough to stand upright between heaven and earth."

The old blacksmith picked up a small shaping hammer and tapped it gently against the anvil's horn.

*CLANG————!*

A crisp, resonant, bell-like ring sang out from the depths of the stone forge, piercing through the thick mountain mist of Jiufen, echoing long and true across the slumbering valley.

---

#### 5

Two o'clock in the morning.

When Zi-yang dragged his exhausted legs back up the stone stairs to the "Overdue Post Office" beneath the ancient camphor tree, the rain had tapered off into a fine, swirling white mist.

The heavy timber door was left ajar, a beam of warm amber light spilling out across the blue-stone slabs.

Zi-yang pushed the door open.

Old Gu still sat in his woven rocking chair, holding a dog-eared volume of *The Centennial Chronicles of Taiwan's Postal Service*. At the edge of the long red cypress table, the bowl of ginger tea had long since stopped steaming, but upon the iron stove, a fresh ceramic kettle was humming softly with heat.

Zi-yang walked up to the counter.

He unbuckled the green leather mailbag, folded its strap neatly, and laid it flat upon the center of the wood.

Strangely enough—this time, the bag possessed none of that crushing, leaden weight. It felt supple, light, carrying only the honest scent of cured cowhide and the washed clarity of rain.

"Was the letter delivered?" Old Gu set down his book, looking up at him.

"Delivered," Zi-yang replied, lowering his head. His voice was hoarse, but those eyes, once bristling with hostility and defiance, were now as clear as crystal washed clean by mountain springs.

"What did Master Zhang say?"

"He said... a broken sign can be recast." Zi-yang took a deep, steady breath, lifting his chin to meet the old postmaster's tranquil gaze:

"He said... if a man learns to run away, he'll never be able to forge hard iron again."

Old Gu studied him quietly, a faint, deeply gratified smile blooming in the corners of his eyes.

The elder slid open the wooden drawer, retrieved the Juvenile Court document, picked up the timber official seal soaked in red ink, and pressed it down firmly into the first blank box marked "Day One Evaluation."

*Thump.*

A vivid crimson square bloomed upon the paper.

"Go take a hot bath in the back. There are dry blankets in the side wing," Old Gu murmured, lifting his fresh cup of tea:

"Tomorrow morning at six o'clock, the second letter will be waiting for you."

"Student Lin Zi-yang."

Zi-yang stood by the counter, gazing at the old man's snow-white hair and the warm emerald banker's lamp. After a long moment, the seventeen-year-old youth bent his waist and bowed deeply toward the elder:

"Yes. Old Postmaster Gu."

The boy turned and walked down the creaking wooden corridor toward the back rooms.

And behind him, beneath the ancient camphor tree, the red-brick Western house of Jiufen kept its lonely, gentle light burning bright, piercing through decades of mist, warming the mountain night.

> *We foolishly imagine that if we run fast enough, if no eyes bear witness, our errors will simply scatter into the wind with the passage of days. Yet all worldly evasion serves only to erect an impassable wall of lonely stone within our spirit.*  
> 
> *承擔過錯的第一步，往往伴隨著撕裂自尊的劇痛與無地自容的冷汗。*  
> *但唯有敢於在受傷的人面前低下頭、親口說出一聲「對不起」的人——*  
> *他的雙肩才能卸下逃亡的枷鎖，他的脊樑，才能真正頂天立地地走出陰霾，打出生命中最堅硬的鐵。*  
> 
> *Taking the first step to shoulder our transgressions is invariably accompanied by the tearing agony of wounded pride and cold sweat of shame.*  
> *Yet only one who possesses the courage to lower their head before those they have hurt, to utter with their own lips the words "I am sorry"—*  
> *Only their shoulders can cast off the shackles of flight; only their spine can stand tall and upright beneath the sky, forging the hardest, truest iron of this life.*

### 💡 Story Encyclopedia: Centennial Taiwan Postal History and the Heritage of the Green Canvas Mailbag

* **Historical Legacy of the Green Canvas Mailbag**:
  Across the early chapters of Taiwan's postal history, the military-green heavy canvas mailbag stood as an enduring collective memory for generations. Stitched by hand from high-density, water-resistant cotton canvas and reinforced at the edges with raw cowhide, its wide, padded shoulder strap was engineered to distribute the punishing weight of dozens of kilograms of correspondence. Beyond serving as a utilitarian delivery tool, it symbolized the postal courier's sacred professional duty: delivering promises without fail, rain or shine.
* **The Meiji 34 Cast-Iron Cylindrical Postbox**:
  The antique mailbox standing before the Overdue Post Office is patterned after the iconic cylindrical cast-iron pillar boxes introduced during Taiwan's Japanese colonial period. Cast in a single pour from high-purity raw pig iron with a rain-diverting conical cap, its massive, weighted base was crafted to withstand the harsh coastal typhoons and corrosive sea salt mists of mountain towns, enduring across a century of wind and rain.
