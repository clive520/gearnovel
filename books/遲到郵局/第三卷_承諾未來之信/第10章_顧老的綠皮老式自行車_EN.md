# Volume III: Letters of Promise to the Future

## Chapter 10: Old Gu's Green Vintage Bicycle

### Evening Sorting Desk & The Depths of the Cast-Iron Safe

After winter set in across the mountain town, dusk always descended with startling haste. Shortly past five in the afternoon, thick, damp, cold greenish-gray mist surged down from the steep mountain passes on either side like a heavy sheet of cold rice paper, sealing the aged wooden window lattices of The Overdue Post Office in absolute isolation.

Inside, the forty-watt tungsten bulb suspended from the ceiling swayed gently in the cross-breeze, bathing the long cypress sorting desk in an amber patina of aged warmth. Lin Ziyang sat before the sorting desk, holding a fine chamois brush and meticulously dusting away the specks of grime from a stack of postcards from the 1970s that had just been retrieved from the hollow of the ancient camphor tree mailbox.

Months of tempering as a messenger had washed away the initial restlessness and disorientation in Lin Ziyang's brow. The pads of his fingers had developed calluses from years of handling rough cotton paper and ink postmarks; his gaze was no longer tight and guarded as when he first arrived in the mountain town, but was imbued with a quiet serenity and tenderness born of bearing witness to countless joys and sorrows.

At the other end of the sorting desk, Old Gu stood with his back to him before the heavy, antique cast-iron safe in the corner. That safe boasted a history of nearly a century; its dark green heavy steel plates were covered with mottled rust and scratches, and the dual-dial pure brass combination lock had long since lost its original sharp edges from wear, resembling a silent, impregnable fortress of time.

Normally, what was stored inside the safe were special-grade archives marked by unique space-time coordinates or intended for recipients who were exceptionally difficult to find. But tonight, Lin Ziyang noticed that Old Gu's movements were unusually sluggish. The old man's spine was hunched lower than usual, his left hand pressed tightly against his chest where his knuckles turned slightly pale from strain, and an extraordinarily heavy, irregular wheezing faintly escaped his ribcage.

"Old Gu, is your chest acting up again? Let me pour you some warm water and bring the sublingual tablets prescribed by the doctor." Lin Ziyang immediately set down the brush, rising hastily to support him.

"Stay seated... don't fret, Ziyang." Old Gu waved his hand without turning around, his voice low and raspy, like gravel rolling across a desiccated riverbed in the night wind. "An old complaint... this heart of mine has beat for over eighty years, like worn-out clockwork whose gear teeth have flattened down, just looking for any chance to catch its breath."

The old man took a slow, deep breath, grasped the heavy brass handle with both hands, and amid a dull, dry screech of cast-iron friction—clack, clack—the heavy door, fully five inches thick, was finally pulled wide open.

The interior of the safe was divided into several tiers by horizontal partitions, each level neatly stacked with archival boxes sealed in kraft paper. However, Old Gu did not look toward any of those boxes. Instead, he bent low, reaching his hands toward a hidden compartment at the very bottom that almost brushed against the freezing floor tiles.

Accompanied by the faint click of a spring-loaded wooden panel giving way, Old Gu retrieved two items from the deepest recess of the secret compartment.

Lin Ziyang instinctively held his breath. The first item was a stack of envelopes bound tightly with coarse black cotton twine, their corners severely frayed and yellowed; the second was a small parcel wrapped tightly in aged coarse cloth thoroughly steeped in tung oil.

Cradling these two items, Old Gu turned and paced slowly back toward the sorting desk. His gait appeared far more halting and heavy than on ordinary days, and with each step he took, the aged cypress floorboards groaned in protest.

"Ziyang, during the days you've spent here at The Overdue Post Office, you have delivered the pig trotter longevity noodles brimming with filial longing, the red sweater that mended a fractured sisterly bond, the silent balance wheel, the umbrella of forgiveness, and the old soldier's train ticket." Old Gu gently set the thick stack of letters and the oilcloth bundle flat in the center of the sorting desk, his gaze piercing through the hazy tungsten glow to rest upon the young apprentice. "You have witnessed so much of other people's regret and redemption. But have you ever wondered where this post office's own letters are destined to go?"

---

### The Snowy Night of Huaihai, Sixty Returned Letters, and the Tiger-Head Bootie

Lin Ziyang was taken aback, his eyes falling upon the stack of envelopes bound with black cotton twine. The envelopes were made of the cheapest rough straw paper, their surfaces long since dotted with dark brown oxidation spots. On them, written in regular brush calligraphy, was the identical recipient name and address: "To Nianzhuangwei, South of Xuzhou City; Wife, Xiuying, to Receive; Son, Nianping, Attached."

On the upper right corner of each envelope was stamped the red postmark of various postal bureaus, yet without exception, stamped right across the center of each envelope was a glaring black oblong wooden mark: "Recipient Unknown, Returned"; "Disrupted by War, Undeliverable"; "Original Address Destroyed, Returned to Sender."

Lin Ziyang's eyes swept across the postmarked years and locations one by one: 1948 Xuzhou, 1949 Guangzhou, 1950 Kinmen, 1953 Keelung Harbor, 1959 Da'an, Taipei... Exactly sixty letters, spanning thousands of miles across mountain passes engulfed in warfare and several decades of turbulent parting.

"Sixty letters." Old Gu's gaunt, withered hand caressed the rough stack of envelopes, his fingertips trembling slightly. "From the howling blizzards of the Battle of Huaihai, across the Yangtze, through the retreat to Taiwan, all the way to when I donned this green mail carrier's uniform in this mountain town. I wrote for sixty full years, until thirty years after my discharge when family visits across the strait were permitted, and I returned in person to that village which had long turned to scorched rubble and ash..."

The old man gently unwrapped the tung-oil coarse cloth of the second object. As the layers of rough cloth scented with aged oil were peeled away, a tiny, delicate child's bootie was revealed.

It was a handcrafted "tiger-head bootie" fashioned from thick crimson woolen broadcloth. The little tiger embroidered on the toe with black and yellow silk threads was vivid and lifelike, yet the silk threads forming the tiger's eyes were severely frayed and worn away, and the upper was stained with indelible dark brown bloodstains and hardened black mud. The size of the shoe clearly fitted only a toddler of three or four years.

Lin Ziyang's chest tightened violently, his eyes instantly brimming with red heat: "Old Gu... is this..."

"This was what I retrieved from the snowy trench on the night of the breakout at Nianzhuang in the winter of 1948." Old Gu's gaze drifted into a bottomless expanse of memory, his tone heartbreakingly calm, as if recounting a tale from an entirely different lifetime. "The snow that night was heavier and colder than several decades of mountain town cold snaps combined. The sky was filled with tracer rounds and the whistle of mortar shells; the entire earth was burning and searing hot."

"Xiuying was searching for me in the torrent of refugees with Nianping in her arms. But by the time I retreated from the position with my unit, all I found beneath a collapsed low mud wall shattered by artillery fire was Xiuying, frozen stiff as stone." Old Gu closed his eyes, and a solitary turbid tear slipped silently down the deep crevices of his crow's feet. "The swaddling clothes clutched tightly in her bosom were already empty. Only this tiger-head bootie lay in the snowy sludge, still carrying the warmth of Nianping's ankle and the blood coughed from Xiuying's lips."

"Nianping was barely four years old then; he couldn't even call me 'Dad' with complete fluency." The old man extended a finger, caressing that battered, icy cotton bootie with infinite tenderness. "That night, I searched like a madman through heaps of fallen soldiers and fleeing refugees, screaming until my throat tore open and I coughed mouthfuls of bloody froth, yet the only reply was the biting north wind and the unending barrage of gunfire."

"Later, the troops retreated all the way south and boarded the naval transport ship bound for Taiwan. On the pitching deck crossing the Black Ditch, everyone was weeping, but I didn't shed a single tear. I stitched this tiger-head bootie into my undershirt right against my heart, and I told myself that Nianping must still be alive. He might have been scooped up by some kind soul, and he must be waiting somewhere in this vast mortal world for his father to find him."

Looking at the sixty returned letters that had never reached their destination, Old Gu gave a tragic smile: "Sixty-eight years. These sixty letters were sent to our ancestral home in Xuzhou, to various provincial fellow-townsman associations, and to the missing persons bulletin board at the Veterans Affairs Commission. Every time I mailed one, I prayed that a living hand would take it and call me Dad; but what came back every single time was only this icy, piercing stamp: 'Recipient Unknown'."

Lin Ziyang felt as if his throat were choked with coarse sand, and tears finally spilled past his eyelashes. He finally understood why Old Gu stood watch over this Overdue Post Office overflowing with regrets, why the old man held every forgotten letter as sacred as life itself, and why he was always able to offer boundless tolerance and warmth to others in the deepest pit of despair.

Because the old man himself was the one who bore the heaviest and most agonizingly protracted "overdue arrival" in this mortal world.

---

### Fifty Years of Ruts on the Green Vintage Iron Steed

"Ziyang, come walk with me outside." Old Gu gently rewrapped the tiger-head bootie in the tung-oil cloth, placing it with dignity beside the stack of returned letters in a corner of the sorting desk. "Tonight is the Winter Solstice. The children out there need to change into warmer clothes."

The old man turned and walked beneath the rain shelter in the post office's rear courtyard, wheeling out the steed that had accompanied him for half a century.

It was a vintage "Yongjiu" (Forever) bicycle crafted from heavy raw iron, finished in dark olive green. Across the center of the frame ran an exceptionally sturdy double crossbar. The green enamel on the frame had long since peeled away from decades of wind, rain, and scorching sun, exposing countless dark red patches of rust-inhibiting primer and rough weld spots from repeated repairs.

The handlebars were wrapped tightly in layers of black electrical tape, and welded beside the left grip was a palm-sized chrome-plated cast-iron bell. Hanging from either side of the rear rack was a pair of large green mailbags handcrafted from thick, rigid cowhide, their leather worn smooth and glossy by the years, radiating a distinctive scent of leather oil blended with aged machine grease. At the front wheel hub was mounted a compact bottle dynamo connected by wire to the large headlight with a convex lens.

This vintage iron steed was as heavy as a mobile anvil, weighing fully over twenty kilograms even when completely empty. An ordinary young man would pant simply pushing it up an incline, yet Old Gu, with his hands lined with veins and calluses, steadied the handlebars with consummate familiarity.

Lin Ziyang hurried forward, taking the old man's overcoat and flashlight, only to see Old Gu already bent over, laboriously fastening three large, bulging parcels wrapped tightly in thick waterproof bags onto the cowhide mailbags on the rear rack with hemp twine.

"Old Gu, these parcels are..." Lin Ziyang reached out to help pull the hemp rope taut, his fingers meeting a dense, soft resilience.

"Down jackets, and thirty pairs of extra-thick, non-slip warm winter shoes for children." Old Gu said calmly as he tied a secure knot. "The cold snap coming down the mountains these past few days has been fierce. The old red-brick dormitory at the orphanage faces away from the sun, and the window gaps leak drafty wind terribly. The kids have chilblains on their hands and feet; they wake up crying from the cold at night."

"The orphanage?" Lin Ziyang started slightly. "You mean the Hope Orphanage on the hillside of Shuinandong?"

"Let's go. We'll push the bicycle and walk up slowly." Old Gu patted the firm leather saddle. He did not mount the bicycle, but instead pushed the heavy iron steed, stepping steadily past the wooden gate of the rear courtyard.

The night was like ink, and the mountain town on the Winter Solstice was gripped by piercing cold winds. Frigid needles of drizzle mixed with mountain vapor whipped against their faces, washing the blue flagstone paths until they glistened as slick as mirrors.

Old Gu pushed the handlebars with both hands, his pace somewhat slow and weighed down, yet every step he planted on the stone stairs and slopes was resolute and grounded. As the wheels rolled, the roller of the bottle dynamo pressed against the tire's sidewall with a soft, humming whir, and the amber beam from the front searchlight pierced through the thick night mist, illuminating the rugged and slippery mountain path beneath their feet.

"Ding-ling—"

As they navigated a steep slope around a hairpin turn, Old Gu's thumb gently flicked the cast-iron paddle on the handlebar. A crisp, melodious, and deeply penetrating chime of metal rang out, rippling across the silent, icy valley and stone walls like a morning bell shattering the loneliness of the long night.

"This bicycle has accompanied me on my routes for fifty years." Old Gu panted as he pushed against the incline, speaking to Lin Ziyang beside him. "Back in the 1950s when I was discharged and passed the postal exam, there were no proper paved asphalt roads in the mountain town—only mud, gravel, and stone steps hand-chiseled by the old miners. In those days, I carried sixty kilograms of letters and parcels every day. Wherever I couldn't ride, I had to heave this iron beast onto my shoulder and climb step by step."

"This bike has gone through seven inner tubes, four sets of chains, and worn out three pairs of pedals, but its skeletal frame has never broken." The old man gazed at the heavy fog parting before the headlight, a gentle smile gracing the corners of his mouth. "Back then the mountain folk were poor, and many couldn't read. When I delivered letters, I also had to read aloud letters of safety from grandsons serving in the South Seas to their grandmothers, and fill out money order slips for miners' wives to send to their parents back home. Whenever this bell chimed, children from across the entire hollow would run out barefoot to greet me, shouting, 'The Green Uncle is here! The Green Uncle is here!'..."

Lin Ziyang walked quietly beside the old man, discreetly bracing his own shoulder against the rear cargo rack to help shoulder the uphill resistance of the heavy parcels. Listening to the humming dynamo and the crisp echo of the bell, he seemed to glimpse the young mail carrier of fifty years ago—tall, upright, and vigorous in his green uniform—pouring his youth and devotion inch by inch into this rain-drenched earth.

---

### Winter Night Vigil Outside the Mountain Town Hope Orphanage

More than half an hour later, the elder and the youth, pushing the bicycle, finally arrived before an old compound situated midway up the mountain in Shuinandong.

Suspended above the iron gate was a weathered white wooden sign with mottled lettering: "Mountain Town Hope Orphanage Foundation." Inside the courtyard stood a two-story red-brick building, from whose vintage wooden windows seeped faint, dim nightlights, resembling guttering candles in the freezing night drizzle.

Old Gu did not ring the electric bell by the iron gate, nor did he call out. He merely leaned the green bicycle against a sheltered stone eave beside the gate with practiced ease, then gently untied the three large bundles of heavy clothing from the rear rack.

"Old Gu, shouldn't we knock and have the director come out to sign for them?" Lin Ziyang asked in a low whisper, his warm breath turning to white vapor in the frigid air.

"Don't wake them. The director cares for over thirty orphans during the day while fretting over next month's food and provisions; she is so exhausted her back can hardly straighten, and it is rare for her to get a peaceful night's rest." Old Gu lowered his voice, drawing a thick kraft paper envelope from inside his coat. On the envelope was written in neat brush strokes: "Year-End Winter Solstice Educational and Meal Fund, Respectfully Presented by an Old Mountain Town Mail Carrier."

The old man placed the envelope atop the three bundles of down jackets and children's shoes, then settled the neatly arranged provisions securely onto a long wooden bench directly beneath the rain awning inside the iron gate. The ground there was elevated, out of reach of rainwater, ensuring that when the director opened the door at dawn to light the cooking fire, it would be the first thing she saw.

Having done all this, Old Gu did not leave immediately. Grasping the icy, damp iron bars with both hands, he looked through the gaps between the rails, gazing quietly toward the row of closed dormitory windows on the second floor.

Inside, all was quiet, with only the faint, even breathing of sleeping children audible, punctuated by an occasional sleepy murmur in their dreams.

A mountain wind swept through, rustling the leaves of the ancient flame tree in the courtyard. In that instant, Old Gu's gaze became infinitely tender and yearning. His lips trembled faintly, and facing that deep, warm darkness, he whispered in a breath audible only to himself:

"Nianping... do you see? Tonight the children all have new clothes to wear, and thick shoes to wear..."

"Their feet will be warm, and they won't wake up crying from the cold in the night anymore..."

Standing just a step behind the old man, Lin Ziyang could no longer hold back his tears, which streamed silently down his cheeks.

He finally understood, to the absolute depth of his being, the soul of this octogenarian.

The old man had spent his entire life searching for the four-year-old child lost amid howling snow and war; for sixty-eight years, he had dispatched sixty letters that vanished like pebbles into the sea, tramping across thousands of rivers and mountains, yet never once did an answering voice come.

Yet, the old man had not been consumed by bottomless despair or resentment. He had transformed that unfathomable longing, agony, and debt toward his own flesh and blood into a selfless love spanning half a century—a silent, nourishing rain poured quietly upon every child in this mountain town who lacked parental shelter and shivered in the cold.

The old man's way of seeking Nianping was not to drown in the mire of self-pity, but to ensure that every suffering, shivering child in the world like Nianping could receive the warmth and guardianship of a father.

"Let's go, Ziyang." After a long while, Old Gu withdrew his gaze and turned back to smile gently at Lin Ziyang. That smile looked so serene and untainted under the pale glow of the streetlamp. "The parcels are delivered. The children can have sweet dreams tonight."

---

### The Redemption of Altruism and the Inheritance of the Courier's Heartbeat

By the time they returned to The Overdue Post Office, the night had deepened completely.

The charcoal in the hearth was crackling briskly, driving the damp chill out the door degree by degree. Upon the long cypress sorting desk, that stack of sixty yellowed returned letters and the battered crimson woolen tiger-head bootie still rested quietly beneath the dim tungsten glow.

Old Gu sat upon a bamboo chair, wearily warming his hands near the brazier. His complexion appeared somewhat ashen and his breathing slightly shallow, yet the light shining in his eyes was brighter and clearer than ever before.

"Ziyang, come sit here." Old Gu patted another wooden stool beside him.

Lin Ziyang sat as bid, his hands clasping the old man's withered, cool palms tightly. "Old Gu, all these decades, did you secretly deliver things to the orphanage like this every month after receiving your stipend?"

"It wasn't anything done in secret; it was just an old postman taking along some en-route parcels for the children." Old Gu smiled faintly, his gaze resting tenderly on the returned letters atop the desk. "When I was young, my heart was filled with bitterness and fury. I cursed that damned war, cursed that lethal blizzard, cursed why Heaven stole Xiuying and made me lose my only son... For a long time, I felt that my living was a cruel joke, that I was nothing more than a walking corpse lingering on in this world."

The old man paused, pointing to his chest: "Back then, in here, it felt every day as if countless rusty, blunt blades were carving and gouging away. Until one day, riding that old green bicycle in a raging storm, I delivered a notice of wartime death and a small bereavement pension into the hands of a widowed mother. That elderly mother wept until she fainted; upon waking, she shakily ladled a piping bowl of sweet potato soup into my hands and said: 'Young postman, the rain is heavy and the road is slick; drink something hot before you go, don't catch cold...'"

"In that instant, I suddenly understood." Old Gu locked eyes with Lin Ziyang, his voice turning solemn and magnificent, like the whisper of mountain ranges. "In this life, if a person's eyes remain fixed solely on his own wound, that single inch of wounded flesh becomes the collapse of heaven and earth, and you will never escape the prison of agony your entire life; but if you raise your eyes to behold the suffering of the multitudes in this mortal realm, to hold up an umbrella for those who hurt more, freeze harder, and despair deeper than you, your heart will be stretched wide open by heaven and earth."

"Child, altruism has never been some unreachable monument of morality, nor is it some grandiose, hollow self-sacrifice." Old Gu squeezed Lin Ziyang's hands, a faint yet extraordinarily resolute strength transmitting from his palms. "Altruism is the only and most thorough self-redemption that we broken, fractured beings can ever find for ourselves in this cold mortal world."

"When your heart holds the whole universe and countless people who need warmth, your own pain becomes as tiny as a speck of dust."

Listening to the old man's words, Lin Ziyang felt his blood surging and boiling in the depths of his chest. He recalled how he had initially fled to this mountain town because of business failure and debt, drowning in resentment and self-pity, feeling that the whole world had wronged him; yet compared to this old man who had endured wartime devastation and the loss of his dearest family, yet guarded this mountain town for half a century, how petty and shallow his own setbacks and disillusionments truly were!

Just then, Old Gu's brow suddenly furrowed fiercely, his body lurching forward as his right fingers violently gripped the green woolen uniform over his chest!

"Old Gu!" Lin Ziyang turned pale with fright, instantly leaping up to support the old man.

"I'm fine... don't panic..." Densely beaded cold sweat broke out across Old Gu's forehead, the color draining from his face in a flash, yet he gritted his teeth, fighting fiercely against the churning agony deep in his chest cavity, squeezing out broken words through his clenched teeth: "Sublingual... sublingual in the third drawer..."

Lin Ziyang bolted like an arrow to the medicine cabinet, yanked open the drawer to retrieve the small vial, poured out a white emergency pill, and swiftly slipped it under Old Gu's tongue, followed by warm water that he carefully coaxed down the old man's throat.

Fully five minutes passed before Old Gu's labored, irregular gasping slowly subsided. Leaning back against the bamboo chair, his taut body gradually unwound.

"Old Gu, tomorrow morning I must accompany you to the large hospital in Keelung for a thorough checkup!" Lin Ziyang's eyes were bloodshot, his tone carrying a firmness close to pleading. "Your heart cannot be delayed any longer; these attacks are becoming more and more frequent!"

Old Gu slowly opened his eyes, looking at Lin Ziyang whose face was flushed with frantic concern, his gaze brimming with love and solace. The old man did not answer whether he would go to the hospital; he merely lifted his hand with effort and gently patted Lin Ziyang's shoulder.

"Silly child... the doctor told me long ago that this heart of mine has dilated like a broken bellows, with most of the vessels blocked; it's on its last legs." The old man's voice was very soft, yet carried an open detachment that had seen through life and death. "To have met you at The Overdue Post Office, to see you carry the mail messenger's pack with such steadiness and honor—Heaven has already treated me far too generously."

The old man turned his head, gazing out the door toward the dark olive-green vintage bicycle resting quietly in the night. Though the dynamo lamp was extinguished, the vintage cast-iron bell on the handlebar still gleamed with a steadfast, warm metallic sheen under the lingering glow of the indoor hearth.

"Ziyang, remember the chime of this bike tonight." Old Gu said softly, his gaze distant and peaceful. "This postal route is very, very long. The night will always darken, and the rain will always fall, but as long as there is still a cold heart waiting in this world, the mail messenger's wheels can never stop."

That night, Lin Ziyang sat before the sorting desk, keeping vigil beside the sleeping elder until dawn. He gently gathered the thick stack of returned letters and the tiger-head bootie that carried sixty-eight years of blood, tears, and grand love, carefully rewrapping them. In the warm embers of the hearth, the young messenger straightened his spine. He knew that a sacred calling—one surpassing blood ties and transcending the passage of years—had at this very moment, like an unceasing river, flowed silently and weightily into the marrow of his soul.
