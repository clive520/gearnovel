# Volume III: Letters of Commitment to the Future
## Chapter 09: The Last Train Ticket in the Old Camphor Hollow

#### 1

As the seasons turned to winter, the continuous cold rains over the mountain town began to mingle with the northeast monsoon howling from the Pacific, bone-chilling maritime mists wrapping the entire Jinguashi mountain range layer upon layer like a heavy, sodden quilt.

At six in the morning, a delicate glaze of white frost crystallized upon the courtyard flagstones of the Overdue Post Office. A thin layer of ice skimmed the water vat beside the courtyard, the air filled with the desolate chill of withering winter foliage.

Passing through the back hall, Lin Ziyang was startled to see that Old Gu was already impeccably dressed—the elder had donned a washed-pale, starch-stiff dark green vintage postman's woolen greatcoat, around his waist cinched a dark-brown service belt of raw cowhide, darkened by years of sweat and scarred by abrasions along its edges. It was the formal postal uniform worn only on statutory ceremonial occasions of state postal service.

In his hands, the elder held a gleaming small brass spade, a weighty steel machete, and a windproof kerosene hurricane lantern shielded by a wire cage.

"Ziyang, there is no need to bring the sorting satchel today." Old Gu walked out beneath the eaves, his breath billowing in white clouds, his tone measured and profoundly solemn. "Go to the storeroom and put on high-top anti-slip rain boots, and bring thick hemp gloves. Follow me today into the old Jinguashi mining district deep in the back mountains."

Feeling a surge of solemn awe, Lin Ziyang hastily changed into protective gear, took the kerosene hurricane lantern from the elder's hand, and followed closely behind him.

One before the other, the two stepped out through the post office's rear portal, setting foot upon an ancient railway roadbed completely swallowed by rampant wilderness and wild silvergrass.

It was a narrow-gauge mineral railway abandoned in the wilderness for half a century. Its ties were long rotted, blackened, and overgrown with damp wild mushrooms; two rusted black rails intermittently surfaced through loose shale, mud, and thorny brambles, resembling a sleeping, rusted python buried in the depths of the earth, its scales flaking away.

#### 2

The mountain path was exceptionally steep; with every step climbed upward, the ferocious maritime gale whistled sharply against their ears.

After walking for nearly an hour, the two came to a halt beside a wind-sheltered precipice facing the sea halfway up the mountain. There stood an ancient camphor tree of colossal girth, so immense it would take four adult men with linked arms to encircle its trunk.

It was an ancient colossus that had weathered the catastrophes of heaven and earth. Decades ago, a rare thunderbolt had blasted away the half of its crown facing the sea, leaving charred, charcoal-black boughs thrusting into the heavens like a frozen cry of defiance in the storm; yet the other half, sheltered from the sea winds, was tenaciously, miraculously alive, its massive branches verdant and proud, its dark-green camphor leaves whispering in the freezing wind, releasing an intoxicatingly clean botanical fragrance of natural camphor.

Old Gu walked up to a massive burr on the shaded flank of the ancient camphor.

The gnarled bark was twisted and raised high, resembling the weathered countenance of a patriarch who had survived ten thousand storms. Right at the center of the burr lay a flattened hollow, its edges healed over by time and callous rings.

Old Gu sank slowly onto his haunches, raised the brass spade, and meticulously scraped away the dry, blackened moss and caked loam sealing the exterior of the hollow.

Approximately an arm's depth within the cavity, a metallic gleam surfaced. Reaching into the narrow burr with effort, Old Gu extracted a cylindrical tinplate canister, about twenty centimeters in length and as thick as a teacup.

Around the canister and across its seams, thick layers of natural yellow beeswax had been applied by someone long ago, sealing it airtight. Over fifty-seven years, the beeswax had hardened into a rock-like outer shell, insulating the canister completely from external dampness.

Brushing away the loam with his faded sleeve, Old Gu solemnly placed the canister into Lin Ziyang's hands: "Ziyang, scrape away the wax with your pocketknife and open it."

#### 3

Taking that chilled, weighty canister, Lin Ziyang retrieved his pocketknife, held his breath, sliced through the hardened beeswax, and unscrewed the rusted metal lid.

Inside the canister, there was no moisture; it remained as dry as the day it was sealed.

What was extracted was an object wrapped tightly in several layers of tung-oil-soaked kraft paper. As Lin Ziyang peeled back the brittle layers of oiled paper, several artifacts exuding a distinct scent of bygone decades emerged beneath the amber light of the hurricane lantern.

At the very top lay an impeccably preserved stiff-card train ticket.

It was a reserved-seat express ticket issued by the Taiwan Railway Administration on November 12, the fifty-eighth year of the Republic, from "Jinguashi Station" to "Hualien Station." The seat was printed in black lead type as "Car 3, Seat 14," stamped across the middle with a striking vermilion "Express" seal, its edge bearing the deep diamond-shaped notch punched by the conductor's clippers so long ago.

Resting right beneath the train ticket was a dark golden metal ring.

It was no commercial gold jewelry, but something hand-carved and polished from a raw nugget of high-purity auriferous copper ore using grinding wheels and rasps. The band was thick and unrefined; inside, a small, five-petaled wild lily had been crookedly, clumsily, yet devoutly carved with an ultra-fine steel chisel.

And at the bottom of the oiled wrapping was a long strip of coarse linen cloth.

It was a strip torn from the sweatband inside a miner's hardhat. Across its face, written in dried, dark-red blood mixed with fine black coal dust, was a brief, urgent message penned with forceful strokes:

"'Wait for me to return and marry you; the moment my shift ends, we depart. —Jinshui's final words.'"

#### 4

"Fifty-seven years," Old Gu's voice was low and gravelly, like water seeping through mountain crevices, carrying a weight that stirred the soul.

"At three o'clock in the afternoon on November 12, the fifty-eighth year of the Republic, Jinguashi Pit No. 7 suffered the most catastrophic gas explosion and water inrush since the founding of the mines; hundreds of millions of tons of granite and mud collapsed in an instant, pulverizing the main vertical shaft three hundred meters beneath the earth. Chen Jinshui was twenty-four that year—a model worker for three consecutive years and the youngest tunneling crew leader in the entire mountain."

"At noon on the day of the disaster, he snuck out of the mine mouth during lunch break. Unable to afford a readymade gold ring at the town's jewellers, he spent his night shifts grinding this ring from high-grade raw ore he had saved; then he queued for two hours at the station to buy this express ticket to Hualien."

The elder's gaze swept toward the ruins of the mountain town shrouded in sea mist below: "He promised that girl at the foot of the mountain that as soon as his afternoon shift concluded and he washed the coal dust from his skin, he would board the train with her to Hualien to watch the ocean and hold a splendid wedding. He concealed the ring, the ticket, and his vow within the old camphor hollow—the 'Tree Hollow Post Office' they had agreed upon in their youth to pass secret notes."

"Yet that afternoon, he never walked out of that pitch-black mine mouth again."

Lin Ziyang clasped that frigid, heavy ore ring tightly, his fingertips brushing against the uneven wild lily inside, feeling an agonizing constriction in his chest: "That girl... what became of her afterward?"

"She waited for an entire lifetime upon the platform of that abandoned railway station," Old Gu sighed heavily. "Her name is Shen Ayun; she turned eighty this very year. Go on, Ziyang. Go to the old platform, and place this train ticket and this ring personally into her hands."

#### 5

Tucking the tinplate canister securely into his canvas satchel, Lin Ziyang cradled it against his chest, racing down toward the ruins of the old railway station below.

Traversing the abandoned railway switches choked with silvergrass taller than a man, a roofless, desolate platform—with only half-crumbled red-brick walls remaining—emerged beneath the gloomy sky.

The sea wind whistled across the empty, frigid rails, emitting a mournful sound like weeping.

And right at the center of that weathered wooden bench, pitted by time and encrusted with moss, sat an elderly, slender woman with snow-white hair.

She wore an impeccably laundered vintage cloth cheongsam trimmed with navy velvet piping, her silver hair pinned into a dignified, flawless bun with a wooden hairpin. Her hands rested serenely over a floral cloth bag on her lap, her back held ramrod straight, resembling a statue of a classical maiden frozen in the river of time.

Her gaze pierced through layers of swirling sea mist, fixed in tranquil, undivided concentration upon the two rusted rails stretching into the empty mountains.

Whenever the mountain gale whistled through the abandoned tunnel in the distance, a gentle ripple would stir in her eyes, as though fifty-seven years had never passed, as if she were still that eighteen-year-old mountain girl sitting on the platform, waiting with pristine love for her sweetheart to finish his shift.

Fifty-seven whole years.

The gold rush had dissipated, the mining bureau was dismantled, the station had been shuttered thirty years ago, and even the spikes upon the rails were rusted to dust.

Generations of townsfolk had come and gone. Suitors had beaten a path to her threshold, neighbors pleaded with her, and her parents had wept on bended knees before their deaths, begging her to find an honest man to lean on, calling her a madwoman for keeping vigil for a wandering ghost lost beneath the earth.

Yet Shen Ayun had merely smiled with tender grace, saying with serene calm:

"Jinshui is no liar; he told me that as soon as his shift ended, he would take me away. Whatever he promised me, even if heaven falls and the earth dissolves, he will surely come to fetch me."

#### 6

Lin Ziyang lightened his footsteps, stepping across the gravel onto the desolate platform.

"Grandmother Ayun..." Lin Ziyang's voice trembled slightly.

Startled faintly, the elderly woman slowly withdrew her gaze from the horizon and turned her face toward him.

It was an aged countenance furrowed with wrinkles like chrysanthemum petals, yet those eyes resting within sunken sockets were unimaginably clear, clean, and crystalline—free of the murkiness and exhaustion of old age, carrying only a serenity and brilliance refined by half a century of storms.

"Young man, are you waiting for the train as well?" The elder's voice was extraordinarily gentle, tinged with the soft melodic cadence of the mountain town's older generation, like a light breeze plucking the strings of an ancient zither: "Today's express to Hualien seems to be running just a little late. Do not fret; the fog is heavy in the mountains. Jinshui told me that when trains travel slowly through heavy fog, the journey is safe and sound."

Looking into the elder's tranquil eyes, Lin Ziyang's own eyes welled with tears.

Stepping forward, the youth sank slowly onto one knee before her bench, holding up that elongated tinplate canister with both hands.

"Grandmother Ayun, today... the train is not late," Lin Ziyang choked, every word laden with reverence spanning a century. "Mr. Chen Jinshui entrusted us at the Overdue Post Office... to deliver your letter."

The moment the name "Chen Jinshui" rang out, Shen Ayun jolted violently!

The elder's gaze fell upon that yellowed, stiff-card train ticket unfolded atop the canister.

When her eyes took in the five lead-type characters "Jinguashi—Hualien" and the deep-red stamp of "November 12, Year 58," the calm deep within those crystalline eyes that had rested for fifty-seven years suddenly surged with mountainous waves!

#### 7

The elder's hands began to shake uncontrollably.

Extending her withered, slender fingertips, she gently caressed that brittle ticket, feeling the diamond-shaped notch clipped into its edge; immediately following, her fingers touched that dark golden, rustic ore ring.

As her fingertips traced that five-petaled wild lily carved into the band with a chisel, two streams of scalding tears spilled from the corners of her eyes.

Finally, her fingers rested upon that blackened strip of linen cloth stained with blood and coal dust.

Fighting back his tears, Lin Ziyang read aloud the secret words buried beneath the earth in pitch-black darkness for more than half a century:

"'Ayun, darkness has fallen; fallen boulders have blocked every path. The sounds of inrushing water and gas are everywhere; one by one, the crew have ceased breathing.'

"'I can hear the wind blowing from down the mountain; I know... you must still be sitting on that bench upon the platform, waiting for me.'

"'I am sorry. This time, Jinshui may be late—late for a very, very long time...'

"'Do not weep; save your tears for sunny days. I bought the tickets; I hid the ring in the tree hollow. If I cannot make this train in this lifetime, forget me, find an honest man, and live a bustling, full life.'

"'Yet if you still keep a corner in your heart for me, then please believe: until the final beat of Chen Jinshui's heart, his eyes were looking only upon that kerosene lantern on the platform down below.'"

The words drifted upon the cold wind across the empty, desolate platform.

Shen Ayun did not wail aloud, nor did she collapse into hysterics.

She merely gazed in stillness upon the ring in her hand, two streams of scalding tears coursing silently down her deeply furrowed cheeks, splashing onto the yellowed ticket, remoistening the fifty-eighth-year paper fibers.

"Foolish Jinshui... you silly man..."

Extending the withered, slender ring finger of her left hand, with exquisite gentleness and grace, the old woman slipped that chilled, heavy ore ring onto her finger, millimeter by millimeter.

The fit was entirely, impeccably perfect!

#### 8

"Look, I always knew... I always knew you would never lie to me."

Stroking the ring upon her finger, a dazzling, radiant smile blossomed across Shen Ayun's face.

That smile shed eighty years of hardships and weathering, rekindling the tender joy, modesty, and brilliance of an eighteen-year-old maiden freshly fallen in love beneath the amber sunlight!

"Fifty-seven years... the entire world laughed at me for being foolish, for being mad, claiming you had abandoned me long ago." The elder lifted her head, letting the frigid sea breeze brush through her snow-white hair: "Yet in my heart I always knew that my Jinshui was the most honorable, most faithful man under heaven. He promised to marry me; even across ten thousand fathoms of bedrock, even across the divide of life and death, he would surely see this ring placed into my hands."

Steadfast vigil is never a stubborn wager against fate, and loyalty is by no means a futile squandering of years.

It is the soul's supreme hymn to a pristine promise. Countless vows crumble amidst the tide of material desire, and countless sweet whispers dissolve into dust beneath the erosion of years; yet upon this desolate, abandoned platform, a fidelity that bridged more than half a century forged, through eighty years of solitary wait, the most brilliant and immortal monument of human grace.

Shen Ayun folded that fifty-eighth-year express ticket with utmost reverence, gingerly tucking it into the pocket of her blue cheongsam closest to her heart.

Taking Lin Ziyang's hand, the elder's voice was tranquil, tender, and deeply fulfilled:

"Young man, thank you. In these fifty-seven years, I never felt a single day of bitterness. For waiting for someone who loves you with his whole heart and soul is, in itself, a profound and wondrous blessing."

"Today, my train has finally arrived."

Slowly the elder stood up. She did not cast another glance upon those two cold, rusted rails; instead, turning around, she walked with light, serene steps toward the small town below. The breeze fluttered the hem of her navy cheongsam, her silver hair gleaming beneath the pale sun like diamonds of noble splendor.

Standing upon the platform, Lin Ziyang gazed long at the old woman's departing silhouette.

Upon the distant mountain ridge, that ancient camphor tree—half-scorched, half-verdant—stood resolute against the biting wind, its branches whispering like a loyal sentinel guarding the timeless covenants of this mountain land for all eternity.

### 【信差迴響 / Messenger's Echo】
> **本章品德力量：【忠誠與堅守（Loyalty & Endurance）】**
> **Virtue of this Chapter: 【Loyalty & Endurance】**
>
> 諾言易許，堅守難為；世人常因路途遙遠而背棄初衷，因歲月漫長而淡忘誓言。
> Promises are easily spoken, yet fidelity is hard to sustain; men often forsake their original intentions when the journey is long, and allow their vows to fade beneath the erosion of years.
>
> 然而，最崇高的忠誠，能穿透生死的迷霧，在漫長的苦難與孤寂中綻放出永不凋零的花朵。真正的堅守，不是徒勞的執念，而是以一生的純粹與深情，回應另一顆靈魂的生死託付。
> Yet the loftiest loyalty pierces the mists of life and death, blooming into an unfading flower amidst prolonged hardship and solitary vigil. True endurance is not a futile obsession, but responding to another soul's sacred trust with a lifetime of purity and deep devotion.
>
> 當歲月的風沙抹平了一切世俗的功名利祿，唯有堅貞不渝的愛與承諾，能在時間的荒原上刻下永恆的印記，證明生命的崇高與尊嚴。
> When the shifting sands of time erase all worldly riches and hollow acclaim, only steadfast love and unyielding promises can carve an eternal imprint upon the wasteland of time, bearing witness to the nobility and dignity of life.
