# Volume II: Letters of Empathy for Others
## Chapter 05: That Bowl of Cold Pork Trotter Longevity Noodles Beneath the Steamer

#### 1

In the deep autumn of the mountain town, crisp, clear days were never to be found; thick, curdled white mist, mingling with the bone-chilling maritime gale sweeping in from the East China Sea, coiled like countless icy, damp shrouds layer upon layer around the tiered black-tiled rooftops and the slick bluestone stairs.

At five-thirty in the morning, the standing water in the courtyard of the Overdue Post Office gleamed with a leaden, frigid sheen. Lin Ziyang had just kindled the firewood stove in the back room; the air ought to have been permeated with the dry, botanical fragrance of burning pine and vintage archive paper, yet today, a peculiar odor—a faint, anomalous gamey scent of dried, aged soy sauce, caramelized star anise, and rusted sheet iron—faintly overpowered every trace of ink.

Carrying a warm bowl of water of coarse stoneware, Lin Ziyang stepped into the sorting hall to find Old Gu standing before that massive camphor sorting counter.

The old postmaster was not wearing his iconic dark-green woolen postman's greatcoat today, but an unadorned short tunic of gray homespun, his sleeves rolled up high to reveal a pair of forearms mottled with age spots yet still spare and remarkably steady. In his hands, the elder clutched a wad of fine steel wool soaked in rapeseed oil, gently and with agonizing slowness scouring an object that rested upon the desk.

It was a single-handled small aluminum pot, barely the breadth of a rice bowl, one of its twin ear-lugs having long since sheared away.

The pot's rim was battered with dents and gouges, bearing witness to innumerable violent impacts and the relentless scouring of time; its original silvery luster had long been blackened by decades of dense woodsmoke, and in the pits of the base lingered faint traces of congealed, blackened grease from years long past. Beside the aluminum pot lay an antique bamboo picnic basket woven from fine cane strips and coated in peeling vermilion lacquer, alongside a brittle, yellowed leaf torn from an old daily tear-away calendar, its four corners frayed and curled.

"Old Gu, today's parcel... is a cooking pot?" Lin Ziyang set down the water bowl, stepping forward to examine it closely.

Old Gu halted his work, his withered fingers lightly grazing the jagged dents around the rim, his gaze as fathomless as the dark ocean tides heaving off a rain-drenched port deep in the night.

"A letter is the resting place of the human heart. Some carve their sorrows upon bamboo slips; others brush their vows upon rice paper. Yet the heaviest, most agonizing devotions are too weighty for paper to bear; they can only be dished into rice bowls, locked away within iron pots." Old Gu slowly straightened his spine, his eyes settling upon the impenetrable morning fog beyond the window. "This pot has slept in the cool storage cellars of this post office for twenty whole years."

#### 2

"Twenty years?" A faint stir passed through Lin Ziyang's heart.

"To be precise, twenty years ago on New Year's Eve." Old Gu fished a brass magnifying loupe from his breast pocket and handed it to Lin Ziyang. "Examine the underside of this pot."

Taking the magnifying glass beneath the dim morning daylight, Lin Ziyang brought the lens close to the soot-blackened base. There, within the sunken contour of charred aluminum, someone had used the dull tip of a kitchen cleaver to score three deep, crooked characters, stroke by agonizing stroke—'Chen Guoshun'.

Beneath the name, a faint, wavy line had been carved—the rudimentary astrological glyph for the year of the Tiger.

"Chen Guoshun... who was he?"

"Thirty years ago, throughout the docks of Badouzai Harbor and Heping Island in Keelung, no one ever uttered his given name; they all called him 'Mad Dog Xiang,' or simply 'A-Xiang'." Old Gu's voice was low and gravelly, like the opening of a heavy iron door caked in decades of rust. "It was a turbulent era of rampant smuggling and ruthless wharf gangs. A-Xiang's father had been a deep-sea fisherman, swallowed by the sea when the boy was only seven, his bones never recovered. His mother, Sister Chunjiao, scraped together borrowed coins to buy a wooden tricycle cart, setting up a modest noodle stall beside the mudflats of the docks."

"On frigid winter nights when maritime gales howled, fishing boats would dock to unload their catch, and stevedores encrusted in fish scales and frost would pack beneath the canvas tarpaulin, hungering for nothing so much as Sister Chunjiao's bubbling pot of angelica pork trotter longevity noodles, dark with rich soy, its savory perfume penetrating into the very marrow of one's bones. To put A-Xiang through school, Sister Chunjiao would brave the piercing sea squalls before dawn to haul the cheapest offal scraps from the slaughterhouses, soaking her hands in ice water to pluck pig bristles until her ten fingers cracked open with bloody fissures, covered in purplish-black chilblains."

"Yet that boy, reared on sea salt and fish reek, was born with a feral, untamable savagery in his bones. He loathed the pitying, even insolent glances the dockworkers cast toward his mother; he loathed her meek smiles and humble bows before the street thugs just to sell an extra five-dollar bowl of noodles. He believed that books could never save a home like theirs; only fists and underworld brotherhood could command true dignity."

"At sixteen, A-Xiang began watching over docks for smuggling syndicates and running debt enforcement for neighborhood gang bosses. Standing tall and broad-shouldered with a merciless hand, he carved out a fearsome reputation across the Port of Keelung within two short years. He strutted in gaudy floral shirts, a sharpened rebar spike tucked into his waistband, flanked by a pack of disreputable hooligans, never casting so much as a glance toward that steaming wooden food steamer as he paraded arrogantly past his mother's stall."

Lin Ziyang listened in silence, his chest feeling as though stuffed with cotton soaked in ice water. He recalled himself years ago—swaggering through Taipei's financial district, roaring short-sale orders into the telephone. Had he not worn the very same armor of arrogant conceit, ruthlessly tossing into the trash the parcels of produce and handwritten admonitions sent by his elderly mother far to the south?

#### 3

"Then... why did he leave Keelung? And how did this pot find its way here?" Lin Ziyang pressed, unable to restrain himself.

"Because of a fatal misunderstanding, and a slap across the face that severed thirty years of mother-son ties." Old Gu exhaled a long, heavy sigh, picking up that yellowed, fragile calendar leaf from the counter.

"It was the midnight of the twenty-eighth day of the twelfth lunar month in the seventy-third year of the Republic. A deluge poured over the Port of Keelung. A massive gang war erupted within the wharf warehouses—smuggled salt and foreign cigarettes were raided, and the younger brother of a territorial syndicate boss was beaten within an inch of his life. Police mobilized from every corner of the harbor to seal off the docks. In truth, the one who struck the brutal blow and fled with the contraband was A-Xiang's sworn brother, A-Biao, with whom he had grown up since childhood; but A-Biao had a bedridden wife and a nursing infant at home. For the sake of what he held to be sacred 'underworld brotherhood,' A-Xiang shouldered the entirety of the blame, shoving the bloody iron bar into his own jacket."

"Drenched in mud and reeking of blood, A-Xiang fled back to the docks through the howling storm. He intended only to look upon his mother one last time and snatch his identification card from the stall's drawer before running for his life. Yet police sirens screamed, cordoning off the stall, while dozens of neighborhood residents and wharf laborers gathered in the downpour. Right before Sister Chunjiao's eyes, the officers hauled the dripping weapon from beneath A-Xiang's coat."

An agonizing, bone-deep sorrow surfaced in Old Gu's gaze: "Sister Chunjiao had just dished up a bowl of pork trotter noodles intended for the night-shift dockworkers. The moment she beheld that bloody iron bar, this woman, who had endured half a lifetime of suffering and guarded her son's honor more fiercely than her own breath, broke down entirely. Before the eyes of the entire neighborhood, amidst the flashing glare of red cruiser lights, Sister Chunjiao stepped forward trembling and, with every ounce of strength in her frame, struck A-Xiang across the face with a ferocious slap!"

"The crack of that slap was deafening, exploding through the storm like a thunderclap. Pointing her finger at A-Xiang, tears streaming down her face, Sister Chunjiao shrieked in grief that tore the air: 'You shameless beast! I, Lin Chunjiao, slaved like an ox for thirty years, bowing and scraping in the mud—was it all to raise an armed robber and a murderer? Get out! I have no son like you! I'll pretend that when I gave birth to you thirty years ago, I threw you straight into the ocean to drown!'"

Lin Ziyang's breath hitched solid in his throat.

"A-Xiang was only eighteen years old," Old Gu closed his eyes slowly. "A boy's pride is more brittle and sharper than a steel blade. He had taken the fall for his brother, carrying a mountain of grievance with nowhere to voice it. He believed the entire world might spit upon him, but that his own mother would understand his unbending spine. That single slap, delivered in front of the whole street, pulverized the final remnants of his youthful dignity to dust."

"A-Xiang uttered not a single syllable of defense. Wrenching free from the officers' grip, his bloodshot eyes locked onto Sister Chunjiao. Biting through his lip until it bled, he bellowed into the rainy night: 'Fine! Lin Chunjiao, you think I bring you shame? If I walk out of this harbor today and fail to make a proper man of myself out there, if I don't return clad in glory, may lightning strike me dead and leave my corpse without a grave if I ever set foot into your noodle stall again!'"

"With that, like an enraged, wounded predator, he shoved through the crowd, plunged headlong into the pitch-black, freezing sea, swam to an incoming smuggling trawler, and vanished completely into the roaring tempests of the Taiwan Strait."

#### 4

"And... what became of Sister Chunjiao?" Lin Ziyang's fingers trembled faintly.

"The instant A-Xiang leaped into the sea, Sister Chunjiao was consumed by regret," Old Gu's voice groaned like the ancient beams of an aged attic. "Like a madwoman, she dropped her ladle, stumbling toward the slick, moss-covered stone stairs of the pier. The night was too dark, the rain too furious, the stairs too treacherous... Her foot slipped, and she tumbled down more than a dozen stone steps directly into the jagged boulders of the shore. Her right kneecap shattered completely, her blood painting the puddles of mud."

"Three days later, when A-Biao, devoured by his conscience, ran into the hospital ward and threw himself onto his knees, confessing the whole truth and admitting that A-Xiang had taken the fall for him... Sister Chunjiao collapsed upon her bed. Overnight, her raven hair turned entirely white as snow."

Old Gu gently flipped that brittle calendar leaf over.

On its front was printed the yellowed red date of January 1, the seventy-fourth year of the Republic. On its reverse, across the coarse, blank paper, dense lines had been scrawled in blue ballpoint ink, scarred by weeping tears. The ink had dried to a pale brown—the heart-wrenching agony penned twenty years ago by an illiterate old mother, carving each stroke with a quivering right hand:

"'A-Xiang, my boy:'

"'It was your mother who wronged you; it was that slap of mine that broke my son's back. Your mother deserves to die; I did not know you were taking the fall for someone else. I only feared you would stray from the straight path and throw your life away...'

"'Your mother's leg is broken, and I can no longer shoulder the baskets to search for you. The doctors say this leg can never heal; from now on, I can only climb these stone steps on a crutch. But do not fret: I haven't closed down the stall on the pier. I bought the place out; every single night, your mother still keeps the lantern burning there.'

"'It is New Year's Eve; firecrackers are bursting across the streets, and every household is gathered together. Using this small aluminum pot you loved best as a child, your mother stewed the pork trotter noodles you crave most. Longevity noodles stretch long, praying my boy lives a hundred years; pork trotters turn fortune around, washing clean all the injustice and ill luck from your shoulders.'

"'The noodles are kept warm beneath the steamer. The door is unlocked; your mother sits on the low stool by the threshold, waiting for you. A-Xiang, even if you become a beggar out there, even if you haven't a single copper to your name, as long as you push open that door, there will always be a bowl of piping hot noodles waiting for you. Do not fear; you don't need to prove anything or become any grand figure. In your mother's eyes, you living safe and sound is the greatest figure this world could ever ask for...'

"'My son, the weather is cold; come home and eat a warm bite of noodles. Mother... misses you so dearly.'"

Reaching the final line, Lin Ziyang's throat felt as though seared by a branding iron, and the tears brimming in his eyes finally fell silently onto the marble floor.

#### 5

"A-Xiang... never returned after that?" Lin Ziyang's voice was hoarse.

"He never returned," Old Gu sighed heavily, his gaze piercing through the mist outside. "A man's vanity and pride are sometimes the deadliest poison in this mortal realm. He had sworn a solemn oath: before he 'made a proper man of himself,' he would never turn back. Yet he never comprehended that parents never care whether you wear silk brocades or tattered rags; what they yearn for is merely the gentle voice that calls out when you turn your head toward home."

"Beneath that drafty, damp pier awning, Sister Chunjiao kept vigil for ten whole years. Every single New Year's Eve, she would fill this small aluminum pot with steaming pork trotter noodles, placing it carefully on the lowest rack of the steamer. Beside a dim kerosene lamp, she would sit from twilight till dawn, waiting for a wooden door that was never once pushed open. Until one freezing winter night ten years ago, Sister Chunjiao suffered a fatal heart attack, collapsing over the cutting board beside the steamer—her hand still clutching this calendar page tight."

"In accordance with the elder's dying wishes, the neighbors delivered this small aluminum pot with its cold noodles and this letter to the Overdue Post Office. Before she passed, Sister Chunjiao took her neighbor's hand, whispering: 'If my A-Xiang ever returns one day... please place this bowl of noodles into his hands, and tell him... his mother never blamed him.'"

Old Gu lifted that weighty, red-lacquered bamboo basket and that oil-stained calendar letter, placing them squarely into Lin Ziyang's arms.

"Lin Ziyang." The old postmaster's eyes were as immovable as mountains. "A-Xiang is fifty-eight years old this year. He never returned in triumph; he does not even have a hearth of his own. For thirty years, to flee his guilt, to flee that Port of Keelung where he swore his toxic oath, he surrendered his life to a twenty-five-ton long-haul semi-truck, hurtling madly day and night across Taiwan's highways."

"He eats in his cab, sleeps in his cab. He believes that as long as the truck's engine never dies, as long as its wheels keep turning, he can leave that bowl of shaming noodles and his mother's scalding slap far behind in the darkness of the road."

Old Gu raised his hand, pointing south toward the murky horizon where sea and sky blurred: "Go to Su'ao Port in Yilan, to the heavy truck parking depot behind Nantian Temple. Find that vintage dark-green Scania twenty-five-ton rig bearing the plate **【KG-588】**. Deliver this pot of cold noodles, thirty years overdue, directly into the hands of that son who has spent a lifetime running."

#### 6

Ten forty-five in the morning.

The heavy truck parking depot behind Nantian Temple at Su'ao Port, Yilan.

The ocean air was thick with the reek of heavy diesel exhaust, the acidic tang of oxidized rust, and the sticky salt fog drifting in from the Pacific. Dozens of steel behemoths—three to four meters tall and over a dozen meters long—lay crouched across the pitted gravel mudflats, their massive tires encrusted with dried mud and asphalt flakes.

Lin Ziyang wheeled the vintage green bicycle forward, the red-lacquered bamboo basket bound securely to both sides of the rear carrier with coarse hemp rope, picking his way step by step through the narrow crevices between the towering rigs.

Tucked into a sea-bluffed corner corroded by salt spray and gales, he finally beheld that vintage dark-green Scania twenty-five-ton articulated rig.

The cab was battered and scarred; the lower right corner of the windshield was plastered with faded toll booth stickers from across Taiwan, the paint flaking in wide scabs, while upon the front grille hung a scratched iron license plate—**【KG-588】**.

The doors were locked tight.

Perched high above, two grease-blackened towels hung behind the side windows, the blackout curtains drawn shut, resembling an airtight steel coffin on wheels.

Propping up the bicycle, Lin Ziyang untied the red-lacquered basket from the rear rack. The youth lifted his head, gazing up at that towering, chilly cab, drawing a deep breath of cold maritime air.

Raising his right hand, he brought his knuckles down hard against the thick alloy door:

*Thud! Thud! Thud!*

The dull clang of metal tore sharply across the empty, silent corner of the depot.

A full half-minute passed before irritable, heavy rustling sounds and the creak of cot springs drifted from within the cab. Immediately following came a gravelly, ferocious roar:

"What the hell are you banging for! Trying to wake the dead? I just clocked off night shift—get lost!"

Lin Ziyang did not budge. Raising his hand again, he struck the metal panel with even greater force:

*Thud! Thud! Thud!*

"Mr. Chen Guoshun, the Overdue Post Office has a letter for you!"

#### 7

A sudden, uncanny silence fell within the cab.

After ten seconds, a loud metallic *clack* echoed as the deadbolt was violently thrown open, and the heavy door swung wide, releasing a suffocating blast of stale cigarette tar, fermented betel nut juice, and the rancid sweat of a space unventilated for weeks!

A burly man nearly sixty years of age, his frame massive yet weathered by hard decades, appeared upon the iron boarding steps.

His hair was disheveled and streaked with gray, his face etched with furrows deep as knife scars, his bloodshot, triangular eyes gleaming with the predatory, weary ferocity of an old animal. He wore a threadbare, yellowed gray undershirt; in his right hand, coated in calluses and black motor grease, he gripped a half-meter-long, heavy alloy lug wrench.

"You little punk, what did you just call me?" The man glared down at Lin Ziyang, betel nut shreds churning between his lips with every ragged breath, his stare lethal as a blade. "Who gave you permission to speak that name? Who sent you here? Beat it!"

Standing in the mud, Lin Ziyang looked up at this "Mad Dog Xiang," a man pulverized by life and time. Deep within the elder's eyes, he recognized something painfully familiar—absolute self-loathing, absolute defense, and a terror to the marrow of the bone concealed beneath a savage exterior.

That look was identical to the one he had worn three years ago, cornered by creditors in a Taipei alleyway in the downpour, clutching a shard of broken glass with trembling hands.

"Mr. Chen, no one sent me," Lin Ziyang held up the bamboo basket with both hands, his voice calm and anchored. "I was entrusted by the late Ms. Lin Chunjiao of Badouzai Pier in Keelung to deliver a letter that has been overdue for twenty years."

The instant the name "Lin Chunjiao" reached his ears, Chen Guoshun's towering frame shuddered violently!

The alloy lug wrench slipped from his grasp, clattering loudly against the cab's iron stirrups. The man's pupils dilated wildly, all the blood draining from his face in mere seconds, turning his features a ghastly, deadened gray!

"Get... GET OUT!" Chen Guoshun suddenly erupted into frenzied roars, his voice shrill and splintered, his hands clutching the door frame as veins bulged like writhing worms across his brow. "I don't know any Lin Chunjiao! That old woman cut ties with me decades ago! Take your trash and get out! One more word and I'll start this rig and crush you into pulp!"

Extending a massive, violent hand, the man shoved hard toward Lin Ziyang, intending to cast the youth down into the sludge and gravel.

#### 8

Pushed back two paces, Lin Ziyang's boots skidded through the mud, yet his arms clamped tightly around the red-lacquered basket, refusing to let it tilt by so much as an inch.

"Chen Guoshun! Just how long do you intend to keep running?!"

Lin Ziyang snapped his head up, unleashing a roar that detonated like thunder!

That shout cut through the howling maritime gale, freezing Chen Guoshun rigid upon the iron ladder.

"You've driven like a stray dog along these highways for thirty years! You don't even dare sleep in a real bed; you don't even dare stop to eat a hot meal!" Lin Ziyang's eyes burned red as he pressed forward step by step, each word dripping with blood. "What do you think you're fleeing from? Are you fleeing from your mother, or are you fleeing from your own selfish, cowardly self who doesn't even have the guts to turn back and look at his own flesh and blood?!"

"Shut your mouth! What do you know?! What does a wet-behind-the-ears kid like you know about anything?!" Clutching his head in frenzy, red veins bursting across his eyes, Chen Guoshun bellowed like an old wolf backed into a corner. "She was the one who cast me aside first! She slapped me in front of all of Keelung! She cursed me as a shameless beast! I swore an oath... I swore that if I didn't make something of myself, I would never set foot in that door again!"

"And look at me now—a filthy truck driver reeking of motor oil! I have no money, I have no status, I made nothing of myself! What right do I have to go back and face her?! What right do I have to look into her pitying, charitable eyes?!"

"She never wanted to see you become some grand figure!"

Lin Ziyang ripped open the bamboo lid of the red-lacquered basket!

Extending both arms, he thrust that small aluminum pot—its ear-lug missing, its surface scorched and battered, the name 'Chen Guoshun' scored deep into its base—unflinchingly before the man's eyes!

"Look! Look at what this is!"

Chen Guoshun's gaze collided headlong with that small pot.

In that split second, time seemed to grind to a total halt. The wind ceased, the breakers fell silent, and the clamorous harbor depot seemed to plunge into the depths of a soundless sea.

Chen Guoshun froze entirely, his eyes bulging as he stared transfixed at that battered, aged aluminum pot. His lips quivered uncontrollably; slowly, hesitantly, his calloused, grime-darkened fingertips reached out, brushing against the familiar gouges around the rim, touching that wavy line at the base signifying his Tiger zodiac...

That was the pot his mother had salvaged from a scrap shop and hammered flat to serve as his personal bowl when they were too destitute to buy tableware; that was the pot his mother used to brew bone broth to soothe his bruises whenever he came home bloody from neighborhood brawls...

Thirty years.

More than ten thousand days and nights. He had convinced himself he had long since erased that decrepit noodle stall from his mind, yet every single scratch upon this metal was branded into the deepest chamber of his soul!

#### 9

"Within this pot once lay ten full years of New Year's Eve longevity noodles," Lin Ziyang's voice softened, laden with boundless sorrow and tenderness.

The youth retrieved that yellowed, fragile calendar leaf from his breast. The paper fluttered gently in the maritime breeze, rustling with fragile whispers.

Beneath the somber daylight, Lin Ziyang read aloud each word with ringing, solemn clarity:

"'A-Xiang, my boy:'

"'It was your mother who wronged you; it was that slap of mine that broke my son's back... Your mother deserves to die; I did not know you were taking the fall for A-Biao. I only feared you would stray from the straight path...'

Chen Guoshun's throat emitted an uncanny, strangled rattle, like an animal whose airway had been severed. His hands hung frozen in mid-air, every muscle throughout his frame convulsing violently.

Lin Ziyang read on:

"'Your mother's leg is broken, and I can no longer shoulder the baskets to search for you. The doctors say this leg can never heal; from now on, I can only climb these stone steps on a crutch... But do not fret: I haven't closed down the stall on the pier; every single night, your mother still keeps the lantern burning there.'

"'It is New Year's Eve; using this small aluminum pot you loved best as a child, your mother stewed the pork trotter noodles you crave most. Longevity noodles stretch long, praying my boy lives a hundred years; pork trotters turn fortune around, washing clean all the injustice from your shoulders.'

"'The noodles are kept warm beneath the steamer. The door is unlocked; A-Xiang, even if you become a beggar out there, even without a single copper, as long as you push open that door, there will always be a bowl of piping hot noodles waiting for you. Do not fear; you don't need to prove anything or become any grand figure. In your mother's eyes, you living safe and sound is the greatest figure this world could ever ask for...'

"'My son, the weather is cold; come home and eat a warm bite of noodles. Mother... misses you so dearly.'"

As the final word dissolved into the howling sea wind, the entire depot plunged into a tomb-like hush.

*THUD!*

With a dull, heavy crash, the burly, towering truck driver, nearly sixty years old, gave way at the knees, collapsing without a shred of pride into the foul mud of oil and gravel!

#### 10

"MOTHER——!!!"

An agonizing wail suppressed for thirty years, tearing through marrow and soul, erupted like thunder from the throat of this hardened man!

Flailing with oil-stained, calloused hands, Chen Guoshun tore the cold, dry aluminum pot from Lin Ziyang's grasp, crushing it frantically against his chest, pressing it desperately against his hollowed cheek, against his very heart!

Burying his frosted hair deep between his muddy knees, the man wept without restraint, his massive shoulders heaving and convulsing, splashing muddy water, sobbing until his breath hitched into violent spasms!

"Mother! I am a beast... I am the most unfilial beast under heaven!"

"Why didn't you hate me... why were you still waiting for me... I never even let you taste a warm bowl of noodles; I didn't even return to look upon you one last time!"

Thirty years.

Thirty thousand kilometers of asphalt, ten thousand lonely, frigid nights.

He had believed he was wagering his stubborn spine against fate, believed he was proving his mettle to his mother through exile. Not until this moment, struck as though by lightning, did revelation shatter him: across these thirty years, when had he ever been a proud, unbending man? He was nothing more than a coward held hostage by his own petty vanity!

That slap from his mother had long since melted into thirty years of warm vigil beneath the steamer; yet he, with thirty years of cold-blooded flight, had driven that crippled mother on the mossy wharf steps into a lonely, icy grave!

Rain mingled with the salty sea mist began to fall, tapping softly against the scarred metal of the aluminum pot with fine, clear chimes.

With shaking, blackened fingers, Chen Guoshun caressed the blurred ink on the paper again and again, pressing his cheek into the hollow of that dry aluminum pot, as though the warmth of angelica, soy sauce, and a mother's embrace from thirty years ago still lingered there, refusing to depart.

#### 11

Standing amidst the veil of drizzle, Lin Ziyang watched the aged trucker weeping like a child in the mud.

The youth's own tears poured unbidden down his cheeks.

In Chen Guoshun's heartbreaking cries, what Lin Ziyang beheld with absolute clarity was none other than himself thirty years hence.

Three years.

Ever since his ruin in Taipei's financial waters, carrying crushing debt and manslaughter on his conscience as a fugitive, had he not been hiding in this rainy mountain town like a stray dog? Had he not used "I am ruined, I have no dignity to face anyone" as a vile excuse to sever all contact with his aged mother in the south?

He had fancied his flight as the final defense of his dignity, unaware that in every single second of his self-pity and resentment, his mother far away was standing by an empty doorway in identical, shattered agony, waiting for a wayward son who dared not return home!

"Ziyang, do you understand now?" Old Gu's words from that morning echoed like a bronze bell striking deep within the youth's soul:

"'Children always assume parents are mountains that will never collapse, assuming they will stand in place forever, waiting for us to prove ourselves, waiting for us to return in triumph.'

"'Yet time is the cruelest one-way street. A parent's aging happens in a single split second; and that warm bowl of soup in their trembling hands may very well be the final warmth they ever leave in this world.'

"'Learning to empathize with your parents' limitations and embracing their clumsy love is the very first great trial of becoming truly mature.'"

Lin Ziyang drew a deep, ragged breath, tilting his head back to let the sea wind scour his face. In that instant, the shadows and cowardice that had haunted him for three years were utterly incinerated by this blaze of filial devotion spanning thirty decades.

Stepping forward, he crouched down, placing a warm and resolute hand gently upon Chen Guoshun's shuddering shoulder.

"Uncle Chen, stand up," Lin Ziyang's voice was gentle yet unshakeable, carrying a resonance that pierced the soul. "The rain is still falling, but the heart within that bowl of noodles... has reached your hands today."

"Mother never blamed you; she only wanted you to come home."

Clutching the small aluminum pot, Chen Guoshun raised his face, streaked with mud and tears. Looking at Lin Ziyang through bloodshot, cloudy eyes, deep within his remorse flickered a faint light unseen in thirty years.

A long silence passed before the elder gritted his teeth, struggling with immense solemnity to rise to his feet from the sludge, inch by inch.

Stripping off his stained undershirt, he wrapped the aluminum pot and the calendar leaf with exquisite tenderness, tucking them deep into the inner pocket of his jacket, closest to his heart.

Turning around, for the first time in thirty years, Chen Guoshun shut off the fuel valve of the rig and pulled the ignition key that had symbolized a lifetime of flight.

"Young brother... thank you." The elder's voice was raspy as scraping bark as he bowed that spine, which had never bent before any man, deeply before Lin Ziyang. "Today... I am going back to Keelung."

"I will kneel before my mother's grave and beg her forgiveness; beside her tombstone, I will cook a steaming bowl of pork trotter noodles with my own hands."

Lin Ziyang smiled and nodded.

Mounting the vintage green bicycle, the youth brought his feet down upon the pedals, riding slowly toward the road back to the mountain town.

Behind him, the harbor mist was quietly parting, and faint beams of daylight pierced the gloom, illuminating that long, winding road stretching toward home.

> The world often mistakes worldly success for filial devotion and returning in glory for honor; yet in the eyes of our parents, robes of a thousand gold pieces cannot rival the warmth of gathering around the hearth, and towering acclaim can never replace the gentle sound of a child calling by their knees.
>
> 我們習慣了向外索取世界的認可，卻往往將最殘忍的傲慢與苛責留給了最愛我們的父母；我們賭氣揮霍著歲月，自以為在抗爭命運，卻不知那扇虛掩的家門背後，父母的生命正隨著蒸籠裡漸冷的餘溫一同無聲凋零。
> We grow accustomed to demanding the world's validation, yet so often reserve our cruelest arrogance and harshness for the parents who love us most; we squander years in stubborn pique, believing we are battling fate, unaware that behind that half-open door, our parents' lives are silently withering away alongside the cooling embers beneath the steamer.
>
> 體諒父母的笨拙，寬恕家庭的傷痕，是人世間最深沉的仁義。不要讓虛榮成為歸途的阻礙，不要讓傲慢演變成終生的絕症；趁暮色未深、柴火尚溫，推開那扇門，接過那一碗凝聚了人間極致深情的熱湯吧。
> To empathize with our parents' clumsy love and forgive the scars of our childhood is the profoundest benevolence under heaven. Never allow vanity to block the road home, nor let arrogance metastasize into an incurable regret of a lifetime; while the twilight has not yet deepened and the hearth coals still glow, push open that door, and receive that bowl of warm soup that holds the most supreme devotion of this mortal realm.

### 💡 Story Encyclopedia: Taiwanese Pig's Trotter Vermicelli and Traditional Fortune-Turning Customs

* **Cultural Heritage of Pig's Trotter Vermicelli for Warding Off Misfortune**:
  In Taiwanese folk tradition, whenever a family member endures hardship, completes a prison sentence, or reaches milestone leap years, elders lovingly prepare a piping-hot bowl of "Pig's Trotter Vermicelli." The pig's trotter symbolizes kicking away lingering misfortune, while uncut, long thin noodles represent prolonged vitality and enduring blessings, transcending culinary delight to embody unconditional parental forgiveness.
* **Traditional Slow-Braised Angelica and Dark Soy Sauce Craft**:
  Authentic Taiwanese trotters are first rendered in lard with shallots, caramelized with rock sugar and dark-brewed black soybean soy sauce, then simmered for three hours in pure rice wine alongside Chinese angelica, astragalus, and wolfberries. Rich collagen dissolves into a velvety gelatinous broth that firms into a savory jelly as it cools, locking in its deep aroma across decades of memory.
