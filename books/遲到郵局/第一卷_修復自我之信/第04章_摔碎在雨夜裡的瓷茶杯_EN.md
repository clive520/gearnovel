# 《遲到郵局》（The Overdue Post Office）

## 第一卷：修復自我之信 / Volume I: Letters of Self-Restoration
### 第 04 章：摔碎在雨夜裡的瓷茶杯 / Chapter 04: The Porcelain Teacup Shattered in the Rainy Night

---

#### 1

The morning mist on the fourth day was heavier than all preceding dawns.

The dense, chalk-white vapor hung like unslaked lime slurry, completely swallowing the historic street of Jiufen, Mount Keelung, and the distant East China Sea beyond. Cold moisture crawled upward through the fissures of the blue-stone steps, condensing into heavy, trembling beads of water upon the wooden veranda pillars of the "Overdue Post Office."

Lin Zi-yang had awakened early.

Stuck across the palm of his hand was a cotton medicinal plaster gifted to him yesterday by the old blacksmith of Ah-Quan's Forge; the raw, bloody abrasions torn by the hemp ropes while hauling the cart had already closed into dry scabs. Clad in a clean black hooded sweatshirt, he held a long bamboo broom, meticulously sweeping the fallen leaves shed by the ancient camphor tree into tidy piles before the stone stoop.

His movements no longer carried the frantic agitation and bristling resentment of his first morning. With every sweep, the friction of bamboo bristles against the wet flagstones fell into a clean, rhythmic cadence: *Shaaa—shaaa—shaaa.*

"Zi-yang."

Old Gu's voice drifted out from the sheltered veranda.

Today, the old postmaster had changed into a thick, charcoal-gray woollen Sun Yat-sen suit of vintage cut. Holding his familiar ceramic teapot in one hand, his gaze peered through wire-rimmed reading glasses, watching the upright youth before the threshold with a gentle, approving stillness:

"Come inside. Today's letter... is somewhat special."

Zi-yang stood the broom upright against the doorframe, brushed leaf fragments from his palms, and walked briskly into the dry, cedar-scented sorting hall.

Resting dead center upon the red cypress table was an old, dark package bound tightly with coarse hemp twine. Wrapping the exterior was a hand-knitted navy blue wool sweater, heavily pilled with age, its ribbed collar frayed and stretched out of shape.

And directly atop that worn sweater lay an antique, battered envelope.

Crafted from the cheapest brown wrapping paper, the envelope was mottled with black grease stains and coal dust, faintly emitting a sour, fermenting stench characteristic of street vagrants and rain-soaked gutter alleyways.

Across the center of the envelope, written in clumsy, trembling brushstrokes with watered-down ink, ran a row of characters:

*【No. 36, Jishan Street, Ruifang District, New Taipei City / Yongfa Grocery Store / For the Attention of Owner Chen Shui-fa】*

And firmly taped across the back of the envelope with yellowed cellophane tape were two banknotes that had ceased circulating more than half a century ago—**Ten-Dollar New Taiwan Dollar Notes, Series of the 43rd Year of the Republic**!

Weighing down the brittle paper currency were three shards of blue-and-white porcelain bound together with coarse red cotton thread. The edges of the shards were as sharp as surgical razors, their glazed surfaces bearing half a faded peony blossom painted in traditional cobalt blue.

"This... is this a thief's letter of apology?" Zi-yang's heart skipped an involuntary beat, his eyes locked upon those two blackened ten-dollar bills.

"This is an apology... purchased with forty-five whole years of wandering, begging, and exile."

Old Gu walked slowly to the edge of the table, extending a white-gloved hand to smooth the grease-stained edges of the envelope:

"The sender was named Lin Chun-sheng. Forty years ago, the folk of Jinguashi and Ruifang Old Street called him 'Mute A-Sheng.'"

---

#### 2

Old Gu opened a lower drawer, retrieved a dog-eared volume of *The Annals of Ruifang Township: Commercial Directory of the 1960s*, and flipped to page thirty-six.

Mounted upon the yellowed page was an archival black-and-white photograph documenting the bustling "Yongfa Grocery Store" on Jishan Street in its heyday. Along the wooden counter stood rows of glass candy jars, vintage tobacco boxes, and a neat line of fine porcelain teacups.

"The winter of the 61st Year of the Republic," Old Gu began in a voice as low and solemn as glowing charcoal in a hearth:

"That year, it rained continuously in Ruifang for three straight months. The Keelung River surged its banks, and the copper mines suspended all shifts. Seventeen-year-old Lin Chun-sheng was an orphan, born deaf and mute, hauling sacks as a day laborer along the docks. On that freezing night, he had burned with a violent fever for three days, starving, shivering, collapsing near death in the alleyways."

"In the dead of night, he stumbled toward the door of the Yongfa Grocery Store. The shop had long shuttered, but the wooden hurricane boards barricading the entrance were rotted by rain, and a central latch had fallen away."

"Lin Chun-sheng squeezed his frail body through the gap. Groping blindly through the pitch-black counter space, all he wanted was a mouthful of leftover rice. But in the dark, his elbow knocked against an expensive Jiangxi Jingdezhen blue-and-white porcelain teacup displayed upon the counter."

Old Gu's cadence was quiet, yet every word struck Zi-yang's sternum like an iron punch:

"The teacup shattered against the concrete floor into multiple pieces. The sharp crash was deafening in the dead of night. The shop owner, Mr. Chen Shui-fa, came charging down the attic stairs carrying a storm lantern."

"Lin Chun-sheng lost his mind to sheer terror. A seventeen-year-old boy, terrified out of his wits, scooped up the only two ten-dollar notes resting in the open cash drawer, grabbed three jagged shards of the broken teacup from the floor, rammed through the loose wooden boards, and bolted headlong into the torrential rain."

Zi-yang's throat constricted violently, his fists tightening until his knuckles ached.

This scene... was too agonizingly familiar.

On that midnight when his motorcycle had demolished the sign of Ah-Quan's Forge, he too had been paralyzed by blind panic, killing his headlights, fleeing like a sewer rat into the black mountain air.

"Owner Chen Shui-fa called the police," Old Gu continued. "Though the loss was only twenty dollars and a broken cup, Chen was a man of unbending principle who despised thievery above all else. He pasted wanted notices across the old street, swearing he would drag the thief before a judge to confess his crime."

"And Lin Chun-sheng... while fleeing through the dark, tumbled down a ravine, fracturing his right leg. The wound festered and swelled with poison. Staring at those two mud-spattered ten-dollar notes and the razor-sharp porcelain shards in his fist, the terror in his soul completely crushed him."

"He dared not turn himself in; he dared not return to Ruifang; he dared not pause in any town where an acquaintance might recognize his face."

Old Gu looked at Zi-yang:

"To escape the crime of stealing twenty dollars, Lin Chun-sheng wandered the island of Taiwan as a fugitive for forty-five years."

"He hauled cargo at Keelung Port, crushed stone in the Hualien marble quarries, cut sugarcane as an unregistered laborer in Taitung. He never dared marry; he never dared apply for a national identity card; he never dared collect wages through a legal employer. Whenever he caught sight of a police uniform, whenever a siren wailed in the distance, he would scramble on all fours like a scalded dog, diving into storm drains and bridge abutments."

"He turned his entire life into a nameless shadow."

"Until last month... inside an indigent homeless shelter beneath the Taipei Main Station underground mall, he was diagnosed with terminal lung cancer."

Old Gu pushed the package wrapped in the frayed navy sweater across the table toward Zi-yang:

"As he lay dying, with trembling hands, he pulled this envelope from the deepest lining of his rotted shoe—soaked in forty-five years of sweat and fear—and handed it to a volunteer social worker, begging that it be delivered to our old post office."

"Inside, he wrote only a single sentence:"

Old Gu turned the envelope over, pointing to the crooked brushwork blurred by countless dried tears:

*“Owner Chen... I was not born a thief. These twenty dollars, and these three shards of your cup... I return them to you. I beg of you... do not record my name in the ledger of wicked men. The sinner Chun-sheng, kowtows in repentance.”*

---

#### 3

Zi-yang stared transfixed at the two blackened ten-dollar bills, a searing, blinding burn rushing into his eyes.

That sensation was not pity.

It was the **agony of an unsterilized scalpel tearing open his own chest**, dragging the festering, rotting abscess hidden in the deepest corner of his soul out into the daylight!

"Forty-five years..." The boy's voice shook uncontrollably, his fingernails digging deep into his palms:

"For twenty dollars... for a broken porcelain cup... he destroyed his entire life?!"

"What destroyed him was not those twenty dollars."

Old Gu rose, stepped around the counter, and rested a heavy, withered hand upon the boy's shoulder:

"What destroyed him... was the porcelain cup shattered in the mud that night; the prison of terror within his own heart that **refused to face error, refused to turn back and reflect upon itself**."

"Zi-yang."

The old postmaster's voice was like a temple bell reverberating through the dawn:

"Owner Chen Shui-fa passed away twenty years ago. The person currently keeping watch over No. 36 Jishan Street is Chen's seventy-year-old eldest son, Mr. Chen Yong-sheng."

"Slap your green mailbag across your shoulder."

"Go to Jishan Street."

"And deliver this apology—purchased with a vagrant's entire lifetime—to the doorstep of the Chen family."

……

Nine o'clock in the morning.

Jishan Street, Jiufen.

The commercial street had just stirred to life after the rain. Steamer baskets at morning breakfast stalls puffed white clouds of sweet bun vapor, the wet stone pavers gleaming softly under the climbing sun.

Zi-yang, with the green leather satchel slung across his chest, parted the gathering crowds with heavy, deliberate strides, coming to a halt before No. 36 Jishan Street.

It was a two-story Japanese-era brick townhouse crowned with a carved Baroque pediment. Though the red masonry was weathered, the timber display cases were wiped immaculate. Upon the dark cypress counter rested an orderly row of traditional clay tea canisters, beneath a century-old black lacquer sign inscribed in gold:

*【A Century of Heritage · Yongfa Fine Teas】*

Inside the shop, an elderly gentleman in a navy linen smock, his silver hair neatly combed, leaned over the counter wearing wire-rimmed glasses, carefully portioning freshly roasted Wenshan Paochong tea with a wooden scoop.

Zi-yang stood beyond the threshold, drawing a deep, shuddering lungful of air.

He reached down to touch the satchel at his hip. That greasy, sour envelope was resting quietly within the leather pocket.

"Young brother, looking for tea? Fresh winter roast, come inside and have a cup of hot water," Mr. Chen Yong-sheng called out, looking up with a kindly wave upon noticing the stiff, stone-faced youth on the stoop.

Zi-yang stepped across the wooden threshold.

The youth walked straight to the counter. No pleasantries, no hesitation. With both hands held level and solemn, he withdrew from the green bag the package wrapped in the old sweater, the two battered ten-dollar bills, and the three porcelain shards tied with red thread.

The instant those porcelain shards and vintage banknotes touched the polished marble counter—

The warm smile upon Mr. Chen Yong-sheng's face froze solid.

The elder's gaze locked dead upon the cobalt-blue peony petal glazed across the porcelain. The wooden tea scoop in his fingers began to tremble violently, dried tea leaves spilling across the stone:

"This... this is... a shard of the 'Kangxi Wucai Peony Cup'?!"

The old shopkeeper pushed his spectacles up with shaking hands, cradling the three shards in his palms, cloudy tears flooding his eyes in a single second:

"This was... my father's most treasured tea vessel! Forty-five years ago... shattered by that mute boy who broke in during the storm..."

The elder snapped his head up, his gaze shaking as he stared at the youth before him:

"Child... where on earth did you get this?! Where is the person who took it?!"

---

#### 4

Zi-yang looked at the trembling elder, his lips white as paper.

The boy drew a long, ragged breath, let his arms drop straight to his sides, and bent his waist into a rigid, perpendicular bow before Mr. Chen Yong-sheng:

"Sir."

Zi-yang's voice was quiet, yet it carried the crushing weight of blood and bone:

"The sender... was named Lin Chun-sheng. Forty years ago, you all called him 'Mute A-Sheng.'"

"Last week... he passed away in an indigent homeless shelter in Taipei."

Mr. Chen Yong-sheng's body jolted as if struck, the porcelain shards clinking against his ring: "Passed... passed away?!"

"When he died, he owned nothing in this world except an old sweater and this letter."

Zi-yang pushed the greasy, tear-stained envelope gently across the marble toward the old shopkeeper:

"Forty-five years ago, he was seventeen. Burning with fever for three days, starving to death, he slipped into your shop looking for scraps of cold rice and knocked over your father's cup. Terrified by the storm lantern, he grabbed twenty dollars from the cash drawer and three shards from the floor, snapping his leg as he tumbled into the ravine."

Zi-yang's eyes burned crimson, tears welling at the rims:

"To escape the crime of taking those twenty dollars... he wandered Taiwan as a fugitive for forty-five years. Hauling rocks, digging ditches, sleeping under bridges... he never dared marry, never dared hold an ID card, never dared hold his head high before the law."

"These twenty dollars were dug out from the deepest lining of his rotting shoe as he took his final breath."

"In his letter, he begs of your family... he begs you not to record his name in the ledger of wicked men."

The boy's voice broke completely into a ragged sob.

Mr. Chen Yong-sheng stood paralyzed behind the counter. Extending withered, trembling hands, he cradled the filthy envelope, reading the clumsy brushstrokes on the reverse—*【The sinner Chun-sheng, kowtows in repentance】*.

Scalding tears cascaded down the old shopkeeper's cheeks.

"Foolish boy... how could you be so foolish, ahhh————!"

The old man slammed his palms against the counter, crying out in a heartbroken wail that shattered the stillness of the shop:

"A-Sheng... you foolish, tragic child!!"

Weeping uncontrollably, the old shopkeeper turned, shuffled to the household altar in the rear, and pulled an aged, yellowed ledger from the bottom drawer.

Opening to the very first leaf, there ran a line of regular script brushed in vermilion ink:

*【Night of the 13th of the Twelfth Month, 61st Year of the Republic: A thief broke in, shattered a Peony Cup, stole Twenty Dollars in legal tender. The thief was young, lame, starving and freezing; his plight is deeply pitiable.】*

And directly beneath those characters, the entry had been crossed out with thick black ink, accompanied by an addendum brushed by the elder Chen shopkeeper in his own hand:

*【Morning of the 14th of the Twelfth Month: Confirmed the thief was the dockside mute orphan, A-Sheng. If he returns to confess, this old man wishes to take him in as an apprentice tea-picker to spare him from hunger. If he dares not return, this debt is erased forever by this brush, never to be pursued.】*

"Look at this... look at this, child!"

Mr. Chen Yong-sheng shoved the yellowed ledger before Zi-yang, his entire frame shaking violently with grief:

"My father called the police that night only because he was angry that a young boy was turning to crime! The very next morning, when he learned he was a mute orphan starving in the gutter, my father went to the police station at dawn to withdraw the report!"

"My father crossed out the debt with his own brush! He hung a recruitment sign outside our door for three whole years, waiting for him to come back, wanting to give him a hot bowl of rice and teach him the tea trade!!"

"Why didn't he come back?! Why didn't he turn his head to look back even once?!"

The old shopkeeper slumped over the counter, clutching the three porcelain shards and the two banknotes to his chest, weeping bitterly:

"Forty-five years... forty-five whole years!! For twenty dollars, for a few broken shards... he threw his entire life into the mud under bridges... how could there be such a heartbreakingly foolish boy in this world!!"

The grief-stricken weeping resonated through the tea-scented hall.

Zi-yang's knees buckled beneath him. Slumping against the timber counter, he collapsed onto the cold stone floor on his knees.

---

#### 5

The boy's tears cascaded like a storm, completely soaking the front of the green leather satchel.

Kneeling upon the stone, clutching his skull with both hands, he unleashed a raw, suffocating, agonizing sob.

"He wasn't foolish..."

The boy dragged those ragged words from the depths of his tearing windpipe, tears and mucus flooding into his mouth, bitter as bile:

"He wasn't foolish... because I did the exact same thing..."

Lin Zi-yang knelt upon the marble floor, facing the empty air, facing that yellowed ledger, frantically confessing the filthiest, most cowardly demon lurking within his own soul:

"Four nights ago... the person who smashed the sign of Ah-Quan's Forge on the highway was ME..."

"I shattered the post, I saw no one came out... and my first instinct wasn't to knock and apologize, but to kill my headlights and run like a rabid dog..."

"In court, I lied to the judge, sneering that it was just a pile of scrap iron... I acted like I didn't care... I told myself nobody died so it wasn't a big deal..."

The boy violently slapped his own cheeks twice, blood welling at the corner of his lips:

"If I hadn't been sent to this post office... if I hadn't seen this letter from A-Sheng..."

"Forty-five years from now... I would have become the next Lin Chun-sheng!! I would have hidden in sewer drains, spending an entire lifetime of lies to cover up the cowardice of my seventeen-year-old self!!"

"I was a coward... I was a wretched coward who was inches away from an irreversible abyss!!"

Mr. Chen Yong-sheng's weeping ceased.

Wiping his tears, the old shopkeeper shuffled out from behind the counter, reaching down with warm, thick hands to pull the weeping, self-flagellating boy into an embrace:

"Child... don't cry... don't cry anymore..."

The elder's tears fell warm against the boy's neck:

"A-Sheng is gone... but he delivered you back to the light."

"The fact that you can kneel here and weep, that you can see the stains upon your own spirit... means you did not walk down that dead-end road!"

"Child... we do not fear making mistakes in this life; what we fear is spending an entire lifetime lacking the courage to turn back."

Mr. Chen Yong-sheng picked up the ledger, lit three sticks of fragrant incense before the household altar, and reverently placed them into the bronze censer.

He laid the greasy letter, the two vintage banknotes, and the three porcelain shards gently before the rising smoke:

"A-Sheng... rest in peace."

"The ledger of the Chen family never once recorded you as a wicked man."

"Your debt... is cleared."

Thin ribbons of incense smoke coiled upward, drifting through the carved Baroque transoms of the century-old shop, dispersing serenely into the clouds beneath the warm morning sun of Jiufen.

……

Three o'clock in the afternoon.

Before the doors of the "Overdue Post Office."

Zi-yang walked back up the stone steps, carrying the empty green mailbag.

His eyes were still swollen, his cheek bearing the red scrape of fingernails, yet his posture had undergone an astonishing, marrow-deep transformation—his chest was thrown out straight, every step upon the stone as rooted and immovable as an ancient pine anchored in granite.

Old Gu stood beneath the porch, watching the returning youth.

The elder offered no hollow platitudes, merely unclipping from his belt a small brass craft shears that had accompanied him for fifty years, pressing it into Zi-yang's hand.

"Go," Old Gu said, pointing toward the ancient Acacia tree in the rear garden:

"The tenons for Master Zhang's new forge bench are still three millimeters too proud. Take your tools, and finish building that table."

"Tomorrow... your first volume of probation draws to a close."

Zi-yang took the shears and woodcarving chisel, his eyes as clear as morning dew. The seventeen-year-old youth bowed deeply toward the old postmaster:

"Yes. Old Postmaster Gu."

The boy turned and walked toward the workshop in the back, his footsteps ringing crisp and resolute through the quiet halls of the old Western house.

### 【信差迴響 / Messenger's Echo】
> **本章品德力量：【自省與贖罪（Self-Reflection）】**  
> **Virtue of this Chapter: Self-Reflection & Atonement**
> 
> *自省，是靈魂在幽暗處點燃的第一盞明燈。*  
> 
> *Self-reflection is the very first lamp lit by the soul in the dark.*  
> 
> *我們每個人在人生路上，都曾摔碎過屬於別人的「瓷茶杯」。*  
> *有時是因為年輕氣盛，有時是因為一時貪念，有時僅僅是因為懦弱與恐懼。當清脆的碎裂聲在黑夜裡響起時，最容易的選擇，往往是轉身逃跑，將錯誤甩在身後的迷霧中。*  
> 
> *Every single one of us has, at some point along our life's journey, shattered another's "porcelain teacup."*  
> *Sometimes it is born of youthful arrogance; sometimes of fleeting greed; sometimes of pure, unvarnished cowardice and fear. When that crisp sound of destruction rings out in the dead of night, the easiest choice is almost always to turn on our heels and run, hurling our mistakes into the fog behind us.*  
> 
> *我們總以為自己逃開了懲罰，卻不知在那轉身的一瞬間，我們已經親手給自己的靈魂戴上了一副終身無法卸下的無形鐐銬。四十五年的流浪，換不回一刻的坦然；無數個深夜的噩夢，永遠在拷問著那個不敢回頭的逃兵。*  
> 
> *We foolishly tell ourselves that we have slipped past punishment, blind to the truth that the very instant we turned to flee, we clamped an invisible shackle around our own spirit that no key can ever unlock. Forty-five years of wandering cannot purchase a single moment of peace; countless midnight nightmares will forever interrogate the deserter who lacked the spine to turn back.*  
> 
> *承認自己犯過錯，需要撕心裂肺的劇痛與刮骨療毒的勇氣。*  
> *但真正的救贖，從來不在於別人是否原諒，而在於你是否敢於在陽光下直視那個滿身污泥的自己，停下逃亡的腳步，轉身向著光明走去。*  
> 
> *To confess that we were wrong demands agonizing, bone-scraping courage.*  
> *Yet true atonement has never depended on whether others grant their forgiveness; it rests entirely upon whether you dare to look squarely at your mud-stained self beneath the sun, halt your flight, and turn your face toward the light.*  
> 
> *自省的眼淚，是世間唯一能洗滌靈魂的甘泉；*  
> *唯有敢於痛哭認錯的人，才能在破碎的廢墟之上，重塑生命的尊嚴。*  
> 
> *The tears of self-reflection are the only spring on earth that can wash the soul clean;*  
> *Only one who possesses the courage to weep and own their shame can rebuild, atop the shattered ruins of their errors, the true dignity of life.*
