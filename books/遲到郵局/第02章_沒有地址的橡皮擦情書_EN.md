# 《遲到郵局》（The Overdue Post Office）

## 第一卷：修復自我之信 / Volume I: Letters of Self-Restoration
### 第 02 章：沒有地址的橡皮擦情書 / Chapter 02: The Addressee-less Eraser Letter

---

#### 1

At five-thirty in the morning in Jiufen, the mountain wind carried a piercing, damp chill.

Dawn had not yet fully broken; across the eastern sea horizon of the Pacific, a faint wash of dark violet morning glow was being torn into fragmented ribbons by tumbling, low-altitude mists.

Lin Zi-yang awoke to the crisp, rhythmic swish of a bamboo broom sweeping across flagstones: *Shaaa—shaaa—shaaa.*

He rolled over, and the muscle aches left behind by yesterday's violent rush and prolonged emotional tension washed over his calves and shoulders like a returning tide. Yet when he opened his eyes and took in the orderly rows of Formosan fir rafters lining the ceiling of the side wing, and inhaled the faint mingling of incense and charcoal smoke, his first reaction was not the hollow irritation that usually greeted him in his Keelung rental room, but a quietude he had not felt in years.

He quickly dressed and walked out into the front hall.

Aboard the iron stove behind the red cypress sorting counter, an aluminum kettle was already whistling softly, puffing plumes of white steam. At the corner of the wood lay two freshly steamed white mantou buns, a small saucer of home-cured golden dried radish strips, and a bowl of white rice congee shimmering with a delicate layer of sweet rice oil.

Old Gu, leaning on his long bamboo broom having just cleared away the fallen camphor leaves before the threshold, stepped inside through the frame.

"Awake?" Old Gu stood the broom behind the timber door and untied his gray canvas work apron. "Wash your face, sit down, and drink your congee. If you deliver letters on an empty stomach, your shadow will drift on your feet; and once your shadow begins to drift, your heart will panic with it."

"Old Gu..." Zi-yang stood by the counter somewhat awkwardly, rubbing his palms against the rough denim of his jeans. "Today's letter... what kind of letter is it?"

Old Gu did not reply at once. Walking behind the long timber table, he reached for the third tier of wooden pigeonholes, pulling open the slot labeled "Jinguashi Copper Processing Plant · 65th Year of the Republic."

The withered fingers of the old postmaster reached deep within, withdrawing not a typical parchment envelope.

It was a hardbound dictionary nearly five centimeters thick, its covers rubbed raw and detached at the spine—*The Commercial Press New Practical Mandarin Dictionary*. The red vinyl binding was reinforced along every seam with yellowed cellophane tape; the binding cords had snapped, and the dog-eared, curling pages exuded an ancient, musty scent of mothballs and graphite lead that had slept at the bottom of a cedar chest for half a century.

Old Gu laid the dictionary gently down before Zi-yang on the red cypress wood.

Taped securely onto the very first flyleaf was a solitary, brittle sheet torn from a lined student notebook.

At the top of the paper, penned in formal regular script with blue-black Parker fountain pen ink, were six words:

*【To the Person I Envy Most】*

Yet the entire body of the letter was chilling to behold—

What had once been dense rows of tightly scribbled pencil lines had been subjected to some violent, nearly frantic eraser, rubbed back and forth across the paper countless times with obsessive, furious pressure!

The paper fibers had been chafed raw, shredded into fuzzy tears, and in the center, two transparent, three-centimeter-long holes had been worn completely through! Countless black eraser shavings and graphite smudges were ground like dried mud into the jagged lacerations of the sheet.

Only in a small, unrubbed blank space at the very foot of the paper did a single sentence survive, preserved only because the steel nib had gouged deep through the fibers into the backing card:

*“...I stole the recommendation form. But I lack the courage to look into your eyes. Autumn, 1976.”*

And in the recipient and sender fields upon the envelope, everything was completely blank.

No recipient name, no sender address, not even a cancelled postage stamp.

---

#### 2

"This... this letter doesn't even have an address?" Zi-yang stood dumbfounded, pointing his finger at the battered dictionary. "Old Gu, how am I supposed to deliver this? There are twenty-three million people in Taiwan. Thousands upon thousands have walked out of Jinguashi over the years. With just one sentence saying 'I stole the recommendation form,' where on earth am I supposed to find the recipient?"

Old Gu lifted the hot kettle, pouring boiling tea into the coarse ceramic bowl set before Zi-yang.

The elder raised his clear, deep eyes, gazing at the youth through the swirling column of steam:

"Zi-yang, why do you think... the sender of this letter, fifty years ago, took an eraser and repeatedly rubbed away the words he had written?"

Zi-yang looked down at the paper worn raw with holes, his brows knitting tight. Rebellious though he was, the boy possessed sharp instincts; after surviving on the streets through countless evasions and lies, he held an innate understanding of the emotion known as concealment:

"Because... he regretted it? Or... he was terrified of someone else seeing it?"

"The person he feared seeing it was not someone else." Old Gu extended a calloused finger, tapping lightly against the frayed edge of the hole in the paper:

"He was terrified of seeing it himself."

The elder's voice was soft, yet it resonated like a temple bell through the quiet hall:

"When a person commits a despicable act deep in their heart, their first instinct is rarely to apologize to the victim. Instead, they frantically invent excuses to rationalize their deed: *'It was just bad luck on his part,' 'He was never worthy to begin with,' 'Anyone else in my shoes would have done the exact same thing.'*"

"He took an eraser and rubbed this paper again and again, tearing the fibers, blackening the desk. He foolishly believed that as long as the pencil lead was wiped clean, the sin born of his envy could be treated as though it had never existed in this world."

Old Gu drew an aged, hardbound black-and-white photograph from his breast pocket and slid it across the wood toward Zi-yang:

"The sender of this letter is named He Jing-yao. Today, he is a Distinguished Emeritus Chair Professor in the Department of Chemical Engineering at National Taiwan University, and a senior academician of the Academia Sinica."

The photograph captured the graduation class of the Jinguashi Copper Processing Plant Technical School in the 65th Year of the Republic.

Standing side by side in the center of the front row were two eighteen-year-old boys in coarse canvas work uniforms. The youth on the left was broad-shouldered and thick-browed, holding an honor certificate for mechanical drawing, his grin brilliant as midsummer sunshine; while the boy on the right was frail, wearing thick horn-rimmed glasses, his posture stiff, carrying a brooding, defensive shadow in his eyes—the young He Jing-yao.

"The bright, smiling boy on the left was named Chen Han-song," Old Gu murmured, a distant, sorrowful tenderness entering his gaze:

"In 1976, the Jinguashi Copper Processing Plant held a rigorous half-year technical competition among more than three hundred young apprentices to select one promising worker for a state-funded fellowship to study non-ferrous metallurgy at Waseda University in Japan. Chen Han-song ranked first in practical forge skills and first in theory. The single recommendation document had already been signed by the plant director and locked inside the technical school's administration archive."

"Yet on the night before the document was to be dispatched to the Ministry of Education in Taipei, the security bars of the office window were pried apart."

"The recommendation form vanished from the cabinet. There were no fingerprints at the scene, only an abandoned hacksaw left behind in the corner."

Zi-yang's breath caught slightly: "He Jing-yao stole it?"

"He Jing-yao had ranked second in the evaluations," Old Gu replied, his tone as even as mountain wind. "With the original form stolen, the Ministry's strict deadline lapsed. Under immense pressure, the director invoked the emergency alternate clause, sending the first runner-up—He Jing-yao—aboard the passenger ship bound for Yokohama."

"And what happened to Chen Han-song?" Zi-yang could not stop himself from asking.

"Chen Han-song's family was desperately poor. His father had been paralyzed from the waist down years earlier in a mine gas explosion, leaving four younger siblings with empty bellies. Stripped of his overseas scholarship, and burdened by vicious rumors that 'he had staged the theft out of negligence,' he had no choice but to stay behind in the dark pits, swinging a pickaxe to mine copper."

Old Gu took off his reading glasses and let out a soft, heavy sigh:

"Three years later, in 1979, an immense flooding cave-in struck the Seventh Adit of Benshan in Jinguashi. To cover the retreat of six fellow miners behind him, Chen Han-song braced his shoulder against a snapping cedar timber prop. A forty-ton slab of copper-bearing granite crushed down from above... obliterating his ribs and spine on the spot."

"When he died, he was only twenty-one years old."

---

#### 3

Dead silence filled the sorting hall.

The early morning mountain breeze seeped through the cracks of the wooden door, flipping the brittle pages of the aged dictionary with a papery rustle. The eraser-shredded letter trembled violently in the draft, like a white moth with broken wings in a storm.

Zi-yang felt as though a frozen fist had clamped tightly around his heart.

"Then... what about He Jing-yao?" Zi-yang's throat felt dry as bone.

"He Jing-yao earned his doctorate at Waseda University. At thirty-five, he became the youngest full professor at National Taiwan University; at forty, he patented a revolutionary high-purity electrolytic oxygen-free copper refining technique. He accumulated countless honors and worldwide renown."

Old Gu looked up, pushing the green leather mailbag into Zi-yang's arms:

"At ten o'clock this morning, at the NTU Alumni Hall on Xinsheng South Road in Da'an District, Taipei, Academician He Jing-yao's Eightieth Birthday Academic Disciples' Gratitude Symposium will take place."

"Lin Zi-yang."

Old Gu locked eyes with the boy, his gaze sharp as a tempered blade:

"Take this dictionary."

"Go tell that academician standing upon his gilded pedestal—"

"This letter, which he has spent fifty whole years trying to erase, must today be read aloud by his own hands."

……

Nine forty-five in the morning.

International Lecture Hall, First Floor, NTU Alumni Hall, Xinsheng South Road, Da'an District, Taipei.

The atmosphere here was an entirely different world from the damp, freezing stone steps of Jiufen. Central air conditioning hummed softly, maintaining a crisp, dry twenty-two degrees Celsius; the floor was blanketed in plush, deep-crimson velvet carpeting, and dozens of crystal chandeliers bathed the banquet hall in shimmering gold.

Above the stage hung a massive gold-leaf banner:

*【A Master of Generations · The Peach and Plum Speak Not—Celebrating the 81st Birthday and Academic Legacy of Academician He Jing-yao】*

The hall was packed with senior executives from Taiwan's chemical industry conglomerates, deans and chancellors of national universities, and dozens of well-heeled scholars and disciples in tailored suits carrying bouquets of flowers.

At that moment, at the center of the lectern, an elderly gentleman in a bespoke navy-blue suit, his silver hair combed impeccably back, stood radiating distinguished elegance and confidence as he delivered his keynote address to the hundreds of scholars before him.

He Jing-yao.

His voice was warm and sonorous, carrying the unhurried authority of a world-renowned intellect:

"...Fifty years have passed. Whenever I look back upon those days walking out from that barren, impoverished mountain town of Jinguashi, I am filled with profound gratitude for the grace of fate. There are no shortcuts upon the road of science. Only absolute diligence, pure focus, and an unclouded, candid pursuit of truth can sustain us as we pierce the veil of the microscopic material world..."

Thunderous applause erupted from the floor.

He Jing-yao smiled warmly, adjusting his rimless spectacles, basking in this crowning pinnacle of his life's achievements.

Yet at the precise instant the applause began to taper off and the master of ceremonies prepared to wheel out the ceremonial cake—

The two heavy acoustic double doors at the rear of the hall were slammed open from the outside with a deafening crash!

*BANG————!*

Hundreds of heads whipped around in unison toward the entryway.

There stood a seventeen-year-old youth in a black hooded sweatshirt and muddy sneakers, an aged, dark-green cowhide mailbag slung across his shoulder, standing stone-faced at the edge of the crimson carpet, his clothes glistening with the chilly Taipei drizzle.

Several private security guards turned pale and rushed forward to intercept him:

"Kid! This is a closed academic summit! No entry without an invitation! Get out immediately!"

"Move."

Zi-yang did not spare the guards a single glance.

His eyes were startlingly calm, remarkably clear, carrying a cold, immovable gravity that he had never possessed before. Clamping his right hand over the bronze buckle of the green mailbag, he marched forward, step by step, down the crimson runner toward the podium ten meters away.

The yellow mountain mud clinging to the soles of his shoes left a stark, filthy trail of footprints across the pristine carpet.

"Lin Zi-yang! Halt!" A guard reached to seize his shoulder.

Zi-yang twisted his torso sharply, his shoulder ramming the guard aside, surging up the three steps of the dais to plant himself directly before Academician He Jing-yao's lectern, beneath the shocked, horrified stares of the entire assembly!

---

#### 4

"Who... who are you?"

The patrician smile on He Jing-yao's face froze solid. Decades of sitting in high administrative seats could not shield him from the ominous, predatory momentum radiating from this mud-splattered youth.

"Professor He Jing-yao."

Zi-yang spoke. The boy's voice, captured by the high-sensitivity gooseneck microphone on the lectern, reverberated across the cavernous banquet hall with the sharp, chilling crack of breaking stone:

"You have a registered letter. It is overdue by exactly fifty years and three months."

"Please sign for it."

The entire hall fell into a bizarre, suffocating silence.

Two of He Jing-yao's star disciples lunged forward, barking furiously: "Insolence! Where did this feral brat come from?! Today is Academician He's birthday celebration! Security! Throw him out this instant!"

"Let him speak."

He Jing-yao suddenly raised a trembling hand, halting his disciples.

The elder's gaze was locked dead upon the green leather mailbag slung across Zi-yang's chest. In that fraction of a second, the rosy color drained from the old scholar's cheeks at a terrifying speed, his pupils convulsing in spastic terror.

He knew that bag.

It was the bag carried by Old Gu, the only mailman in Jinguashi fifty years ago who pedaled his bicycle through the mining mist day after day!

Zi-yang unfastened the bronze buckle of the bag with absolute composure.

He drew no weapon, no blade.

From the satchel, he lifted with both hands the heavy, red vinyl volume bound with yellowed tape—*The Commercial Press New Practical Mandarin Dictionary*.

The moment this battered dictionary was slammed down onto the polished lectern beside an expensive arrangement of white lilies—

He Jing-yao's body shuddered violently. His hands gripped the mahogany ledge so hard his knuckles turned ghastly white, a strangled, choking gasp tearing from his windpipe!

"This dictionary..." He Jing-yao's voice shook, every syllable trembling.

"You left this inside your desk at the Jinguashi technical school fifty years ago."

Zi-yang extended an unwavering finger, slowly turning back the front cover of the dictionary before hundreds of university presidents, chancellors, and academicians.

The sheet of paper, frayed into threads and punctured with two holes from frantic eraser scrubbing, was instantly captured by the high-definition document camera and cast across the ten-meter-tall projection screen behind the stage!

Countless black pencil smudges, crushed paper fibers, and that final row of gouged fountain pen script—

Were magnified for all the world to see!

The room erupted into stunned gasps!

Scholars and students alike held their breath in sheer horror; several NTU professors sprang from their seats, pushing their spectacles up in disbelief to read the projected line:

*【I stole the recommendation form. But I lack the courage to look into your eyes. Autumn, 1976.】*

"Academician He," Zi-yang's gaze drilled into the old man's eyes like an ice pick:

"Over the past fifty years, you have published four hundred papers in international journals, preaching to tens of thousands of students about 'the candid pursuit of truth.'"

"Then allow me to ask you—" Zi-yang jabbed his index finger into the center of the torn paper:

"You used dozens of erasers on this sheet! You rubbed it raw, you wore holes through it!"

"You erased Chen Han-song's name, you erased the truth of your theft!"

"But can you erase the blood Chen Han-song spilled when forty tons of granite crushed his ribs at the age of twenty-one in the Seventh Adit?!"

"Can you erase the coward who has hidden in your chest like a sewer rat for fifty years, terrified to face the light?!"

Every single interrogation tore into He Jing-yao's heart like a massive iron maul!

"Stop... please stop..."

He Jing-yao's lips turned purple, his knees shaking uncontrollably as he slid down the wooden front of the lectern.

"Professor!" His disciples panicked, rushing to hold him up.

"GET AWAY FROM ME!!"

He Jing-yao suddenly unleashed a feral, agonizing roar!

He shoved his disciples away with frantic violence, collapsing onto the podium on his knees. Clasping the battered dictionary in both arms, he buried his forehead directly into the shredded, eraser-torn letter!

"Han-song... Han-song, I'm sorry!! Ahhh————!!"

An agonizing, heartbroken wail that had been bottled up for half a century tore violently from the chest of the eighty-year-old academician!

Tears, saliva, and mucus instantly ruined the immaculate front of his tailored suit.

Before hundreds of his students and peers, this world-renowned scholar knelt upon the stage like a thief stripped of every garment of vanity, knocking his forehead frantically against the shredded paper:

"I stole it... I stole it all!!"

"I was jealous of you... I was terrified of spending my entire life swinging a pick in that dark pit... I sawed open the window bars... I burned your recommendation form in the coal boiler!!"

He Jing-yao beat his fists against his own sternum, weeping so hard he could scarcely draw breath:

"Fifty years! The day I was elected to the Academy, standing on that grand stage... I saw everyone clapping for me, but all my eyes saw was your crushed body under that stone!!"

"I was too cowardly to speak... I was terrified of losing my fame, my rank... I lived my whole life on a foundation of lies... I am a thief... I am a coward!!"

The old man's weeping echoed across the vast hall.

No one spoke.

Hundreds of impeccably dressed intellectuals and dignitaries bowed their heads, their faces pale with the shock of a devastating moral reckoning.

The truth was soaked in blood.

Yet before this bloodstained truth, all hypocritical halos and academic titles were stripped away to bare bone in a single second.

---

#### 5

Zi-yang stood at the edge of the stage, quietly watching He Jing-yao weeping upon the carpet.

Strangely enough, there was no thrill of vengeance in the boy's chest, nor any haughty arrogance.

He felt only a profound, chilling sorrow and an unyielding clarity.

A man could deceive the world with lies for fifty years, fooling courts and history books alike, but he could never deceive the heart within his own ribs that rotted in secret agony.

Zi-yang stepped forward, drew a clean white handkerchief from his pocket, and laid it gently across He Jing-yao's trembling hand.

Then, turning on his heel, the boy marched with measured, steady strides down the mud-stained crimson runner toward the exit.

No one barred his path.

Hundreds of guests parted silently to either side, opening a wide, solemn corridor for this seventeen-year-old mailman with his green leather satchel.

……

Four o'clock in the afternoon.

The rain over Jiufen had ceased entirely. Golden afternoon sunlight pierced the retreating mists, washing the blue-stone stairs in warm amber.

Beneath the ancient camphor tree, the doors of the "Overdue Post Office" stood wide open.

Old Gu sat behind the long red cypress table, holding his bowl of ginger tea, the black cat curled at his slippers, dozing peacefully in the sunbeams slanting across the porch.

Zi-yang stepped over the wooden threshold.

His hoodie had dried; the green leather mailbag across his shoulder was entirely empty, exuding the clean scent of cured hide.

"You're back?" Old Gu looked up.

"I'm back." Zi-yang walked to the table, unbuckling the bag and laying it flat upon the wood.

"Did he sign for it?"

"He signed for it." Zi-yang pulled up a stool and sat down, resting his elbows on his knees, watching the Pacific glittering through the window:

"Old Gu... He Jing-yao confessed everything before hundreds of people. He announced his resignation from every post and title, and he's transferring all his assets into an education foundation for miners' children named after Chen Han-song."

Zi-yang paused, his eyes reddening slightly:

"But... Chen Han-song can never come back. He died at twenty-one... he will never know that He Jing-yao said sorry."

Old Gu reached out, resting a withered palm gently over the back of the boy's hand.

The elder's gaze was as deep and vast as the ocean that had watched this coastline for centuries:

"Zi-yang, the dead may no longer hear. But He Jing-yao's soul, after fifty years... was finally released this afternoon from the cage he forged out of his own lies."

Old Gu stood up, opened the drawer, brought out the court document, and pressed the timber seal down firmly into the second square marked "Day Two Evaluation."

*Thump.*

The cinnabar ink shone brilliantly in the sunset.

"To face oneself with honesty is never about fixing tragedies that have already come to pass."

Old Gu looked at Zi-yang's clear, unwavering eyes, murmuring gently:

"It is so that the living... no longer have to drag their souls through the muck of falsehood behind a hypocrite's mask."

"See you tomorrow, Zi-yang."

The boy rose to his feet, bowed deeply toward the old postmaster, and a bright, truly unburdened smile touched his face:

"Yes. Old Postmaster Gu. See you tomorrow."

> *殘缺的真實，永遠勝過虛偽的光鮮。*

### 💡 Story Encyclopedia: Postal Regulations on Undeliverable Mail and Vintage Stationery

* **Postal Handling of "Undeliverable Mail"**:
  Under postal statutes, correspondence lacking return addresses, bearing nonexistent destinations, or rendered undeliverable due to untraceable recipients is classified as "Undeliverable Mail." Post offices operate specialized review committees empowered under legal supervision to unseal envelopes solely to seek contact clues. Letters remaining unclaimed past statutory holding periods are permanently accessioned into postal archives or decommissioned in accordance with statutory protocols.
* **Vintage Erasers and Nostalgic Stationery**:
  During the 1960s and 70s, Taiwanese schoolchildren commonly used rectangular white erasers molded from natural rubber and calcium carbonate filler. In that era of material scarcity, carving secret messages, initials, or heartfelt apologies into the side of an eraser with a penknife was a unique, humble medium for shy youths to express unspoken remorse.
