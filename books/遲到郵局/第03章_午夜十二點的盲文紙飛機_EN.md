# 《遲到郵局》（The Overdue Post Office）

## 第一卷：修復自我之信 / Volume I: Letters of Self-Restoration
### 第 03 章：午夜十二點的盲文紙飛機 / Chapter 03: The Midnight Braille Paper Airplane

---

#### 1

After nightfall in Jinguashi, the boundary between mountain and ocean was completely obliterated by dense sea fog.

A biting, damp chill rolled down the sheer cliffs of Teapot Mountain, steeping the tiered stone village in an ethereal, deep-blue silence.

Lin Zi-yang sat on the blue-stone steps beneath the porch of the "Overdue Post Office," holding a strip of fine sandpaper, painstakingly polishing a piece of native Acacia timber he had salvaged from the mountain ridge yesterday. It was a gift brought to him by Old Gu from Master Zhang's blacksmith shop—the old smith had offered to teach the boy how to cut traditional mortise-and-tenon joints to assemble a new forge bench.

Blisters had worn open across the boy’s palms, yet he did not pause. The rough, earthy bite of wood dust and grit against his skin brought him, without his conscious knowing, an anchor of peace he had never experienced before.

Suddenly, from within the house, the resonant chiming of an antique pendulum clock broke the stillness.

*CLANG——CLANG——CLANG——*

The Japanese-era Seikosha weight-driven clock in the sitting hall struck twelve measured times into the night air.

Almost at the exact fraction of a second the twelfth chime faded into the draft—

*Pat.*

A sound as faint and brittle as a dried autumn leaf striking pavement landed squarely atop the cylindrical cast-iron pillar postbox before the porch.

Zi-yang paused his sanding, turning his head in alert curiosity.

Under the dim, amber cone of the porch's tungsten bulb, a solitary white object rested peacefully atop the dark-green domed cap of the Meiji-34 postbox.

It was a paper airplane.

It was not folded from an ordinary lined student notebook, but crafted from a heavy, crisp sheet of honey-colored archival stock with a delicate honeycomb texture. Its wings were creased with razor-sharp symmetry, and across the broad expanse of both wings rose hundreds of tiny, needle-punched bumps arranged in strict rows—**standard six-dot Braille lettering**!

As a gust of breeze ruffled its edges, the paper airplane trembled softly against the iron cap, releasing a pungent, ancient fragrance of aged camphor oil, pine shavings, and dried sea brine.

"It has arrived again."

Old Gu’s voice floated softly from the shadows of the entrance.

Carrying a storm-proof kerosene lantern in one hand and leaning upon his bamboo walking stick with the other, the old postmaster stepped over the high wooden threshold. His gaze drifted past Zi-yang’s shoulder, settling upon the Braille paper airplane perched atop the postbox, a long, weary sigh stirring in the depths of his eyes.

"Old Gu... what is this?" Zi-yang stood up, dusting wood shavings from his jeans, pointing bewilderedly at the paper dart. "Someone tossing garbage onto the postbox?"

"That is no piece of garbage, Zi-yang." Old Gu walked forward, extending a hand encased in a thin white cotton glove, lifting the paper airplane with infinite gentleness from the cold iron:

"This is a letter... that has arrived upon the roof of this postbox at precisely midnight, every single night for thirty whole years—**a Letter of a Broken Promise**."

---

#### 2

Zi-yang was stunned: "Every midnight?! For thirty years?! That's... over ten thousand days?!"

"Precisely ten thousand, nine hundred and fifty days." Old Gu looked down at the paper airplane nestled in his palm, his calloused thumb gently tracing the raised Braille dots:

"Thirty years ago, Jinguashi was home to a summer retreat camp operated by the Provincial School for the Blind and Mute. Back then, there was a twenty-year-old piano technician in town named Zhou Bing-hui."

Old Gu turned and walked back into the hall, Zi-yang hurrying close behind.

Aboard the long red cypress counter, the golden flame inside the glass chimney of the kerosene lamp danced softly, casting elongated, shifting shadows beneath the raised Braille dots.

"Zhou Bing-hui was born completely blind in both eyes, but his ears were the most exquisite instruments in the entirety of Keelung Port," Old Gu continued, pulling open a drawer to offer a brass magnifying glass to the boy. "Relying on hearing alone, he could detect a tension variance of a thousandth of a cent across any of the eighty-eight strings inside a grand piano in less than a second. Concert halls across the island kept him on waiting lists for months to tune their nine-foot performance Steinways."

"That summer, the School for the Blind brought a group of children up to Jinguashi to see the ocean. In that camp, Zhou Bing-hui met an eight-year-old girl named Ah-Yin."

Old Gu stroked the sleeping black cat on the counter, his voice as steady as mountain spring water:

"Ah-Yin was born deaf and blind. She could perceive neither light nor sound. Her world was an expanse of absolute silence and frozen void. But in the camp's music room, when Zhou Bing-hui depressed Middle C, the little girl reached out her tiny hand, pressing her palm against the walnut rim of the piano, feeling the faint acoustic resonance vibrating through the wood."

"Zhou Bing-hui held her tiny fingers, guiding them to press the keys to play the simplest children's tune—*Summer Evening Breeze by the Sea*."

"That was the very first time in that deaf-blind girl's life that she realized this world contained something called 'melody.'"

Zi-yang held his breath involuntarily, his eyes riveted to the paper airplane.

"On the night before the camp was to break," Old Gu whispered, "Ah-Yin traced tactile sign language into the palm of Zhou Bing-hui's hand. She asked: *'What does the ocean really feel like? Is it like the deep bass strings of the piano—warm, vast, and deep?'*"

"Zhou Bing-hui's eyes burned with hot tears. Into the little girl's palm, he solemnly traced out a sacred vow:"

"『Ah-Yin, wait for me this Saturday afternoon at three o'clock, before the sun dips below the ridge. I will bring a folding upright piano to the sea-eroded rock terrace at Badouzi. I will play the piano for you, you will dip your bare hands into the tide, and I will take you to see the real ocean.』"

"The little girl was so overjoyed she could not sleep all night. She tore a sheet of tactile sheet music from her only Braille hymnbook, folded it into this paper airplane, and placed it into Zhou Bing-hui's hands as their sacred covenant."

Zi-yang leaned forward urgently: "And then? When Saturday came, did Zhou Bing-hui go?"

Old Gu did not answer immediately. His gaze wandered out toward the pitch-black valley beyond the window. Only after a protracted silence did a bitter, heavy sigh escape his lips:

"On Saturday morning, the premier Taipei Municipal Symphony Orchestra dispatched an executive limousine straight up the winding road to Jinguashi."

"That very evening at the National Concert Hall, an internationally renowned maestro was to conduct Tchaikovsky's Piano Concerto No. 1. During the dress rehearsal, the tuning pin block on the soloist's Steinway slipped irreversibly. Every master technician in Taipei was utterly helpless."

"The orchestra offered an astronomical emergency tuning fee of one hundred thousand New Taiwan Dollars—in 1986, that was enough to purchase a two-story apartment in downtown Keelung outright. Moreover, the general director promised that if he stabilized the piano, Zhou Bing-hui would be appointed Chief Resident Tuning Consultant to the National Concert Hall for life."

"Fame, wealth, a permanent golden key to the highest pantheon of music... all of it crashed down upon the head of an impoverished, blind technician on that Saturday morning."

Old Gu closed his eyes:

"Zhou Bing-hui wavered. He thought to himself: *'Ah-Yin is just an eight-year-old child; she cannot tell Saturday from Sunday. I will go to Taipei, tune the piano, pocket the fortune, and on Sunday I will take her to the coast. It is only a child's promise—what does it matter if I am one day late?'*"

Zi-yang's heart seized violently, his palm slamming down against the edge of the wood: "And then?! Did he go on Sunday?!"

"He could never go again."

Old Gu's voice was as heavy as lead sinking into the abyss:

"At two-fifteen that Saturday afternoon, an unseasonal rogue wave triggered by an offshore tropical depression slammed into the coastline of Badouzi."

"That eight-year-old deaf-blind girl had walked out alone, cradling a small biscuit tin filled with sea snails, sitting upon the basalt shelf, waiting quietly for her piano man."

"At two-forty in the afternoon, a six-meter wall of white water swept across the rocks, dragging the little girl down into the cold, bottomless undercurrents of the Pacific."

"When the rescue divers finally found her three days later... her tiny fists were still clenched tight around that tin of snails."

---

#### 3

"Fuck..."

Zi-yang slammed his fist violently onto the heavy red cypress table, his eyes blazing scarlet with sudden, scalding tears, his knuckles bleaching white under the force of the blow.

The kerosene lamp rattled violently; the black cat sprang from the felt mat with a hissed warning, vanishing into the shadows.

The seventeen-year-old boy's chest heaved like a trapped, wounded beast, his voice vibrating with raw, trembling fury:

"An adult's career... a fucking classical concert... is that more important than a child's promise?! If he gave his word, why didn't he go?! WHY DIDN'T HE GO?!"

The young man's roar reverberated through the rafters of the old post office, shaking dust from the wooden ceilings.

Old Gu looked serenely at the shaking, weeping youth. There was no rebuke in the elder's eyes, only a vast, aching compassion that had gazed upon human weakness for a hundred years:

"Zi-yang. One who promises lightly will invariably prove devoid of faith."

Old Gu laid his withered hand gently across the nose of the paper airplane:

"In this fleeting world of ours, how many adults treat the promises made to the weak, to children, to those closest to them, like scraps of scrap paper whose payment dates can be postponed at whim?"

"We foolishly tell ourselves: once I have made my fortune, once I have secured my rank, once I have finished performing this grand, important drama before the world, it will not be too late to make amends for those tiny promises."

"Yet fate... never waits for anyone upon that shore."

"The night Zhou Bing-hui learned of the tragedy, he took a pair of heavy wire cutters and severed every single tuning wrench and lever he owned. He resigned from every post at the National Concert Hall, returned to an abandoned, wind-battered boat shack on the coast of Badouzi, and imprisoned himself there for thirty whole years."

Old Gu slipped the Braille paper airplane into a reinforced kraft envelope and laid it solemnly before Zi-yang:

"For thirty years, every midnight at twelve o'clock, he relies on the tapping of his cane to feel his way across two kilometers of dark mountain road, tossing a freshly folded Braille plane onto our postbox."

"Inside, he writes the exact same line over and over: *'Ah-Yin, I am waiting by the sea. Won't you come and listen to me play?'*"

"Zi-yang."

Old Gu looked into the boy's reddened eyes:

"Take your green mailbag."

"Go to that abandoned boat shack by the surf of Badouzi."

"And bring this blind man, trapped in the abyss of his own guilt for thirty years—"

"To that sea-eroded rock terrace where he was supposed to stand three decades ago."

……

One o'clock in the morning.

The coastline beneath Wangyou Valley in Badouzi, Keelung.

There were no tourists, no streetlights; only the cold, swept beam of the Keelung Islet lighthouse lashing across the dark swells every six seconds. The gale blew hard, carrying the briny sting of salt spray, crashing violently against the jagged teeth of black volcanic basalt along the shore.

Tucked into a recess of the seawall stood a derelict shack patched together from discarded fishing nets, corrugated tin, and rotting ship timbers.

The door had no lock, hung only with a length of green-crusted iron chain.

Zi-yang pushed the rusted tin panel open.

There was no modern furniture inside; the thunder of the surf echoed through the hollow walls. In the center of the damp, salt-encrusted concrete floor stood an ancient upright piano whose wooden casing had been completely removed, leaving its cast-iron harp and bare steel strings fully exposed to the ocean air.

A gaunt, shriveled old man in a ragged navy work jacket, his eyes sunken and blinded into two terrifying white scars, knelt silently before the naked keyboard.

His ten fingers were as skeletal as dry twigs, his knuckles deformed by age, yet the moment those hands rested upon the yellowed, chipped ivory keys—

An indescribable, statue-like solemnity seemed to command the drafty shack.

*Ding——dong——lang——*

A cascade of ethereal, crystalline notes, pure as moonlight rippling across deep ocean swells, unfurled from the exposed steel strings.

It was *Summer Evening Breeze by the Sea*.

As he played, the old man's withered lips twitched violently, his raspy, broken voice murmuring over and over into the pitch-black void:

"Ah-Yin... the tide is low... the seawater isn't cold anymore..."

"Uncle Zhou tuned the piano... listen... the tension on Middle C is exactly sixty-eight kilograms... not sharp at all..."

"Come touch the wood, won't you... Uncle Zhou will take you to see the sea... Uncle Zhou didn't lie to you... I really didn't lie to you..."

Two tracks of dark, cloudy tears seeped from the hollow depths of the elder's blind sockets, splashing onto the yellowed keys, dotting the ivory with wet stains.

---

#### 4

Zi-yang stood frozen by the threshold, his fingers white-knuckled around the leather strap of the mailbag.

The ocean wind tore through the rusted tin slats, stinging the boy's eyes until they burned.

Looking upon this blind technician who had imprisoned his own soul within an earthly purgatory for thirty years, the boy's mind flashed with searing memories of his own reckless seventeen years—

He remembered promising his mother he would help clean the bowls at her night market stall for her birthday, only to ditch her when his friends called, pulling an all-nighter at a gaming lounge while his mother slipped on the wet pavement packing up alone, throwing her back out;

He remembered promising his middle school homeroom teacher—the only mentor who had ever treated him with warmth—that he would turn in his homework and pass the entrance exam for a public trade school, only to laugh it off, turning his back on every promise to race tuned bikes in the dark...

"We always tell ourselves... that breaking a tiny promise is no big deal..."

Zi-yang whispered to himself, his vision blurring behind a curtain of salt and tears.

The youth stepped forward, his sneakers grinding through sea sand and crushed barnacles, halting beside the exposed skeleton of the piano.

The music stopped dead.

The old blind man's hands seized midair, his clouded, scarred sockets snapping with uncanny precision toward the boy's presence:

"Who is there? Is it... a passing mailman?"

The elder's nostrils flared; he caught the unmistakable aroma of pine resin and old camphor oil radiating from the weathered leather satchel:

"Is that you, Brother Gu? Brother Gu... after thirty years... did the post office receive Ah-Yin's reply?"

Zi-yang drew a long, ragged breath, reaching down to unfasten the cold brass buckle of the green bag.

He drew forth the heavy kraft envelope holding the Braille paper airplane. Kneeling on one knee upon the grit-covered floor, he used both palms to lay the paper airplane with infinite care into the old man's trembling, ice-cold hands.

"Master Zhou."

Zi-yang spoke, his voice hoarse and trembling with a sob, yet each word fell as steady as coastal granite:

"Ah-Yin did not write back."

The old blind man's frame shuddered violently, his fingers clutching the paper airplane as if his heart had been hollowed out, his spine collapsing toward the keys.

"However—" Zi-yang reached out with both hands, firmly supporting the old man's skeletal shoulders:

"The promise on this paper airplane... Ah-Yin has waited for it for thirty whole years."

Zi-yang lifted his chin, his gaze cutting through the torn roof toward the raging black breakers of the Pacific outside:

"Tonight, there is no concert. There is no fame, no fortune, no hundred-thousand-dollar fee."

"The rock terrace at Badouzi is still there."

"Master Zhou, stand up."

"Take your tuning fork, and come with me."

"Tonight—we are going to keep our word."

---

#### 5

Two-fifteen in the morning.

The sea-eroded rock terrace of Badouzi.

The massive black basalt shelf where the little girl had been swallowed by the rogue wave three decades ago was fully exposed by the low spring tide. Waves crashed into the fissures of the rock, sending up deep, thunderous booms like subterranean artillery.

The gale whipped violently against Zi-yang’s sweatshirt.

Bent double, the boy strained with every ounce of muscle in his seventeen-year-old body, pulling a borrowed handcart carrying the eighty-kilogram cast-iron piano frame across the slick, barnacle-encrusted volcanic rock, inch by inch, hauling it to the very center of the shelf, less than five meters from the crashing surf!

The rough hemp ropes tore raw, bloody grooves across his palms, his blood mingling with sea spray and dripping onto the black stones, but his jaw remained clamped tight, not a single whimper escaping his lips.

Leaning on a polished bamboo cane, guided by Zi-yang’s steady shoulder, the blind elder shuffled out onto the freezing basalt.

Salt spray lashed the old man's face, cold as needles.

"Are we there... child?" the elder’s voice trembled, his sightless eyes facing the boundless, roaring black ocean. "Is this... where Ah-Yin sat?"

"This is the place." Zi-yang pulled a wooden crate before the piano, helping the elder to sit:

"Master Zhou. Play."

"Ah-Yin is out there in this water."

"She has listened to the crashing waves for thirty years. Today... she deserves to hear her piano."

The old blind technician lifted his trembling hands.

When those withered fingers touched the freezing, sea-damp ivory keys once more, the thirty years of bitter torment etched into his weathered face melted away into pure, holy stillness.

The music sang out.

*Ding——dong——*

It was not a virtuoso display meant for the glittering chandeliers of a concert hall, but a hymn played with every shred of life and soul left within a dying man—*Summer Evening Breeze by the Sea*.

The melody cut through the screaming ocean gale, cut through the thundering roar of the breakers, ringing sweet, clear, and triumphant across the dark, vast expanse of the Pacific Ocean!

Zi-yang stood beside the iron harp, weeping silently in the dark.

And upon the music rack behind the keys, the Braille paper airplane spread its honey-colored wings in the wind.

Suddenly, an updraft rising from the deep ocean swells caught the paper dart!

The paper airplane took flight, soaring up from the piano into the dark sky, cutting an arc of breathtaking, sacred grace through the brilliant white beam of the Keelung Islet lighthouse—

And then, like a white seabird returning home after a voyage of thirty years, it dived silently, gracefully, into the churning, silver-crested depths of the sea.

Upon the surface of the dark water, a ring of calm, glowing foam bloomed and drifted away.

The music faded to a gentle halt.

The old man’s hands rested quietly upon the final low bass chord.

He tilted his face upward, meeting the very first ray of dawn sunlight that finally pierced the ocean mist, painting the rolling swells in gold.

Two clear tears traced down through his gray stubble, and upon his face bloomed an innocent, tranquil smile he had not worn in thirty years:

"Ah-Yin..."

"Uncle Zhou... finally kept his word."

……

Seven o'clock in the morning.

Beneath the ancient camphor tree at the "Overdue Post Office."

Zi-yang pushed the timber door open, smelling of brine, sea salt, and mud.

Old Gu still sat in his rocking chair, watching the boy walk in. The elder’s eyes dropped to the youth’s bandaged, raw hands, his gaze brimming with quiet, deep warmth.

Zi-yang walked forward, laying the empty green leather mailbag flat upon the red cypress wood.

Old Gu opened the drawer, brought out the court probation paper, lifted the seal, and stamped it firmly down into the third square marked "Day Three Evaluation."

*Thump.*

The cinnabar ink blazed resolutely in the morning light.

"Zi-yang, what did you learn today?" Old Gu asked softly.

The seventeen-year-old youth straightened his spine, his eyes clear as washed crystal, speaking quietly toward the dawn:

"Keeping a promise isn't a favor we do for others."

"It is the only bone in our spine... that we can never afford to lose as we walk through this life."

### 【信差迴響 / Messenger's Echo】
> **本章品德力量：【守信（Faithfulness）】**  
> **Virtue of this Chapter: Faithfulness**
> 
> *承諾，是靈魂向天地立下的一份神聖契約。*  
> 
> *A promise is a sacred covenant forged between the soul and heaven and earth.*  
> 
> *在這個行色匆匆的世界裡，我們常常輕易許諾，又常常以「不可抗力」、「成人世界的複雜」為由，將那些答應過別人的約定隨手拋棄。*  
> *我們以為違背一個微小的約定無傷大雅，卻不知每一次失約，都在另一個人的心頭下起一場永不停歇的冰冷寒雨；也在自己的靈魂底座上，敲下一道洗不掉的裂痕。*  
> 
> *In this hurried, frantic world of ours, we give our word so easily, and discard it just as casually beneath the convenient pretexts of "unavoidable circumstances" or the "complexities of adulthood."*  
> *We foolishly assume that breaking a tiny promise causes no real harm, blind to the truth that every broken word unleashes an unceasing, freezing rain upon another's heart, while carving an indelible fracture into the very foundation of our own soul.*  
> 
> *守信之所以珍貴，正在於它往往需要我們在利益、名譽與誘惑面前，做出痛苦而堅定的割捨。*  
> *諾言或許會遲到，但只要你心中對承諾的那份敬畏與誠意未曾熄滅——*  
> *哪怕隔著三十年的漫長歲月，哪怕隔著萬頃風浪的生死深淵，只要你肯迎著風雨走向約定的終點，人性中最神聖的光輝，終將穿透漫漫長夜。*  
> 
> *The preciousness of keeping faith lies in the reality that it invariably demands painful, resolute sacrifice before the altars of profit, vanity, and temptation.*  
> *A promise may arrive overdue, but so long as the reverence and sincere devotion within your heart have not been snuffed out—*  
> *Even if separated by thirty long years, even if divided by the mortal chasm of raging seas, if you will only face the wind and rain to walk to the terminus of your word, the most sacred light of humanity will, at last, pierce the endless night.*

### 💡 Story Encyclopedia: The Braille System and the Perseid Meteor Shower

* **The Braille System and Heavy Craft Braille Paper**:
  Invented by Louis Braille in 1824, this tactile writing system utilizes a six-dot cell arrangement (two columns by three rows) whose raised permutations represent letters, phonetics, and numerical digits. Specialized heavyweight kraft paper engineered for slate-and-stylus embossing resists moisture and mechanical friction, allowing embossed tactile dots to remain crisply legible even after decades of fingertip reading.
* **Periodicity of the Perseid Meteor Shower**:
  Occurring annually between late July and mid-August, the Perseid meteor shower ranks among the Northern Hemisphere's most spectacular celestial events, originating from comet Swift-Tuttle. Meteroids plunge into Earth's upper atmosphere at extreme velocities of sixty kilometers per second, generating micro-plasma ionization trails and subtle radio-frequency crackles that keen listeners and sensitive tuners can perceive against the night sky.
