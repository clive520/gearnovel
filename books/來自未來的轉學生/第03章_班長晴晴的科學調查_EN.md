# The Transfer Student from the Future

## Chapter 3: Monitor Qingqing's Scientific Investigation

For the next three days, the Class President of Class 6-1, Lin Yunqing, did something even she found a little obsessive.

She turned Lin Weixi into a "subject to observe" list.

"Target name: Lin Weixi. Observation period: three days. Observation items are as follows—"

Qingqing spread open her loose-leaf notebook titled "Natural Science Field Notes," which was filled edge to edge with records that looked like they belonged to a detective on a case:

"Item One: Weather prediction. September 27th, correctly forecast the afternoon shower; September 28th, correctly forecast that 'the wind would shift north'; September 29th, correctly forecast that the class next door would close their windows one minute late... wait, she can even predict that?"

"Item Two: Eating habits. The dishes in her lunchbox are different every day, yet for each dish she can accurately say, 'This was shipped from such-and-such county three days ago.' That's not reasonable for a transfer student."

"Item Three: 'Excessive' concern for the environment. She picks up fallen leaves around campus and talks to dead trees. She doesn't like seeing classmates waste food, but she never scolds anyone—she just gets that look... the look of someone watching a precious thing being thrown away."

"Item Four: The bracelet on her wrist. Worn all day, never taken off. Liu-Liu detected that its signal frequency band does not belong to any known communications specification on Earth."

Qingqing put down her pen and pushed up her glasses, gazing out the window.

"And..." she said softly, "her sensitivity to 'weather' is so fine that it makes me feel... like she's seen 'runaway weather' many, many times before."

Just then, Liu-Liu popped out of the ventilation duct, a tiny shiny sliver in its mouth, nimbly leaping onto Qingqing's desk.

"Beep beep, Class President, intel report!" Liu-Liu placed the shiny sliver in front of Qingqing, "This is a tiny fragment of the bracelet's casing that I 'borrowed' from under Lin Weixi's seat—oh, don't worry, it's just a bit of paint that flaked off when she snagged the bracelet on the desk corner yesterday. I 'kept it safe' for her with my fingernail clippers."

"Liu-Liu... that's stealing." Qingqing held her forehead.

"This is called 'scientific sampling'!" Liu-Liu puffed out its little chest with pride, "You said it yourself, Class President: 'Inference without evidence is speculation; inference with evidence is science'!"

Qingqing sighed, picked up the thin sliver of silver-white paint, and examined it carefully against the light.

The cut surface of that paint chip was as smooth as if it had been laser-cut, faintly shimmering with a flowing blue luster she had never seen on any metal before.

Qingqing's professional instinct told her: this wasn't aluminum, not steel, not titanium, not any metal from her family's motorcycle repair shop.

"What on earth... is this material..." Qingqing murmured.

"Beep beep, Class President," Liu-Liu leaned in, lowering its voice, "I scanned its molecular structure... the scanner said no such substance exists on Earth. Then the scanner itself crashed for three seconds."

The paint chip in Qingqing's hand fell onto the desk with a soft plop.

At noon, inside the classroom of Class 6-1, the children chattered noisily as they ate their lunches.

The television in the corner of the classroom was broadcasting the midday news.

"...We interrupt with an international report. At the Gyirong port on the Nepal-China Tibet border, a massive mudslide triggered by a glacier collapse occurred recently. Based on the initial assessments of meteorologists and geologists, a local glacier fractured at an altitude of 5,200 meters. The collapsed ice plunged down the steep mountain valley at high speed, covering a distance of over twenty kilometers in just six to seven minutes before striking the port directly..."

On the television screen were satellite images comparing before and after the disaster. The once-green valley had become a grayish-brown wasteland of ruins.

"Wow..." A-Xin bit his chopsticks, eyes wide, "A block of ice rolled down! Isn't that like a disaster movie?"

"Block of ice, what do you mean block of ice!" Old Wu, his mouth stuffed with rice, replied indistinctly, "That's a glacier! Didn't the news say how big that ice was? Bigger than our classroom!"

"No, bigger than the playground!" A-Xin added.

The children chattered about the news, each trying to outdo the other, while Lin Weixi, sitting by the window, quietly lowered her head and took no part.

Qingqing didn't miss that.

She noticed that the hand holding Lin Weixi's chopsticks trembled slightly.

"...And there's more." Lin Weixi said in a barely audible voice.

"Huh? Weixi, what did you say?" Lin Yunyun, sitting in front of her, turned around to ask.

"No... nothing." Lin Weixi shook her head, forcing a faint smile, "I was just saying... other countries are flooding too."

The television news just happened to switch to the next segment:

"...Meanwhile, affected by Typhoon Shader, multiple regions in southern China have seen record-breaking torrential rain for days. Several villages and towns in Putian, Fujian, recorded daily rainfall exceeding 500 millimeters—comparable to a full year of rainfall for many northern cities. Local residents say, 'We haven't seen flooding this bad in twenty years.' Over a hundred houses collapsed, with many people trapped or missing..."

"Oh my, 500 millimeters..." Qingqing silently did the math, "That's several months of rainfall for us, all dumped in a single day."

"It didn't use to be like this." Lin Weixi suddenly spoke, her voice soft yet startlingly clear in the sudden silence of the classroom, "It didn't use to rain a whole year's worth in one day, or have so many places breaking down at the same time."

"Weixi, how do you know what it used to be like?" A-Xin asked curiously.

Lin Weixi froze for a moment, as if she had let something slip, then lowered her head and moved her gaze back to her lunchbox: "I... I just feel like the weather has been getting weirder and weirder."

"Right! The news keeps talking about this 'Super El Niño,'" A-Xin tapped the desk with his finger, "It says it's the strongest El Niño in seventy years, and the whole world is either flooding or burning, and even ocean temperatures hit record highs!"

"Yeah, my dad was talking about it the other day too," Old Wu nodded, "He said the TV reported that global average sea temperatures broke records, and even a '1.5-degree threshold' was about to be breached."

"1.5 degrees?" A-Xin blinked, "What's that? A test score?"

"It's the Earth's 'fever temperature.'" Lin Weixi put down her chopsticks and said softly, "Scientists say that once the Earth's average temperature rises more than 1.5 degrees above what it was a hundred-plus years ago, a lot of disasters will..." she paused, as if choosing her words carefully, "will become ordinary things."

The classroom fell silent for two seconds.

"...You say that like you've seen it yourself." A-Xin joked.

Lin Weixi didn't answer.

She just lowered her head and looked at the carefully washed orange in her lunchbox.

Qingqing, sitting up front, saw Lin Weixi's expression reflected in the window glass.

That expression held none of the bewilderment or indifference an eleven-year-old should have when hearing weather news.

It was the calm grief of someone who had been through these things before.

After school that afternoon.

Qingqing didn't go home. She slung her backpack over her shoulder and, keeping close to cover, followed a figure across two streets to the small community park near the school.

Lin Weixi sat alone under an old banyan tree in the corner of the park.

That banyan tree, for reasons unknown, had lost half its leaves; its bark was covered in dry, cracked lines, looking barely alive.

Lin Weixi reached out her right hand and gently stroked the tree's parched, cracked bark.

"It hurts, doesn't it." she said softly, "The roots can't drink water, the leaves can't stand the sun, even the insects won't come to keep you company... I know that feeling."

Qingqing, hidden behind the bushes at the park entrance, felt a strange sensation stir in her heart upon hearing those words.

"In our place..." Lin Weixi continued in a low voice, as gentle as if comforting an injured friend, "there are also many trees, just like you. First their leaves wither one by one, then their branches dry out and crack, and finally..." she paused, "finally, they never wake up again."

The setting sun fell on Lin Weixi's profile. Her eyes shimmered with a thin, crystalline layer of tears.

"I often think," Lin Weixi's voice trembled slightly, "if only we could have done it a little earlier... if only, back then, more people had been willing to reach out..."

"Would we have saved one more tree? One more river? One more..."

She didn't finish the sentence.

Hidden behind the bushes, Qingqing's heart pounded wildly.

That Lin Weixi who was gentle, quiet, and always smiling in class.

At this moment, she was speaking to a withered tree with reddened eyes, saying things no child should say.

Qingqing thought back to the port on the midday news, razed to rubble by a glacial mudslide in seven minutes.

To the flood that dumped a year's worth of rain in a single day.

To the calm, sorrowful eyes Lin Weixi had when she watched the news.

"...This isn't a misperception." Qingqing clenched her fist, making up her mind, "All of these things really are connected."

She took a deep breath, stepped out from behind the bushes, and walked toward Lin Weixi.

In the setting sun, the shadows of the two girls overlapped for the first time.

"Lin Weixi," Qingqing's voice carried a hint of nervousness, "can you... tell me just who you really are?"

Lin Weixi looked up and saw Qingqing standing in the sunset. She was taken aback for a moment.

Her tears weren't dry yet, but a bitter yet gentle smile rose to the corner of her mouth.

"You..." Lin Weixi said softly, "really are very perceptive, Class President."
