# A Class of Feathered Troubles

## Chapter 2: The Gray Heartbeat in the Grass

The rear gate of Luyang Elementary School connected to an expanse of undeveloped, derelict orchards and windbreak woods.

Several dozen century-old banyans, their trunk diameters exceeding a meter, held court there. Over half a century, these colossal green antiques had sprawled their limbs with wild abandon, their dense crowns unfurling like dark-green parasols that interlaced and overlapped, splintering the blinding golden morning rays of Southern Taiwan into fragile fragments. Hundreds of aerial roots hung vertically from midair like the hoary, tangled beards of ancient elders, driving deep into the damp, decaying leaf litter. Over time, they had sculpted natural wooden arches and a labyrinth of rugged, undulating dirt paths across the ground.

The air hung perpetually heavy with the rich, pungent scent of humus, mingled with the sweet, fermented fragrance of rotting wild mangoes that had fallen to earth and the chilly, damp stench of unevaporated morning dew.

For more than ninety percent of the school's students, this forested dirt trail was a forbidding, perilous no-man's-land—palm-sized black-and-yellow golden orb-weaver spiders spun broad webs among the branches, venomous cobras glided like ghosts through the leaf litter, and stray dogs occasionally ventured down from the rear mountain military encampment.

Yet for Lin Jingtang of Class 6-1, this was his sacred, inviolable kingdom.

Taking this path not only shaved ten minutes off circumventing the main thoroughfare, but more crucially, it allowed him to flawlessly bypass Director Zhou Yan, who stood sentinel at the school gates brandishing his metal disciplinary binder, sweeping the grounds with radar-like precision.

At seven-fifteen in the morning, A-Tang was idly chewing on the sweet, tender shoot of a cogon grass stalk newly plucked from the edge of a paddy dike.

His stride was airy and rambling, his canvas schoolbag slung carelessly over his right shoulder, both hands plunged deep into the pockets of his uniform shorts, the toe of his sneaker casually kicking dried, shriveled ear-pod wattle seedpods along the path. The pods tumbled across the dirt, giving off crisp, rhythmic clicking sounds: *clack, clack*.

A-Tang's calves felt brisk ripples of chill in the early morning breeze.

Outsiders saw only the lawless, tyrannical swagger Lin Jingtang exhibited at school, seeing only how he led a pack of rascal boys in raising havoc like an inexhaustible little maniac. Yet nobody knew that inside the old-fashioned brick house A-Tang called home, the air for the vast majority of days was as deathly silent as a desolate tomb.

His father, Lin Chaodong, was a long-haul heavy gravel truck driver. That 35-ton old Renault truck—its cab emblazoned with garish flame decals—was his father's true home. Out of any given month, his father spent at least twenty-five days rumbling through the towering, treacherous peaks of the Suhua or South-Link Highways. On the rare occasions he returned home steeped in the choking stench of diesel exhaust and betel nut residue, it was merely to crash into a dead slumber for twelve straight hours, leaving behind a crumpled wad of thousand-dollar bills and a few cans of instant mixed-congee on the coffee table before vanishing once more into the black night to the earth-shattering roar of an engine.

In that house, no one ever reminded him to tie his shoelaces before stepping out the door; no one ever inspected the stroke-order exercises in his student handbook; and certainly no one was there to apply soothing, gentle antiseptic whenever he skinned his knees in a brawl.

A-Tang had grown accustomed to this vast emptiness. Yet sometimes, that silence weighed too heavily—as suffocating as a waterlogged sponge bearing down upon his chest, strangling the breath in his lungs.

And so, upon arriving at school, he felt compelled to holler, to sprint with all his might, to kick desks until the walls rattled. He hungered for sound—be it the startled shriek of a classmate, the apoplectic roar of an enraged teacher, or the near-riotous euphoria of the entire class in uproar. Only within that ear-splitting tempest of noise could he genuinely feel that he was alive, that he was not merely an abandoned, shredded tire dumped by the fringes of a gravel quarry, claimed by no one.

*Snap.*

Just as A-Tang sent a fifth seedpod flying, the banyan branches overhead suddenly shuddered violently.

A scatter of dark-green leaves fluttered down, spinning as they brushed against his unruly black hair.

Immediately following, from a hollow beneath a tree root scarcely ten paces ahead, came an extremely faint, frantic rustling, like some microscopic, sharp object scraping desperately against the brittle bed of dead leaves.

A-Tang's footsteps froze dead in their tracks.

The primal instincts honed from years of running wild through mountain thickets caused him to drop into a crouch instantly, his entire frame melting noiselessly behind a waist-high clump of wild ginger blossoms.

Peering through the narrow gaps between the pale-yellow petals, he beheld a tableau of brutal, bloody predation.

In a shadow cast by the intertwined, knotted aerial roots of the ancient banyan crouched an immense black cat of terrifying proportions.

That was the legendary sovereign of Luyang Elementary's back mountain—"Black Charcoal."

Black Charcoal was midnight-black from tip to tail without a single stray hair, his sleek coat glinting with a hard, oily sheen in the faint morning light. Rumor had it that this feral beast had dominated the rear mountain for four solid years, surviving countless vicious clashes with stray dogs and monitor lizards. Half of his left ear had been torn away, and a deep scar slashed down the corner of his left eye, lending his blunt feline face an air of sinister savagery. He sustained himself on fat streamside rats and raided cafeteria scraps, fiercely aggressive by nature—even the three-legged golden retriever at the security shack reflexively tucked its tail whenever he prowled atop the boundary wall.

At this precise moment, Black Charcoal's thick, whip-like black tail was sweeping slowly and menacingly from side to side against the damp earth.

His amber eyes were narrowed to slits, his pupils shrinking into two razor-thin black lines in the dim glow, locked unwaveringly onto the base of a withered patch of wild mint three paces ahead.

Sheltered beneath the meager dry leaves of that wild mint clump huddle a miserable, dust-gray scrap of life.

It was a fledgling sparrow.

It was tiny beyond belief—scarcely the size of two glass marbles A-Tang used for games clutched together. Its contour feathers were evidently far from fully grown, with sparse, pale-yellow down clinging haphazardly to its skin, exposing large patches of pink, tender flesh veined with delicate crimson capillaries.

Most fatal of all was its right wing—that tiny wing dangled outward at an unnatural, limp angle, the fragile quill shaft bent sharply at the joint, caked with thick, dark-red blood and black grit that glued half its feathered body firmly to the muddy earth. Clearly, it was an unfortunate soul that had plummeted from its ancestral nest high up in the banyan canopy during the sudden mountain gale that morning.

And the spot where it landed, by cruel misfortune, was dead center upon this merciless hunter's dining plate.

The fledgeling evidently perceived the suffocating shadow of death looming over it.

Its solitary sound left wing flapped frantically against the muddy water, its two thread-thin black claws kicking fruitlessly at the fallen leaves in a desperate bid to drag its heavy body deeper into the cracks of the earth. Its tender beak, edged in pale yellow, was parted wide, its throat issuing a series of faint, agonizing chirps that bordered on inaudible:

*“Cheep... cheep...”*

The sound was far too frail—as delicate as the tremor of a spiderweb brushed by the dawn breeze, easily swallowed by the plop of a single morning dewdrop. Yet to Black Charcoal's ears, it was without doubt the sweetest, most enticing prelude to a feast.

Black Charcoal lowered his belly further still, his four burly paws digging deep into the soil, the muscles of his hindquarters coiling taut like a drawn longbow—

He was about to strike.

In the laws of the natural world, this was an ordinary, everyday drama of natural selection. Every dawn in this forest, countless fledgelings plummeted, countless grubs were torn apart, and countless wild cats filled their bellies. Such was the mechanism of the world: cold, precise, and utterly devoid of mercy.

Under normal circumstances, A-Tang would likely have whistled, watched the bloody spectacle unfold, and then turned on his heel to stroll toward the classroom like a detached bystander.

Yet, an instant before Black Charcoal sprang, A-Tang's gaze collided unwittingly with the fledgling's eyes.

It was a tiny black bead of an eye, almost too minuscule to see.

Amidst the despair of impending darkness, that tiny eye harbored no surrender—only a faint yet desperate terror and defiance. It could scarcely stand, its wing was shattered and bleeding, yet it held its bare, scrawny head stubbornly aloft, using that insignificant little beak to hiss a pathetic, defiant warning toward the cat that loomed like a colossal behemoth.

At that fleeting second, a nerve buried deep within A-Tang's chest felt as though struck by a sledgehammer.

For reasons he could not comprehend, he saw a familiar reflection in that mud-caked, fractured, dying little bird.

The terror of facing the pitch-black night alone in an echoing, empty house; the stubborn yearning to squeeze out a shred of noise to prove one's existence even after being discarded in the dirt by the whole wide world.

"Get the hell away from it, Black Charcoal—!!"

A roar loud enough to shatter the forest erupted without warning from behind the wild ginger flowers!

Before A-Tang's brain could register a single rational thought, his body had already launched forward like an artillery shell tearing from a cannon's barrel!

Wielding his heavy canvas schoolbag packed with thick textbooks in both hands, the veins along his arms bulging like coiling dragons, the boy channeled the sheer momentum of his sprint to swing the bag like a lumberjack's battleaxe, slamming it furiously toward the black spine of the leaping cat!

*“WHOOSH—CRASH!”*

The heavy canvas sliced through the air, letting out an ear-splitting howl of displaced wind.

Black Charcoal had never anticipated that a crazed human would suddenly erupt along this secluded woodland trail. He attempted to twist his body midair, but the overwhelming momentum left him no avenue of escape!

"BAM—!!"

The bag struck Black Charcoal squarely in the lower back.

The cat let out a bloodcurdling shriek midair: *“MEEE-OWWW—!!”*

The massive black feline was hurled four or five meters through the air like a runaway iron weight, crashing heavily against a thick aerial root before tumbling ignominiously into a foul puddle of black, stagnant mud.

Yet the battle instincts of this veteran predator were terrifyingly sharp. The moment his body rolled across the muck, his curved, razor-sharp rear claws utilized the rebound to lash out viciously through the air!

*“RIP—!!”*

The sound of tearing flesh rang out with excruciating sharpness against the morning silence.

To shield the fledgling on the ground, A-Tang had slid headlong into the leaf litter like a baseball runner stealing home. His left forearm was thrown across his chest, intercepting the cat's grit-encrusted claws head-on!

Three bloody gashes, half a finger in length and deep enough to reveal raw flesh, tore across A-Tang's hand and wrist in an instant!

Blood welled up like a breached spring, swiftly dyeing his white uniform sleeve a brilliant crimson.

A searing, blistering agony struck his nerves like a white-hot branding iron. A-Tang sucked in a sharp hiss of air through clenched teeth, the veins around his temples pulsing violently.

Yet his right hand never recoiled.

His five fingers closed inward with miraculous precision amidst the mud and dead leaves, forming an unbreakable iron cage that snapped securely around the tiny, freezing, shivering creature.

"Hah... hah..."

A-Tang knelt on one knee in the dirt, chest heaving raggedly.

In the mud puddle, Black Charcoal scrambled to his feet. Coated in filth, the erstwhile sovereign looked like an apparition clawed straight from the underworld. His spine arched into an iron bridge, every black bristle bristling in furious indignation, his upper lip peeling back to bare four gleaming white, saliva-dripping fangs as a prolonged, murderous snarl rumbled from his throat:

*“HAAA—SSS—!”*

His amber feline eyes bored into A-Tang, blazing with bloodthirsty malice, his front claws tearing restlessly at the mud, poised to pounce once more to shred this human whelp who dared rob him of his meal.

Bracing one hand against the earth, A-Tang slowly rose to his feet.

Blood dripped steadily from the fingertips of his left hand onto the dirt, yet he did not grant it so much as a glance.

Snatching up half a moss-covered solid red brick with his uninjured hand, the boy's eyes flared with a feral ferocity that outmatched any wild beast. Without bothering to wipe the mud spatter from his cheek, he locked his gaze onto Black Charcoal's pupils, his lips parting in a blood-chilling grin:

"Come on, you damn cat. If you don't tear my throat out today, I'll bash your skull in with this brick, skin your black hide, and hang it on the flagpole to dry!"

Morning light fell slantwise across the youth's face.

Across that boyish visage burned a reckless, desperate madness—the look of Luyang Elementary's fiercest demon defending his territory.

Boy and cat stood locked in standoff beneath the ancient banyan for half a minute.

The morning breeze rustled through the canopy with a dry whisper.

Eventually, the murderous gleam in Black Charcoal's eyes flickered and waned before the heavy red brick raised high above the boy's head. The threatening growl in his throat subsided, and with a final, shrill screech of thwarted rage, the cunning predator turned into a streak of black lightning, vanishing into the dense thickets, never looking back.

"Phew..."

Only when the black cat was completely gone did A-Tang's taut legs give out, sending him collapsing heavily onto the damp turf.

Cold sweat had long since drenched his back.

The scratches on the back of his hand pulsed with agonizing, tearing throbs in the breeze, the skin already swelling into angry red ridges. Gritting his teeth, A-Tang clamped his mouth onto the hem of his uniform shirt, ripping away a long strip of cloth, and clumsily bound his bleeding wrist with one hand until his fingers turned pale and the hemorrhage slowed to a trickle.

Having tended to his wound, he drew a deep breath and slowly raised his right hand—which he had held cradled to his chest, scarcely daring to exert the slightest pressure—up before his eyes.

Morning sunlight slipped through the leafy canopy overhead like a warm, golden spotlight, pooling directly in the hollow of his palm.

One by one, A-Tang's fingers gently uncurled.

In the center of his palm, the tiny gray creature lay curled tight.

From A-Tang's wild sprint and scuffle, the fledgling's plumage had been rendered even more disheveled by his sweat and muddy water, looking more pathetic than ever—like a wad of gray, sodden cotton balled up by a careless child.

Its tiny frame was shuddering violently in convulsions brought on by sheer terror and hypothermia.

A-Tang lowered his head, bringing his face close, impossibly close.

In that quiet moment, the boy held his breath.

Never before had he observed a living bird from such close quarters. In his memory, sparrows were merely rows of insignificant specks perched along telephone wires, casual targets when plinking with a slingshot, or annoying crop-thieves in the paddy fields.

Yet now, this tiny creature rested squarely in the hollow of his hand.

The rough calluses of A-Tang's palm distinctly registered the sensation—it was not a sensation of physical weight. The creature was impossibly light, as if the gentlest gust of wind might scatter it into a wisp of smoke. And yet, beneath that ribcage thin as a cicada's wing, an unimaginably minuscule heart was hammering away at a frantic, desperate cadence.

*Thump-thump, thump-thump, thump-thump, thump-thump...*

As rapid as the drumbeats of summer rain pelting a tin roof.

So terribly fragile, yet so fiercely alive.

It seemed as though the entire universe's vitality and stubborn defiance had been compressed into this single, pea-sized organ.

The fledgeling seemed finally to register that the colossal flesh beneath it carried no sharp teeth or claws, but rather radiated a comforting, scorching body heat. Its tiny head, nestled deep in gray down, shifted with great difficulty.

Its tightly clamped eyelids twitched faintly, then parted, peeling open a hairline fracture of a slit.

It was an eye of pure, liquid black.

Deep, luminous, without a speck of impurity, reflecting the swaying emerald leaves overhead, reflecting the dappled golden morning light of the woods, and reflecting with crystal clarity A-Tang's mud-streaked face and the fresh scrape across his nose.

The little sparrow did not cry out, nor did it attempt to flee.

It simply stared at A-Tang. After what felt like an eternity, it seemed to expend the final drop of its strength to lean its tiny, grit-dusted beak gently, almost tenderly, against the deep lifeline carved into A-Tang's palm.

A cool, frail touch traveled up the nerve endings of his hand, sweeping through the boy's entire frame in an electric shudder.

A-Tang's throat clamped tight, his eyes stinging with an involuntary warmth.

For the past twelve years, everyone had told him: *Lin Jingtang, you're a stray mongrel, you're a walking disaster, you're good for nothing except smashing things to pieces.*

Yet now, this dying spark of life, teetering on the precipice of oblivion, had placed all its trust and survival unconditionally into the rough, calloused right hand that had just bludgeoned a wild cat.

"Hey..."

A-Tang spoke. His voice was hoarse and unfamiliar, laced with a trembling gentleness he had never known he possessed:

"Don't be scared... You're not gonna die. I'm here. That mangy cat won't touch you."

Sniffling, he wiped his eyes with the back of his uninjured right hand in awkward haste.

What was he to do now?

Rule Seven of the School Code loomed in his mind like an icy monolith: *Strictly forbidden to bring unquarantined live animals onto campus grounds.* Director Zhou Yan's hawk-like eyes patrolled the school gates every single morning. If Director Zhou caught sight of it, this crippled fledgeling wouldn't survive past morning homeroom.

Gritting his teeth, A-Tang snatched up the schoolbag he had cast aside.

He rummaged furiously inside: textbooks, snapped rubber bands, a slingshot, and half a pack of crushed instant noodles were unceremoniously dumped onto the grass. Finally, his eyes fell upon the pristine cardboard shoe box resting at the very bottom of his bag.

It was the box from a pair of cheap sneakers his father had bought him at a night market stall during his rare visit home last month.

A-Tang scooped up several sheets of clean blank paper, crumpling them repeatedly between his palms until the stiff sheets became as soft as cotton wool, carefully lining the bottom of the box. Next, he drew the folding pocketknife he used for sharpening pencils from his belt and, with meticulous focus, punched a dozen neat, pea-sized ventilation holes through the top and sides of the box.

That done, A-Tang scooped up the fledgling with both hands, his touch as reverent as if cradling fragile morning frost, and nestled it securely into the soft paper nest.

Sinking into the paper, the fledgeling curled up comfortably, its dark eyes still fixed steadily upon him.

"Listen up," A-Tang whispered through the ventilation holes before latching the lid, his lips parting once more in his familiar roguish grin. "You're under official arrest. Your code name is Little Ma. Once we're inside the school, don't you dare make a peep, no matter what you hear, got it? If you summon Director Zhou's whistle, both of us are gonna get strung up from the ventilation pipes and beaten."

The lid snapped gently into place.

A-Tang nestled the shoebox carefully into the center of his bag, zipping it only halfway to ensure air circulation.

In the distance, atop the Zhongxiao Building, the preparatory bell tolled its first melodious chime.

Dusting off his shorts and hoisting his bag in one hand, the boy drew a deep breath of the grassy morning air of Southern Taiwan, lengthened his stride, and sprinted headlong toward that roaring schoolhouse.
