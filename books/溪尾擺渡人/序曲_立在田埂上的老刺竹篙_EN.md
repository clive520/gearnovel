# Prologue: The Ancient Thorny Bamboo Pole Standing on the Ridge

The autumn wind swept across the plains on the southern bank of the Wu River, and the endless expanse of golden rice waves rustled as if caressed by a colossal, invisible hand, releasing a rich, crisp aroma of ripe grain.

Beside the playground fence of Xiwei Elementary School in Wuri District, Taichung City, a crowd of fifth and sixth graders in athletic uniforms gathered tightly around an open patch of ground.

"Whoa, it's so thick! Is this really bamboo? I can't even get both hands around it!"

"Look here—it's reddish, glossy, and so slick to the touch, like it was coated in oil."

"Teacher, what are those deep, jagged grooves all over it? Were they carved with a knife?"

The children chattered in astonishment. Resting horizontally between them upon a pair of wooden trestles was a massive thorny bamboo pole, over five meters long and as thick as a soup bowl.

This bamboo was entirely unlike any ordinary garden stake. Its entire body radiated a deep chestnut-crimson hue, steeped by decades of relentless sunlight and river water, polished as smooth as heirloom lacquered wood. Yet along its midsection and lower end lay a dense network of crisscrossing indentations. Those were no deliberate knife carvings; they were the indelible brands of time, etched by tens of thousands of violent collisions against coarse, scalding hemp ropes, jagged riverbed boulders, and furious muddy currents.

Roughly thirty centimeters from its tip, the bamboo's nodes gently arched upward in a natural, dignified curve.

"Those aren't knife marks. They are the growth rings carved by the heavens and the great river."

A clear, gentle voice rang out. A young woman with brisk short hair, wearing a canvas work apron smudged with fine soil, walked over. She was Lin Xiaotang, a hometown youth born and raised in Xiwei Village, and the founder of this agricultural-cultural program called "The Ferryman's Golden Grain."

Xiaotang knelt down, gently running her fingers over the deepest groove on the bamboo pole, her eyes filled with the quiet reverence one might reserve for an elder.

"Sister Xiaotang, what was this giant bamboo pole actually used for? We have steel flagpoles right by the playground, so why keep an old bamboo pole here?" a boy with a buzz cut asked, looking up in curiosity.

Xiaotang smiled faintly and pointed into the distance.

Following her finger, at the far horizon of the golden rice fields, stood a magnificent modern structure—the Xiwei Bridge, inaugurated in 2016. Its red-and-white steel arches soared into the sky, while vehicles darted like beetles across its smooth, expansive six-lane deck. Under a cloudless sky, driving across this bridge to the bustling avenues of downtown Wuri took a mere five minutes.

"You see that bridge? Magnificent, isn't it?" Xiaotang asked.

The children nodded in unison.

"Yet, when your grandfathers, grandmothers, and even your great-grandparents were little, there was no bridge here at all. Not a concrete bridge, not even a single steel rebar," Xiaotang's voice softened, yet fell with unmistakable clarity into the ears of every child. "Back then, Xiwei Village was severed as if by a pair of giant shears wielded by two mighty rivers—the Wu River and the Maoluo River. To the people of Taichung to the north, this place was an abandoned 'inland Penghu,' an isolated enclave surrounded entirely by water."

"Then... if there was no bridge, what did people do when they got sick and needed a doctor? What if they ran out of food and had to buy groceries?" the buzz-cut boy asked, his eyes wide.

"And how did the children get to school?" another girl with a ponytail chimed in.

"They depended on this."

Xiaotang's palm struck the heavy thorny bamboo with a solid, resounding thud.

"In the old Xiwei Village, this pole had a legendary name: the 'Wave-Calming Pole.' More than fifty years ago, before dawn broke each morning—while the whole village still slept beneath heavy fog and the roar of rushing rapids—a dozen children just like you had to shoulder their canvas backpacks and balance upon a rocking bamboo raft by the riverbank. The water was bottomlessly deep, the whitecaps fierce and unforgiving. If the raft tipped, you'd be swallowed straight into the muddy torrents of the Dadu River."

Hearing this, the listening children held their breath involuntarily; a few glanced down subconsciously at the dry, level concrete under their feet.

"In those days, there was an old man. Holding this very pole, he stood at the prow of the raft. No matter how fierce the summer flash floods raged, or how bitterly the winter gales howled, the moment a villager on the opposite bank lit a torch and cried out, 'Crossing the river!' he would push the raft into the dark water without a second thought. Stroke by stroke, punt by punt, he carried the critically ill to hospitals, ferried harvested grain and watermelons to market, and safely delivered trembling, terrified schoolboys across to the classrooms on the other shore."

Xiaotang's eyes glistened slightly as she looked out at the boundless paddies behind her, heavy with drooping golden heads of rice.

"My father, A-Shun, was the most water-fearing child in the entire village when he was little. The mere sight of surging whitecaps made him shiver; he lacked even the courage to step onto a raft. Yet it was that old ferryman, holding this pole, who took him by the hand, teaching him how to fix his eyes on the distant mountain peaks through the spray, and how to harness the river's own force to keep his footing firm."

An autumn breeze swept through, stirring the grain into shimmering golden billows. The air seemed to carry a phantom scent from decades past—the mineral tang of river silt and the crisp rush of an aged bamboo raft cleaving the surface.

"That old ferryman was called Uncle Rong. He was a ferryman officially commissioned by the Wuri Township Office, and he never left this river his entire life," Xiaotang held the end of the pole gently, turning to face this generation raised in modern convenience. "Today, we are going to step barefoot into the muddy paddies to harvest rice, and we will lash together a real bamboo raft with our own hands. But before we begin, I want to tell you the story of Uncle Rong, of my father A-Shun, and of this mighty river."

From the distant Xiwei Bridge came the faint hum of tires slicing through the wind, blending in wondrous harmony with the nearby rustle of the golden rice waves.

It was an echo of time. In the river of memory, the steel ribs of the bridge slowly dissolved, the smooth concrete dikes receded into the mist, and the roaring, churning yellow silt once again spilled across the boundless alluvial wild...

The story begins on an early spring morning more than fifty years ago, with an old village proverb: "Plant a single melon vine, and its tendrils wind through three counties."
