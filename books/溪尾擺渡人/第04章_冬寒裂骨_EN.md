# Chapter 4: Bone-Splitting Winter Chill—The Torches of Life and Death Across the Dark Night

Entering the Winter Solstice, the biting northeast monsoon swept upstream from the river mouth like bone-scraping daggers of ice, howling across the bleak, desolate flats flanking both banks of the Wu River.

This was the coldest dry season of the year. Most of the river's vast expanse lay exposed, giant chalk-grey riverbed boulders littering the shoals, while the once-tumultuous torrents dwindled into several dark, grey trenches of varying depth.

To spare the villagers the exhaustive labor and long waits of daily ferrying, Uncle Rong led the able-bodied men of the village in launching their grand annual undertaking—constructing the hundred-meter "Temporary Bamboo Bridge."

Uncle Rong directed the villagers to fell over a hundred long, rigid thorny bamboo stalks. Lashed in threes to form sturdy tripod trestles shaped like the character "人," these pilings were driven deep into the rubble at the bottom of the deep trenches. Four parallel runs of split giant moso bamboo were laid across the top to form the deck, secured tightly with heavy galvanized wire soaked in sesame oil.

This rickety, makeshift bamboo footpath—barely wide enough for a lone farmer to push a handcart—arched across the freezing water like a suspended white dragon, stitching Xiwei to Tong'ancuo on the northern bank.

Yet the river's temper had always been treacherous and unpredictable.

On a freezing night locked in the depths of the twelfth lunar month, with temperatures plunging below seven degrees Celsius, the pitch-black sky ripped open without warning. The Nantou highlands upstream were battered by a freak, violent winter deluge.

Freezing mountain torrents, laden with shattered timber and boulders from deep ravines, came roaring downstream in the dead of night.

"Rumble—boom—!"

Muffled thunders shook the earth in the dark. Within a mere half-hour, the surging mudflow descended like a ravenous beast, mercilessly tearing the hundred-meter bamboo bridge—the fruit of weeks of community toil—into splinters. Fractured bamboo and severed wires rolled with anguished groans in the rapids, swept downstream in the blink of an eye.

At 2:15 in the morning, the whole of Xiwei Village lay submerged in dead silence and bitter cold.

"Fire! There's fire on the other side!"

Sleeping in the side wing of the courtyard, Ah-Shun was startled awake by frantic pounding on the wooden door and urgent barking from the yard dog. Throwing on a thin, tattered padded cotton coat, he burst outside to find his grandfather, Uncle Shuisheng, holding a storm kerosene lantern, gazing toward the northern bank of the Wu River with a grim, pale face.

Piercing through the boundless murk and whistling north wind, a lone kerosene torch on the stone steps of the abandoned northern dock was waving frantically up and down in desperate circles!

Immediately after, carried by the freezing river wind, came faint, agonizing cries choked with frantic weeping:

"Crossing the river—! Lives are on the line—! Uncle Rong! Save us—!"

The desperate pleas fluttered intermittently in the howls of the gale, yet struck like sledgehammers against the heart of every villager.

"It's the second daughter-in-law of the Lin family in Tong'ancuo!" Uncle Shuisheng's face turned white as chalk, his voice trembling. "She's nine months pregnant... word is the baby is breached and she's been hemorrhaging for two days straight! The northern highway was blocked dead by a rockslide—the county ambulance can't get through! They need to cross the river! Only our Xiwei side has ox carts that can take the back road through Fenyuan to the hospital in Changhua!"

"B-but... the bridge is destroyed!" Ah-Shun cried out in panic. "The river is darker than ink right now, and the water is freezing enough to kill a man! Who could possibly cross?"

"He can," Uncle Shuisheng turned toward the ferry crossing, awe shimmering in his gaze.

When Uncle Shuisheng and Ah-Shun stumbled down to the dock with their lanterns, they saw a massive bamboo raft lashed to an ancient chinaberry tree by heavy hemp lines, rocking violently against the black, turbid current.

Uncle Rong was already aboard. He wore no palm cape, only a thin coarse-cloth work shirt, a hemp rescue rope tied around his waist, his lips shivering with a purplish tint. Yet his eagle eyes, illuminated by the kerosene lantern, gleamed as cold and sharp as twin drawn bayonets.

"Ah-Rong! You can't put in!" The village warden, arriving in a panic, grabbed the hawser, weeping tears of terror. "Winter mountain floods are the deadliest of all—the current is full of submerged logs and rolling ice! Going down there in pitch darkness is pure suicide!"

"Even if it's suicide, I must go," Uncle Rong's voice showed not the slightest tremor, as unyielding as the bedrock of the river. "Waving that torch in three circles is the signal for a life hanging by a thread. That's two human souls over there—a mother and a child who hasn't even seen the light of day. I, Lin Quanrong, accepted the township's appointment plaque. As long as there is breath in my lungs, this river will not sever a person's path to life!"

With that, Uncle Rong drew the small billhook from his sash and cleanly severed the final thick mooring line tethered to the shore!

"Uncle Rong! I'm going with you!"

A crisp, resolute shout from the young boy detonated abruptly in the freezing gale.

Twelve-year-old Ah-Shun lunged forward. Seizing the storm hurricane lantern from its wooden cradle with both hands, before the stunned gazes of the crowd, he leapt without a heartbeat of hesitation straight onto the violently tossing raft!

"Ah-Shun! Have you lost your mind?! Get off there!" Uncle Shuisheng nearly lost his wits in terror, screaming from the bank.

"Grandpa, the northern bank is too dark to see! Uncle Rong needs both hands to punt the pole—there's no one to hold the lantern and spot the shoals for him!" Ah-Shun bit through his lower lip, the salty tang of blood clearing his head instantly. Holding the lantern high above his head, his bare feet wedged firmly between the bamboo joints, he met Uncle Rong's astonished gaze. "Uncle, you told me before—I am this boat's pole-guard! I am not afraid anymore!"

Uncle Rong looked deeply into the young boy's eyes. He uttered not another word of dissuasion, only exhaling a heavy gust of white vapor from his nose: "Good lad! Your spine has turned to steel! Hold that lantern steady—shine it three meters ahead along the waterline. The moment you spot black foam, shout!"

"Boom—!"

The thorny bamboo Wave-Calming Pole pushed off against the shoreline boulders with all Uncle Rong's might. The raft broke away instantly like an arrow loosed from a bow, plunging headlong into the pitch-black, freezing, bottomless expanse of the ink-dark river!

Extreme, bone-piercing cold wrapped around the two of them instantly. The spray crashing into their faces was no longer the lukewarm mud of summer; it was freezing liquid ice, stinging their skin like thousands of shattered steel needles.

Around them lay absolute, impenetrable darkness, save for the swaying lantern in Ah-Shun's hands, casting a fragile, solitary halo of yellow light across the churning black torrents.

"Uncle! Submerged log to our front-left!" Ah-Shun screamed at the top of his lungs.

By the lantern's frail beam, Uncle Rong caught sight of a jagged, broken bamboo piling rolling beneath the surface. Channeling power from his hips, the veteran's pole stabbed lightning-fast into the riverbed, slipping the raft past the razor-sharp stake by inches.

Yet, just as the craft reached the absolute center of the deepest channel, the deadliest catastrophe struck!

A massive boulder, swept down from the mountain highlands by the flash flood, rolled invisibly along the riverbed and smashed directly into the two auxiliary bamboo stalks at the stern!

"Crack—snap—!"

Accompanied by a deafening, splintering roar, the two colossal thorny bamboo trunks shattered into kindling. The taut galvanized binding snapped violently, and one flank of the raft tilted sharply downward. Freezing, pitch-black water surged frantically across the deck, sending the craft spinning into a terrifying downward vortex!

"The raft is splitting! We're going under!" Ah-Shun lost his footing, crashing onto the slick bamboo skin, yet he curled his entire body to shield the frail lantern in his arms.

In that razor-thin margin between life and death, Uncle Rong performed an astonishing feat that Ah-Shun would carry to the end of his days.

The old ferryman shoved the Wave-Calming Pole into Ah-Shun's hands and bellowed: "Ah-Shun, hold the pole tight—don't move!"

Before the words had faded, Uncle Rong hurled himself over the side, plunging straight into the chest-deep, near-freezing black torrent!

"Uncle Rong—!" Ah-Shun cried out in horror.

In that bone-numbing current, Uncle Rong resembled a deity forged of hardened steel. Bracing his shoulders and spine against the splintering bamboo stalks, he took the brunt of the icy surges slamming against his chest and head. His bare feet scraped frantically across the moss-covered reefs on the riverbed, carving bloody gashes into the ice-cold gravel with every desperate step!

"Hold... hold the pole! Ah-Shun... use... use the current to shove us toward the northern bank!" Uncle Rong coughed out a ragged breath mingled with freezing spray, the veins in his neck bulging like gnarled tree roots, his gravelly roar shattering the frozen night.

Ah-Shun's face was drenched in a blur of tears and freezing river water, but he had no time to cry.

The twelve-year-old boy transformed every ounce of terror, shivering chill, and dread into raw, furious strength. Remembering the posture Uncle Rong had drilled into him countless times, he flexed his knees, thrusting the Wave-Calming Pole violently into the gravel bed below. Coordinating with the superhuman shove from Uncle Rong's flesh and bone beneath the surface, he leveraged the river's own savage momentum, shoving the sinking raft inch by inch, foot by foot, out of the clutches of the deadly whirlpool!

Fifty meters... thirty meters... ten meters!

"Caught it! We've got the line!"

Wild shouts of ecstasy erupted from the men on the northern dock. A dozen burly villagers carrying torches plunged into the shallows, grabbing the hemp hawser flung from the bow and hauling the shattered craft up onto the gravel beach with all their might!

"Uncle Rong! Uncle Rong, how are you?!"

The men scrambled into the shallows, lifting Uncle Rong—frozen stiff as iron, his lips and skin mottled a deep, suffocating purple—out of the icy water. The old man's shoulders and back were torn raw by splintered bamboo, the blood congealing into dark-red frost the moment it met the air. Yet his sunken eyes remained locked upon the thatched shelter atop the levee.

"The... the mother?" Uncle Rong's teeth clattered uncontrollably, his voice barely a breath.

"In the ox cart! Wrapped in three layers of quilts! Uncle Rong, if you had arrived fifteen minutes later, both mother and child would have been gone!" A burly farmer from Tong'ancuo dropped to his knees in the mud with a heavy thud, kowtowing deeply to Uncle Rong and Ah-Shun.

Together, the men lifted the unconscious mother onto the ox cart. The driver cracked his whip furiously, and the cart barreled down the muddy road toward Fenyuan and the hospital in Changhua.

At daybreak, a faint sliver of pale morning light appeared along the eastern horizon.

The furious winter rain finally ebbed, and a warm charcoal fire crackled within the thatched shelter on the northern bank. Ah-Shun sat by the hearth, clutching a piping bowl of hot ginger tea with both hands, watching Uncle Rong, wrapped in three heavy quilts, as the deadly pallor on his weathered face slowly gave way to a flush of life.

Just then, along the highway leading back from Changhua, a bicycle came pedaling furiously toward them, the young rider shouting with ecstatic joy the entire way:

"Born! It's a boy! A big, healthy baby boy! Both mother and child are safe! They're safe!"

That clear cry of good tidings echoed through the morning light, scattering the lingering chill across the entire breadth of the Wu River.

Tucked beneath his quilts, Uncle Rong slowly opened his eyes. Listening to the distant celebration, a deeply contented, radiant smile finally surfaced across his chapped, raw lips.

Ah-Shun turned his head, gazing across the river at the ruins of the hundred-meter bamboo bridge, now reduced to a few broken pilings, and then down at the fresh, bleeding calluses worn into his young palms.

Within his heart, the boy heard a sound with absolute clarity—the cry of life triumphing over the abyss, the burning solidarity of human beings watching over one another, and the indomitable soul that this wild, untamed river could never sweep away.
