# Chapter 1: Faint Spring Waters—Plant a Melon, Its Tendrils Sweep Three Counties

On an early spring morning in the 1960s, a heavy white mist draped over the vast alluvial flats and flooded paddies of Xiwei Village like layers of gauze drenched in cold water.

Eleven-year-old Li Shun'an crouched on the dirt ground behind his family's courtyard compound, holding a sharpened bamboo stick, intently prodding a few plump, glossy black luffa seeds.

Behind him, his grandfather, Chen Shuisheng, rolled up his trouser legs and mixed a bucket of freshly harvested compost from the pigpen into the dark silt. The soil gave off a pungent yet warm odor—deep black and viscously rich, as though it had absorbed every ounce of nourishment shed by the entire Central Mountain Range.

"Ah-Shun, don't bury the seeds too deep; leave three fingers' width of soft soil so they can breathe." Uncle Shuisheng straightened his back and wiped the sweat from his forehead with a thick-callused hand, black dirt embedded under his fingernails. He beamed down at his grandson. "This Xiwei soil is prime black mud washed down from high mountains by the Wu River—rich enough to ooze oil. So long as you put your back into it, plant a single seed and it'll repay you with an entire trellis of long, sweet melons."

Ah-Shun carefully covered the seeds with soil and patted it firm with his palm. Looking up with his small face, he asked, "Grandpa, that grand-aunt who married over from Changhua next to the school often says: 'Plant a single melon vine, and its tendrils wind through three counties.' What does that actually mean? No matter how fast a vine climbs, can it really reach all the way into Nantou?"

Uncle Shuisheng couldn't help but burst into hearty laughter, the whistling wind escaping through the gap of his missing front tooth. Dropping his bamboo spade, he took Ah-Shun by the hand and led him out through the bamboo fence, heading toward the massive earthen-and-concrete embankment that sprawled along the edge of the village.

"Silly kid, that's no joke; it's the bitter reality your old grandpa has lived through every single day of his life." Uncle Shuisheng pointed up at the grey earthen rampart that rose two stories high before them, sprawling across the plains like a slumbering dragon. "Look here. Our Xiwei Village is wedged squarely between the Wu River to the north and the Maoluo River to the south. These two mighty rivers act like a pair of shears, slicing us, Taichung, Changhua, and Nantou into a patchwork of tattered rags."

"Look at that drainage ditch," Uncle Shuisheng pointed to a shallow watercourse just outside the courtyard wall. "This side of the ditch is Wuri Township, Taichung County; take one step across and you're in Fenyuan Township, Changhua County; follow this dike less than a mile down, and the deed records it as Caotun Township, Nantou County. Back in the fourteenth year of Showa—when your grandpa was in his twenties—the Japanese, determined to keep downtown Wuri and Taichung City on the northern bank safe from flooding, built this towering Wu River Dike by force."

At this point, the smile on Uncle Shuisheng's face gradually faded. A complicated, bitter sorrow surfaced in the gaze he cast upon the great levee.

"Once the levee was finished, the high officials and townspeople on the northern bank could sleep easy on their pillows; but this dike threw our Xiwei Village mercilessly outside the walls. Whenever the summer floods arrived, the mountain torrents rolling down from Mount Hehuan couldn't drain out—they poured straight into our little 'washbasin.' We hold Taichung household registrations and belong to Changhua's daily living circle, but once the waters rise, we become abandoned, wandering ghosts that no one looks after. That's why they call us an 'island,' the 'Penghu of Taichung'!"

Ah-Shun listened only half-comprehending, but he remembered vividly: during that monstrous rainstorm last summer, roaring yellow waters had submerged the great banyan tree at the village entrance overnight. The despairing bray of his family's yellow ox as it was swept away by the raging current still woke him up in cold sweats in the dead of night. From that day on, whenever he beheld a deep, churning expanse of rolling water, his legs turned to jelly against his will.

"Uncle Shuisheng! Ah-Shun! Come quick—an official notice from the township office has arrived!"

A booming shout pierced the morning fog. The village warden, dripping with sweat, came rattling furiously down the muddy levee path on a battered iron bicycle. A roll of bright red paper was tied to his rack, and a string of crackling firecrackers hung from his handlebars.

"What glorious news warrants lighting firecrackers at the crack of dawn?" Uncle Shuisheng hollered back.

"The Wuri Township Office just approved the official charter—he's been reappointed!" The warden leapt off his bike, breathlessly wiping the mud from his face, his voice trembling with uncontainable joy. "Ah-Rong—Lin Quanrong—has been officially reappointed as our Xiwei Village's public ferryman! The township office will subsidize his monthly wage as usual, and every single soul in our village crosses the river free of charge!"

Upon hearing the announcement, farmers bent over planting seedlings throughout the surrounding paddies straightened their backs one after another, erupting into heartfelt cheers. To this cut-off community, the appointed ferryman was far more than an occupation; he was the sole lifeline that connected several hundred souls to the outside world.

"Come on, Ah-Shun! Let's go down to the ferry dock with Grandpa to see Uncle Rong!" Uncle Shuisheng seized Ah-Shun's hand, striding quickly toward the crossing on the southern bank of the Wu River.

As they traversed the floodway breach in the great dike and reached the sandbank, the morning fog happened to be cleaved in two by the rising sun. Golden-red sunlight angled across the broad river beach, illuminating more than a dozen colossal, emerald-sheened thorny bamboo stalks laid out in immaculate alignment upon the sand.

By the shore sheltered under bamboo groves, a middle-aged man of modest height, lean and sinewy like an ancient tree root, stood barefoot in the warm sand. He was dressed in a coarse cloth short shirt, faded from endless soaking in sweat and mud, his dark skin glowing like polished bronze, the veins along his forearms knotted like thick vines.

That was Lin Quanrong, whom young and old in the village respectfully addressed as "Uncle Rong."

At this moment, a smoldering heap of dry rice straw burned before Uncle Rong. Amidst thick, aromatic white smoke, an ember of dark red charcoal breathed out scorching flames. Uncle Rong held the tail end of a massive thorny bamboo stalk, as thick as a soup bowl, his expression as solemn as a blacksmith forging a sacred blade.

"Ah-Rong, congratulations! The notice from the township office is here—everyone can finally let their hearts rest easy!" Uncle Shuisheng greeted him with a smile.

Uncle Rong did not look up immediately. His eyes remained fixed upon the segment of bamboo pole sizzling as it was roasted over the fire. Beads of sweat as large as beans rolled from his brow, hitting the embers with faint, sharp hisses.

"Uncle Shuisheng, hold the congratulations for now," Uncle Rong's voice was low and gravelly, carrying the resonance of a man who had fought against the river's roar for a lifetime. "Spring has broken, the snowmelt is descending, and the river broadens day by day. Last year's old raft took a battering from the boulders; it won't survive this summer's high water. If I don't take advantage of these sunny days to craft a new raft, what am I going to use to ferry the children to school when classes start?"

With that, the muscles along Uncle Rong's arms bulged fiercely. He braced his shoulder against the midsection of the bamboo and bore down with all his might, bending the fire-softened bamboo tip downward.

"Ah-Shun, get over here!" Uncle Rong turned his head abruptly. His gaze, sharp as an eagle's strike, landed upon Ah-Shun, who stood bewildered with his trouser cuffs rolled up high.

Startled, Ah-Shun instinctively shrank behind his grandfather. He had always been somewhat intimidated by Uncle Rong's stern, unsmiling face, and he feared the damp, silt-scented gravel beach even more.

"Come here—grip this hemp rope and pin the bamboo base with your foot!" Uncle Rong's tone brooked no refusal, yet a steady warmth gleamed in the depths of his eyes. "You're a boy, eleven years old; it's time your bones hardened. Watch and learn—this is called 'fire-bending the bamboo tip.'"

Under his grandfather's encouraging nod, Ah-Shun gritted his teeth and dashed forward. The moment his bare feet struck the sand, he felt the radiant heat of the charcoal fire. Following Uncle Rong's instructions, he threw all his weight into hugging the front of the thick stalk, planting both heels firmly against the wooden stake driven into the sandpit.

While dousing the blistering bamboo nodes with a rag soaked in cold water, Uncle Rong spoke patiently to Ah-Shun: "Thorny bamboo is the hardest, most resilient bamboo of all, but it also has the stubbornest temper. If you don't roast it thoroughly over the flames and work with its grain to bend it gradually into an upturned prow, the raging current will snap it clean in two."

Under Uncle Rong's steady, powerful leverage and the quenching splash of cold water, the rigid tip of the massive thorny bamboo miraculously set into a curve, arching upward like the chest of a waterbird about to spread its wings and crest the waves.

"Do you see that?" Uncle Rong wiped a smudge of soot from his face and pointed to the curved bamboo tip. "With this upturned prow, when you shove off into the water, the raft will glide atop the crests of the waves instead of plowing headlong into a whirlpool to be swallowed whole. People are the exact same way, Ah-Shun."

Uncle Rong reached out a large, callused hand as rough as sandpaper and patted Ah-Shun's slender shoulder gently. The hand felt heavy and warm, carrying the formidable strength of decades baked under the sun and tempered by river rapids.

"Your grandpa told me that ever since last year's big flood, you've been terrified of stepping near the riverbank—that even catching a glimpse of white water sets you shivering. Is that true or not?" Uncle Rong looked straight into Ah-Shun's eyes.

Ah-Shun's face flushed crimson instantly. He lowered his head in shame, twisting the hem of his shirt with both hands, unable to utter a single word.

"Fearing the water is nothing to be ashamed of," Uncle Rong's voice gentled. He lifted his gaze toward the vast, grey river rolling relentlessly westward before them. "The power of nature is thousands, tens of thousands of times greater than man. When facing this Wu River, anyone who doesn't know how to fear it has long since washed out to sea to feed the fish."

Ah-Shun looked up in surprise at the legendary ferryman.

"But you must remember: fear or no fear, your footing cannot falter," Uncle Rong pointed toward the faint silhouette of the Dadu Mountain range on the northern shore. "This river traps us here, but it cannot sever our hearts. In a few days, school starts; no matter how scared you are, you will cross aboard Uncle's new raft to attend your classes. When we hit midstream, if terror grips you, fix your eyes on Mount Dadu. As long as the mountain stands firm, the boat will not capsize, and your spirit will not waver."

Ah-Shun looked in the direction Uncle Rong was pointing. In the distance, Mount Dadu drifted in and out of the early spring mist like a bluish-green rampart standing sentinel over the roaring northern bank.

He looked down at the fire-curved thorny bamboo beneath his feet, which would soon form the backbone of the new raft. Its outer green rind had been roasted into a burnished, deep yellow, still radiating a lingering warmth beneath his fingertips.

At that moment, within the heart of the eleven-year-old farm boy, amidst the deeply rooted dread of the great river, a tiny, unprecedented yearning quietly sprouted—the desire to one day grip that very Wave-Calming Pole.
