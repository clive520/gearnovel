# Chapter 3: Golden Grains of Autumn—Sandbar Watermelons and Champion Black-Soil Rice

Past the solar term of White Dew, the brutal and restless summer floods finally began their slow retreat.

The water level of the Wu River dropped by more than two meters, and the once-turbid expanse regained its composure, its color settling from a murky yellowish-brown into a profound slate-grey.

The receding waters left behind nature's most lavish gift along the southern shore—a layer of fine, rich alluvial black soil, nearly half a foot thick, as supple as soothing balm, blanketing the river beach and adjacent paddies. This was shale and slate weathered over millions of years in the deep heart of the Central Mountain Range, having absorbed the essence of heaven and earth, saturated with minerals and fertile organic humus.

As far as the eye could see, the plains of Xiwei Village were transformed into an ocean of boundless, resplendent gold.

Heavy ears of rice bowed their heads, each stem as sturdy as a young girl's braided hair. Whenever a breeze swept through, billows of rice surged and tumbled in layered waves, filling the air with an intoxicating sweetness—a delicious fragrance blending dry sunlight, fresh rice bran, and pure earth.

Twelve-year-old Li Shun'an stood barefoot on the cool, damp black ridge of the paddy, holding a small crescent sickle ground to a mirror shine.

"Ah-Shun, harvesting rice takes a deft wrist! Press downward and draw the blade diagonally against the base of the stalk!" Uncle Shuisheng bent at the waist, his sickle flying through the grain with crisp, rhythmic swishes. Clusters of plump rice heads fell obediently at his feet, stacking into neat rows along the ridge.

Uncle Shuisheng straightened his spine and casually plucked a few golden grains, rubbing them briskly between his thick, callused thumb and palm. The outer husks flaked away, revealing crystalline, jade-like kernels. Popping the raw rice directly into his mouth to chew, his face beamed with immense pride.

"Aromatic! Utterly aromatic!" Uncle Shuisheng narrowed his eyes into smiling slits. "Chew a few, Ah-Shun. The rice grown in our Xiwei black soil carries a delicate hint of taro sweetness. When these grains are sent to the grain bureau for evaluation, whose association harvest could ever hold a candle to the granary flushed out by our Wu River?"

Ah-Shun chewed a few raw grains just like his grandfather, and a clean, subtle sweetness immediately bloomed across his tongue. Looking out across the valleys of shimmering gold, the terror and exhaustion of crossing the river for school during the summer storms seemed entirely washed away by this bountiful fragrance.

"Uncle Shuisheng! Ah-Shun! The watermelons are dead-ripe—if we don't harvest them today, the birds will peck them to bits!"

From the direction of the sandbars on the river beach came Uncle Rong's booming shout.

Ah-Shun's eyes lit up. Setting down his sickle, he dashed alongside his grandfather toward the sprawling sandbar on the southern bank of the river.

This was a barren spit formed by coarse grit, river gravel, and thin dried silt—ordinarily unable to sustain a blade of grass. Yet in the hot, parched autumn months, it became prime real estate for growing watermelons. By day, the scalding river stones absorbed the blazing sun; by night, the chilled river wind cooled the ground with haste. This sharp diurnal temperature swing forced every drop of natural sugar into the deepest core of the melon flesh.

There, nestled amidst a dense, emerald carpet of tangled vines, rested massive round melons with dark green stripes, each as large as a washbasin.

Uncle Rong crouched beside an enormous melon, rapping his knuckles lightly against its rind.

"Thump, thump, thump!"

A remarkably crisp, deep vibration echoed through the air, reminiscent of striking a taut calfskin drum.

"Ripened to perfection—paper-thin rind, grainy crimson flesh, and crystallizing sugar!" Uncle Rong nodded with satisfaction. With a swift flick of his knife, he severed the stem and hoisted the watermelon, easily weighing over twenty catties, into his arms. "Uncle Shuisheng, this batch of sandbar melons is exceptional. Ferry them to the morning market in downtown Wuri, and they'll be snapped up in half an hour flat!"

"Magnificent indeed, but with several hundred melons and dozens of burlap sacks of dry grain from the fields, how are we going to haul it all across without a bridge?" Ah-Shun asked, eyeing the mountainous heaps of fruit and produce with furrowed brows.

"Haha! Silly boy, did you forget what we keep at our ferry dock?" Uncle Shuisheng patted the back of Ah-Shun's head, pointing toward the colossal bamboo raft moored along the bank.

This raft was no longer the nimble craft that carried schoolchildren during the summer. To accommodate the autumn harvest, Uncle Rong had lashed four additional thick bamboo trunks to each flank of the original twelve, broadening the deck to nearly three meters and more than doubling its carrying capacity.

"Ah-Shun, today you'll be my 'ballast mate'!" Uncle Rong beckoned. "We're loading grain and melons onto the raft—we call this the 'Waterborne Grain Barge'!"

Ah-Shun answered enthusiastically, rolling up his sleeves to join the line.

The grown-ups formed a human chain, passing the heavy melons hand to hand onto the deck. The watermelons were stowed neatly in the raft's central depression, cushioned with soft rice straw to prevent rolling and bruising. Along the outer flanks, they stacked heavy burlap sacks bulging with premium paddy rice.

With eighty large watermelons and six massive bags of grain aboard, the raft rode remarkably low in the water. More than half of each rounded bamboo stalk was submerged in the cool river, the current gently lapping across the tops of their bare feet.

"Cast off! Out to river!"

At Uncle Rong's prolonged cadence call, Ah-Shun swiftly untied the mooring line and picked up a short cypress paddle, planting his feet firmly at the stern.

Uncle Rong stood proudly at the bow. With a gentle shove of the thorny bamboo Wave-Calming Pole against the gravelly riverbed, the grain barge—bearing an entire year's labor and dreams—parted the glassy surface and glided smoothly toward the opposite shore.

It was twilight. The setting sun, like a pot of molten gold, dyed the placidly rolling Wu River in hues of radiant orange. The river breeze was no longer the icy, feral gale of summer; it was mild and scented with ripening paddies, caressing their faces with gentle warmth.

Holding his paddle, Ah-Shun's eyes were no longer clouded with dread. He had learned to read the subtlest shifts in the raft beneath his soles. Whenever the current tugged sideways, his feet worked instinctively with Uncle Rong's pole to counterbalance the tilt, his small frame bending like a supple willow rooted into the bamboo deck.

"Ah-Shun, the temper of water is just like the temper of a man," Uncle Rong said leisurely as he punted, glancing back at the increasingly poised young lad with the insight of a weathered sage. "When the summer floods roar, you must respect it and yield to it; you cannot pick a fight with its fury. But when the autumn chill sets in, it leaves behind the richest soil and the sweetest water from the mountains. This great river is treacherous, yes, but without it, we would have neither this overflowing granary of aromatic rice nor these honey-sweet sandbar melons."

Uncle Shuisheng sat cross-legged beside the grain sacks. Pulling a polished pocketknife from his sash, he halved a green melon with a crisp "crack."

Vibrant, crimson flesh lay revealed, glistening black seeds nestled within granular pulp, instantly releasing a cool, sweet perfume that drifted across the river.

"Here! Half for each of you—taste the sweet waters our great river washed down!" Uncle Shuisheng sliced two thick wedges, handing the first to Uncle Rong and the second to Ah-Shun.

Ah-Shun took a massive bite.

Cool, sugary nectar exploded across his tongue. That sandy, crisp sweetness chased away every trace of weariness from days of harvesting and hauling, warming him to his very core.

"Sweet! Sweeter than sugarcane water!" Ah-Shun shouted with pure delight.

Three generations of men sat atop the grain barge under the evening glow, chewing sweet sandbar watermelon as they drifted across the water. On the northern bank, carts and town merchants had already gathered to receive the cargo, while spirals of cooking smoke drifted up from the villages beyond.

"Ah-Shun," Uncle Shuisheng wiped the red juice from the corner of his mouth, watching the nearing shore thoughtfully. "You're sharp at school, and your arithmetic is better than anyone else's in the village. But you must never forget: no matter where you go in this world, or how much book-learning you master, your roots sprouted from this very black mud. A person must never forget where he came from—just like a melon vine; no matter how far its tendrils wander, its root remains right in our home soil."

Looking down at the rice nurtured by the black silt, and then at the sweet watermelon in his hands, Ah-Shun nodded with fierce determination: "Grandpa, I will remember. When I grow up, I will use what this land taught me to do great things for our home."

Carrying a deck laden with gold and sweetness, the grain barge bumped softly against the stony beach of the northern shore with a solid, satisfying thud.

Laughter and greetings from both banks mingled into the autumn evening breeze. In that golden hour, the great river was no longer an abyss separating human lives, but a shimmering artery flowing with nourishment and hope.
