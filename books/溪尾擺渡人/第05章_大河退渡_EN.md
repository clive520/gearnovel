# Chapter 5: The Great River Retires the Ferry—The Last Handwritten Ticket

The passage of time can sometimes surge even more swiftly than the turbid rapids of the Wu River.

In the blink of an eye, the hands of the clock turned to the early 1970s.

The colossal wheel of modern progress ground inexorably across this long-silent inland enclave. Dozens of miles downstream, grand modern highways and cross-river viaducts rose one after another from the soil; wide, level ribbons of asphalt stretched continuously from Fenyuan in Changhua and Caotun in Nantou, reaching right up to the thresholds of Xiwei Village.

Along the village's once-quiet dirt lanes, the guttural roar of Wild Wolf 125 motorcycle engines began to echo with increasing frequency.

The rhythms of daily life shifted quietly. Young men bought motorcycles, preferring to spend an extra twenty minutes detouring across the southern Fenyuan Bridge and taking provincial highways into downtown Taichung, rather than trudging through thick, sticky mire to wait for that fragile, rocking bamboo raft at the ferry crossing like their fathers and grandfathers had.

Even the path to school had transformed. Facilities and teaching staff at Xiwei Elementary School gradually improved, ending the need to cross the river for out-of-district schooling, while parents shuttled their children along detour roads on motorbikes. Without anyone noticing, that frantic, tense 6:00 AM "School Crossing"—once laden with the hopes of the entire community—slowly faded into a yellowed relic of history.

The stone stairs of the ferry landing were gradually swallowed by thick green moss and overgrown wild reeds. From dawn to dusk, days would pass without a single passenger stepping aboard.

On a bleak, wind-swept autumn afternoon, an official dispatch from the Wuri Township Office was delivered to the weather-beaten thatched shelter at the dock.

The black characters on the document were polite yet chillingly final: with the nationwide expansion of modern highway networks and the steep decline in ferry passenger traffic, in order to properly steward public funds, the officially subsidized ferryman system was to be formally discontinued starting this month, and the Xiwei Ferry Crossing decommissioned for good.

This meant that Uncle Rong's career of guarding the village's lifeline across the water through wind and storm—spanning more than thirty years—had reached its end today.

Today was the final day of the ferry's operation.

The setting sun cast a pale, mournful wash of gold angling beneath the ancient chinaberry tree, illuminating its coarse, furrowed bark.

Uncle Rong, now past seventy, sat alone on the smooth, worn bluestone slab. His hair was entirely snow-white, resembling the early-winter frost that settled over the Wu River. The great hands that had once wrestled roaring whirlpools were covered with age spots, his skin loose and withered. Yet his knuckles remained as thick and sinewy as seasoned timber, resting quietly against the five-meter thorny bamboo Wave-Calming Pole standing beside him.

Along the shimmering shoreline, the great raft that had accompanied him for half a lifetime was dyed a deep, somber crimson by the last rays of twilight.

There was no festive farewell ceremony, nor any silk commemorative banner bestowed by the township office. The village elders were busy weeding the final crop of the second rice season in the paddies; the young people had all gone off to work in urban factories. The great river continued its relentless westward race, carrying away whitecaps, and carrying away the silhouette of an entire era.

From his sash, Uncle Rong fished out his frayed, oiled-cloth tobacco pouch. Methodically, he rolled a pinch of rustic tobacco and struck a match.

Drawing a deep puff of bitter smoke, a pale plume dispersed slowly into the gathering dusk. The old man gazed upon the roaring waters, his eyes devoid of bitterness—only a quiet, bottomless affection.

"Toot—toot—!"

Suddenly, a crisp, sharp, piercing burst from a bamboo whistle echoed down from the flood levee path above!

Uncle Rong's entire frame jolted. His fingers holding the cigarette froze mid-air.

It was a hand-carved bamboo whistle—a gift Uncle Rong had whittled from a piece of wild thorny bamboo many years ago, given to that very boy who had held the pole for dear life amidst the raging waves!

Trembling, the old man rose to his feet. Shading his brow, he narrowed his clouded eyes to peer up at the crest of the embankment.

Silhouetted against the blood-red horizon, a tall, upright figure was striding briskly down the mossy stone steps.

It was a young man in his early twenties. He wore an immaculately washed and pressed white cotton shirt, brown leather shoes dusted with the road, and carried a heavy black faux-leather briefcase. His frame strongly resembled Uncle Shuisheng in his youth, but with a high bridge to his nose and broad shoulders, his brow radiated a calm, unyielding resolve tempered by modern science and wild waters alike.

"Ah-Shun?!" Uncle Rong's lips quivered as he breathed out a name buried deep in his heart.

"Uncle Rong! It's me! Ah-Shun has come home!"

Dropping his briefcase, Li Shun'an cleared the mossy steps three at a time. Like a boy returning to his sanctuary, he rushed before the old man, grasping Uncle Rong's cold, withered hands tightly in his own.

The once-timid child had become a top graduate from the Department of Civil Engineering at National Cheng Kung University. Having graduated first in his class, he was about to join the national engineering corps dedicated to Taiwan's major infrastructure developments!

"You silly child..." Uncle Rong's eyes reddened. His rough palm thumped heavily against Ah-Shun's broad, solid shoulder. "Your grandpa said you were in Tainan taking your professional engineer licensing exams just a couple days ago... why did you come running back today?"

"Grandpa phoned my dorm, saying the township office was closing the crossing today," Ah-Shun's eyes glistened with tears, his voice catching in his throat. "I took the train to Changhua and ran all the way home on foot. Uncle Rong, how could I not come back?"

With that, Ah-Shun carefully unbuttoned his shirt pocket and pulled out a neatly folded square of red paper.

It was no official pass issued by the government, nor a printed transit ticket. It was a "Handwritten Ferry Ticket" brushed meticulously by a twelve-year-old Ah-Shun with ink and hair-pen all those years ago. In the center of the red paper, four neat characters read: "Valid for River Crossing." In the lower corner, a small, faded red thumbprint was stamped squarely into the fiber.

"Uncle Rong, for over thirty years, every soul in our village rode your boat for free because the township footed the bill," Ah-Shun raised the red paper above his head with both hands, bowing deeply before the elder. "Yet this ticket is what Ah-Shun has owed you all along. Today, please accept this ticket, and ferry me across... for one last time!"

Uncle Rong looked at the faded red paper ticket, taking in those earnest, youthful brushstrokes. The tears gathered in his eyes finally spilled over, trickling down the deep ravines carved into his weathered cheeks.

The old ferryman reached out a trembling hand, accepting that slip of paper—light as a feather, yet heavier than a thousand tons of stone—and tucked it reverently into the oiled-cloth tobacco pouch next to his chest.

"Good! Splendid! Today... Uncle will accept your ticket!" Uncle Rong wiped the wetness from his face and straightened his stooped spine. The frailty of his years seemed to vanish in an instant, as if he had transformed once more into that river god who commanded the tempest.

"Cast off! We're putting out to water—!"

A resonant, prolonged river cadence that had pierced thirty years of wind and storm detonated across the barren gravel beaches of the Wu River once more!

Just as he had done as a boy, Ah-Shun sprang expertly onto the stern to untie the mooring line, seizing the polished wooden paddle.

Uncle Rong tucked the five-meter thorny bamboo Wave-Calming Pole under his arm, planting it down against the shoreline stones with a resounding thud.

"Swish—"

The bamboo raft glided gracefully and effortlessly into midstream.

There was no storm, no flash flood. The evening sun paved the river into a shimmering highway of liquid gold. The raft skimmed across the ripples with absolute serenity, parting the surface with gentle whispers.

"Ah-Shun, I heard you went to the city to study foreign books, learning to... build bridges?" Uncle Rong asked softly, looking out across the tranquil waters as he steered with ease.

"Yes, Uncle Rong," Ah-Shun stood at the stern, his gaze locked unblinkingly upon the river that had nurtured and tested his entire childhood. "In four years of university, I drafted thousands of blueprints and calculated tens of thousands of structural formulas. Every ounce of knowledge I mastered was for the sake of this river."

Ah-Shun lifted his hand, pointing toward the widest, most treacherous reach of the channel before them: "Right now, folks can detour through Fenyuan, but that adds more than a dozen kilometers. When typhoons trigger mudslides, Xiwei will still become an isolated island. Uncle Rong, mark my words! One day, I will drive deep bridge piers right here, hoist the widest steel girders, and construct a Xiwei Bridge that even a hundred-year flood can never wash away!"

Uncle Rong listened in silence, an expression of boundless peace and vindication blooming across his aged features.

"Splendid... a great bridge is splendid indeed," the old man murmured softly. "Uncle has grown old; these hands can no longer wrestle thousands of pounds of brown rapids. But Ah-Shun, the road across this water must never break, and the hearts of the Xiwei people must never scatter."

As they spoke, the raft bumped gently against the stone steps of the northern dock.

It was the final landing this raft would ever make.

Uncle Rong stepped forward, taking that five-meter-long thorny bamboo Wave-Calming Pole—polished to a deep, glossy chestnut by the passage of time—into both hands.

The old man gazed at it intently for a lingering moment. Then, turning around, he brought the heavy pole level across his palms, presenting it solemnly to the strong, capable hands of Li Shun'an.

"Ah-Shun, Uncle's boat has reached the shore today; it will never venture onto the water again," Uncle Rong's eyes were bottomless and sacred, his voice rumbling like the deep undercurrents of the riverbed. "Uncle entrusts this Wave-Calming Pole to you. For thirty years, I ferried our people across this great river on a bamboo raft; for the road that remains, it will be your turn to ferry them across with a great bridge!"

As the heavy thorny bamboo settled into his grip, Ah-Shun felt a resonant tremor through his palms. The pole still retained the fiery heat of Uncle Rong's touch, bearing the coarse scars from tens of thousands of battles against rapids and boulders.

Tears blurred Ah-Shun's vision. Clasping the long pole firmly with both hands, he fell to his knees in deep reverence before the elder: "Rest easy, Uncle! This pole stands planted within Ah-Shun's heart. This great bridge—I swear I will build it for our home!"

As the sun sank completely behind Mount Dadu, twilight yielded to night, and a canopy of brilliant stars adorned the sky.

The old ferryman turned and walked slowly yet steadily up the long earthen dike. Behind him, the young engineer stood tall against the evening breeze, gripping the ancient bamboo pole—like an eternal lighthouse erected beside the dark river, never to fade.
